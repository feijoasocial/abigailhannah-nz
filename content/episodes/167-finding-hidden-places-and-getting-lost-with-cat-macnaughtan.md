When Google Maps offers me a route that's 20 minutes longer, it's usually an instant no. And after this chat, I want to take it every single time.

Cat started a travel blog called Getting Lost 10 years ago, back when blogging was a really big thing. It grew out of a family tradition of getting lost on the way home, and it turned into a card game that sends you down roads you'd never pick yourself. Follow a blue car. Head the way the wind's blowing. That kind of thing.

She's full of brilliant stories (one involves a campervan, a power line and a cow wearing her hat), and she makes a good case that the hidden gems still exist, if we let ourselves find them.

So this post covers how the blog began, finding hidden places in New Zealand, how the Getting Lost game came about, a no-plan road trip to Paihia, back roads and the Forgotten World Highway, the Dunedin do-over, and Cat's tips for letting go of the plan (with guardrails).

## Getting Lost, the blog {#getting-lost-blog}

Cat started her travel blog in 2014. She'd just reconnected with her now husband, they'd brought their two families together, and they were doing all these cool little travel things. So they started writing about it on a blog called Getting Lost.

Those were the early days. They had a WordPress blog and a Facebook page, because Facebook was really all there was, and the community was insane. It grew quickly, with hundreds of comments and instant feedback, and for Cat, working out a whole new blended family, it was like a really, really big support network. She grew with that community. Ten years on, she'll meet people in real life who know things about her, and she remembers she's been oversharing online for a decade.

I feel that with Instagram. A lot of my friends now started out as people I followed and commented on, and now we get to hang out in real life.

## Finding hidden places in New Zealand {#hidden-places}

Cat describes her childhood holidays as pretty boring. They went to the same places, like [Rotorua](https://abigailhannah.nz/038-drone-photography-rotorua-petra-leary/), Thames and Matamata, and later Stillwater. They were always the out-of-the-way spots, always with a big crew of cousins, doing down-to-earth Kiwi things. She didn't get on a plane overseas until her 20s, and she spent that decade focused on a high-pressure career in advertising, so travel took a back seat.

Rotorua, boring! For a lot of people, all that geothermal activity is a once-in-a-lifetime thing. Driving around the South Island, I'm constantly mouth open at a paddock of sheep with mountains behind it, and that's normal here.

### A different kind of adventuring

Her now husband showed her a whole different side to adventuring. He hunted, fished, dived and hiked, and none of it cost money or came from a guidebook. She remembers him taking her to a shipwreck at Miranda, about an hour out of Auckland, and wondering why she couldn't Google it. They kept finding places like that, and when she tried to look them up afterwards, there was nothing. That was her catalyst: this is exploring.

Cat is sure those [hidden gems](https://abigailhannah.nz/north-island-hidden-gems/) still exist, but she thinks search has made them harder to find. It's so good at sending us where we already like to go, and to the most popular places, that our travel gets narrower and narrower. As she puts it, we've got worse at:

> "forcing ourselves to step out of our comfort zone and go down a road … talk to a farmer ask what's over there"

Talk to a local, stop at a pub and ask what's good. We've stopped doing that, and she finds that quite scary. Playing her own game on a recent trip, she ended up on a boat to an hour-long marine bushwalk she'd never have found.

I was low-key hoping she'd say that. On a Doubtful Sound cruise last winter, a Manapōuri local working on the boat told me about a swimming hole about three minutes off the road into Milford Sound. We went back a couple of weeks before recording on a bluebird day, followed his instructions, and it's the most beautiful swimming hole I've ever seen, right off one of the busiest roads in the country. I'd never have found it without that local.

## How the Getting Lost game began {#getting-lost-game}

The blog's name came from one of the first things that bonded them as a family. Cat was driving a manual ute to meet family at an ice cream parlour in Mission Bay, with three kids in the back who hated each other, on one of the kids' birthdays, and it was a nightmare. Then they got lost. The kids got right in behind it, heads out the window, sniffing the air for the ocean, all in it together. From then on, whenever they went out, they'd get lost on the way home.

The blog was just a blog for four years. Then, in 2018, a woman who'd moved from South Africa to the army base at Waiōuru messaged to ask what was good there. Cat suggested the Army Museum, which was the extent of what anyone could Google. Instinctively, though, she knew there had to be more: a field where wildflowers grow, wild horses, interesting things wherever you go. Directions weren't the answer, because that's curating a journey for someone else.

So they wrote a list of rules, based on that woman:

* **Offline.** The best places don't have great cell reception.
* **Portable**, so you can take it anywhere.
* **Replayable.** You should be able to take it out 10 times a year to different places.
* **Fun for adults as well as kids.**

They considered dice and an app, then landed on a card game that plays off things in the environment. The first, the Adventurers Edition, had cards like follow a blue car, head in the direction the wind's blowing, and head to the highest place you can see. It works in a city, a small town, anywhere in the world.

They made it with their community and launched it on Facebook with no advertising. Cat went to sleep, checked around lunchtime, and found they'd sold out, and sold a whole lot of stock they didn't have, because they hadn't set stock limits. It kept selling out for three years. When we recorded, it was in 500 stores around Australia and New Zealand, with 300,000 games sold and 42 different games.

## A no-plan road trip to Paihia {#paihia-road-trip}

Cat has taken her kids on two holidays with absolutely zero planning: get in the car with a pack of cards and go. The scariest was early on, when they were still doing paid blogging. She'd pitched a supermarket that had just launched click and collect: she'd take her kids, and some other people's kids, in her seven-seater with the grocery list ready, and only put in the pickup location once they knew where they were going.

About three hours in, she started to panic. She was being paid for this and had no idea where she was going. There's crazy, which she does quite often, and then there's this is a bit bonkers.

The route, as the cards dealt it:

* **Silverdale and Waiwera**, meandering around, with a stop for swims.
* **Going rogue** out west on the peninsula, past a sculpture on a mountainside of two stick people in deckchairs with two glasses of wine, in the middle of nowhere, and an army tanker in a field. She still couldn't tell you where it was.
* **Whangārei**, which was a relief, because there'd be no supermarket for click and collect up the west coast of [Northland](https://abigailhannah.nz/176-a-locals-guide-to-northland-and-building-hiking-communities-with-hannah-whittaker/), and nowhere to put all those children.
* **Paihia**, after swinging inland. Everything was booked, so they ended up somewhere incredible on a hill out of Paihia, with a massive pool looking over Waitangi and the rivers, close to Kerikeri. They stayed four days and explored water holes.

## Back roads and the Forgotten World Highway {#back-roads}

Cat loves the Forgotten World Highway, through the tiny place that declared itself a republic, where you can get your passport stamped. The road goes crazy narrow, and you start to wonder if you'll actually make it to [Taranaki](https://abigailhannah.nz/156-taranaki-three-peaks-traverse-and-supporting-families-in-the-outdoors-with-jenn-julian/).

It's on my list. With our kayaks on the roof of the [van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/), we're quite tall, and there's a tunnel, so I need to check the height restriction before I get a couple of hours in and have to turn around on a very narrow road.

Their newest game is the Really, Really Lost Edition, an add-on like a ghost chilli for your pack. One card: look up a place on GPS or maps, then turn it off and try to get there unaided.

I'm pretty good at directions, so I'm taking that one on personally. Isaac, on the other hand, uses GPS for a commute he's done for two years.

I once got chatting to a couple of locals in an antique store who were on their summer road trip. Every summer, they'd set out to drive every single road in the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/). I love unhinged goals like that: hit all the dirt roads instead of the tourist spots.

Cat agrees: there are so many weird and wonderful things down different roads. Somewhere in the South Island there's an entranceway with two old cars upended and stacked either side. It's epic, and she has no idea where it is.

## The Dunedin do-over {#dunedin-do-over}

Their biggest mishap became a blog post called the need for a Dunedin do-over. When they travel, each kid gets a day to choose what they do. On this trip they were in a campervan, and they'd just got married in Glenorchy, very spur of the moment, with a celebrant, a photographer and the three kids. So this was their honeymoon, with kids.

One of the kids had chosen [Dunedin](https://abigailhannah.nz/139-slow-travel-in-an-ev-and-catlins-dunedin-and-oamaru-with-mature-flaneur-tim-ward/), and she wanted to play Getting Lost. They all wanted to see Larnach Castle too, and a keen follower in Mosgiel had invited them over, so the kids felt like mini celebrities.

The kids had an absolute meltdown at Larnach Castle. Tears, everyone wanting to go home, chaos. Running late, they pulled their big campervan into the follower's circular driveway, and heard an almighty crash. A low-hanging power line. They'd taken out the power to his house and the neighbourhood. While Cat and the kids ate cold scones and looked at the cows, her husband tried to fix the power line and the campervan.

Then the kid whose day it was still wanted to play Getting Lost. So Cat told her husband to get on the main highway, and she pulled the follow a blue car card. A couple of hours later, a sleepy voice from the back asked if they were still following that car. Yes. They ended up in Cromwell, which by contrast was so beautiful and lovely.

It's the only time she's been to Dunedin, and it wasn't Dunedin's fault at all. She left a hat behind, and the follower occasionally posts a picture of one of his cows wearing it, so she thinks he's seen the funny side.

I was chuckling the whole time. It's reassuring for families too, because Instagram makes it all look picture perfect, and kids do have meltdowns and things do go wrong. I'm already constantly worried about our tall van, and now I'll be watching for power lines even more.

## Letting go of the plan, with guardrails {#letting-go}

With no plan, Cat finds somewhere to stay on Google, and she's never not found somewhere. They've brought out a Restaurant Edition too, and even without reservations, she's never had a problem. Having fewer options takes away the tyranny of choice: planning months ahead, she's weighing up reviews forever.

The places you get forced into are often the best ones. Out of Paihia, they'd never have picked that place on the hill. On a Northland holiday, when each kid also got to choose a night, one of them picked the Hippie Hut. It was really cheap, and it turned out to be an ex-commune. They'd never have booked it in a million years, and it was amazing.

I had the same feeling in Te Anau, where there were lines outside every restaurant with high Google reviews, like London. We got an amazing Thai dinner around the corner instead.

### Cat's guardrails

Cat likes to go wild and also likes to be in control. What a lot of people, particularly neurodiverse people, tell her is that the cards give them permission to let go:

> "it gives me permission to let go because it's not me being crazy. The cards are being crazy."

She still sets guardrails:

* **A budget** and **a time limit**.
* **No more than 10 minutes** following one direction.
* **No more than a three-hour drive** away.

People ask what happens if the blue car goes for five hours. Stop following it! You have free will. One group followed a blue car for ages until it turned its lights on: an undercover cop, wanting to know why they'd been tailing it for two hours.

Her worst case: you'll always find somewhere to stay. At worst you sleep in your car and drive back tomorrow. In the scheme of things, you're going to be fine.

### Trust the little detours

Once, on her way to work, Cat saw fog blanketing the city with only the top of the Harbour Bridge poking out. She pulled over to photograph it and ended up an hour late with no good excuse, and that photo won awards. Her point: you won't remember being late for work or dinner being late. Those are little things, and the thing you did instead is what stays with you.

I'm a gut person: if something feels right, I go with it. I've had a couple of Getting Lost packs for ages and never pulled them out, because you need time to let go of control. Maybe this is the year. I won't be following a blue car from Dunedin to Cromwell, though.
