Hazel is back for her third chat on the podcast, and I was really really excited about this one. If you've been listening for a while, you'll know her as the woman who wrote [Solo](https://abigailhannah.nz/032-ruapehu-solo-hazel-phillips/) and then Fire and Ice, and who calls herself a [Ruapehu](https://abigailhannah.nz/138-an-obsession-with-the-mountains-of-tongariro-national-park-with-hazel-phillips/) addict.

But this time it's something a little bit different. Her new book, Great Hearts: First Ladies of Aoraki Mount Cook, came out on 10 September. It's about the women who were making mountaineering history around Aoraki from the 1870s onwards, and whose stories then got written out.

And I didn't do history at school (I feel like I missed out). So I was hooked from the first story.

We cover how Hazel went from a scared bookworm to a tramper, her take on the Great Walks, and how the book came together. Then the women: the Harpers, the first all-woman climb of Mount Sefton, the skirts, the bluffs, the rescues and the micro erasures. And we finish on what still holds women back in the mountains, and why Hazel thinks Ruapehu is the best place to start.

## How Hazel got into tramping {#hazel-story}

Hazel was never an outdoorsy kid. She was quite scared of the outdoors: it seemed hard and challenging, and it didn't seem to be for women. You saw the big Ed Hillary types out there doing the exciting things, and she internalised that idea. What she loved was books. You couldn't tear her away from a library.

That changed when a friend, an even shorter human than Hazel, walked the [Milford Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/034-milford-track-nz-popular-walk/) carrying all her own gear. Hazel admired her hugely, and thought that if she could do it, maybe Hazel could too. So she went, completely untrained.

Physically, it was a bit of a train wreck: she carried too much and had no idea what she was doing. She's walked the Milford twice more since, and once your body's conditioned and you're carrying the right amount of gear, it feels so easy.

## Great Walks and the almost Great Walks {#great-walks}

The Milford is one of the best, in Hazel's book, along with the [Routeburn](https://abigailhannah.nz/009-solo-travel-routeburn-track-emily/). She also really rates the Rees-Dart for how incredibly scenic that part of the country is, and would love to go back.

She reckons there's almost an order to do the [Great Walks](https://abigailhannah.nz/10-great-walks-in-10-days/) in, from the least scenic to the most, so you finish with the likes of the Milford and the Routeburn. They're great experiences no matter what.

My first Great Walk was the [Tongariro Northern Circuit](https://abigailhannah.nz/172-hiking-the-tongariro-alpine-crossing-and-tongariro-northern-circuit-with-ben-boland/), which I love so much. Rakiura and the Heaphy are still on my list, as is the Rees-Dart.

Hazel thinks the Northern Circuit will get back on the map, with a big new hut at Oturere and the Mangatepopo track restored after fires. Then there are her de facto Great Walks: the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/), the Rees-Dart and Round the Mountain, which you can chunk into little overnight sections.

I need to go back and finish Round the Mountain myself. We did half of it, then a storm came in.

## Writing Great Hearts, from 80 climbers to 30 {#writing-great-hearts}

Great Hearts is Hazel's fifth book. Before Solo, her first outdoorsy one, came a history of New Zealand advertising and a biography of Sir Bob Harvey.

Solo touched on a few women climbers, and the research interest never went away. She grabbed any historic book that mentioned lady climbers, and DOC let her rifle through its archives at Mount Cook and in Westland.

She figured she'd need about 20 women climbers for a book. She ended up with a list of 80. You can't do 80 people justice in one book, so she whittled it down to 30. It runs from 1873, when a woman first walked on the glaciers of Mount Cook, to 1953 and the first all-woman unguided climb of Aoraki Mount Cook. After that there were so many women that it felt like a natural stopping point.

When Hazel wrote the 30 stories as pure history, they sounded like obituaries: she was born, she climbed these things, then she died. So each non-fiction story is followed by a fiction scene, reimagined in first person in the woman's own words. Every scene is based on something real, and notes at the back say how much is reimagined.

## The Harpers and Mount Sefton in 1934 {#harpers-and-sefton}

The woman who first walked on the glaciers was Joanna Harper. Her son, A.P. Harper, was one of the founders of the New Zealand Alpine Club, and his daughter, Rosamond Harper, is on the book's cover.

Seeing Mount Cook was Joanna's heart's desire. When some of the party couldn't get across a river, she and another man went on by themselves and got onto one of the glaciers, while the rest, including her husband, missed out. She wrote to a friend about it, and that letter is really special, because there's so little from these women in their own words. Men wrote for the Alpine Journal. Women operated the lantern slides at club meetings, and the newspaper noted how beautifully they did it.

### The first all-woman unguided climb of a major peak

In 1934, Rosamond Harper, Lella Davidson and Betsy Blunden made the first all-woman unguided climb of a major [3,000-metre peak](https://abigailhannah.nz/017-enchaining-nz-3000m-peaks-alastair-hamish/), Mount Sefton.

By then the Canterbury Mountaineering Club had gone male only, and stayed that way until 1977. Its members owned a lot of the huts around [Arthur's Pass](https://abigailhannah.nz/153-arthurs-pass-and-three-attempts-at-mount-philistine-with-amelia-hayes/) and were famous for turfing women out of them. So women teamed up, made their own gear and climbed together, unguided and manless.

Their porter never materialised, so the three packed everything up the Copland themselves and settled on Sefton. Peter Graham, the chief guide at the Hermitage, tried to talk them out of it. Then kea ripped their tents to bits, went for the purse holding their precious money and ruined their food. Kea don't care what your gender is.

They climbed Sefton from the west anyway. Betsy had guided at Mount Cook, Rosamond was built for alpine climbing, and Lella had made the first unguided all-woman ascent of [Earnslaw](https://abigailhannah.nz/035-mt-earnslaw-mountaineering/) the year before, with Greta Stevenson.

Women were expected to marry and have babies for the good of the nation then, and climbing was thought to destroy your body, so it was very subversive. Rosamond's father had been worried, but when they came back he was incredibly proud and published their success in the Alpine Journal.

## Skirts, puttees and red tartan trousers {#what-they-wore}

In 1910, Freda du Faur became the first woman to climb Aoraki Mount Cook. Women had always climbed in ankle-length skirts, which could get hooked on things, but climbing without one was deeply unfeminine. So Freda wore a half skirt to just beneath her knees, incredibly racy for the time, with puttees (long fabric strips wrapped around the lower leg) and a motoring veil around her hat. Her own book is well worth reading, Hazel says.

Some women did climb in trousers. Constance Barnicoat, a journalist, was part of the first all-woman party to cross Copland Pass in 1903. She came out on the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/) in trousers and everyone thought she was a boy. She shocked the unshockable West Coast. By the 1930s, trousers were no big deal.

The old photos are black and white, so we picture dull clothing. But Doreen Pickens, from the end of the book, was known for her shocking red tartan trousers.

## Doreen Pickens and Betsy Blunden {#doreen-and-betsy}

Doreen was on the first all-woman unguided climb of Aoraki Mount Cook. She was incredibly fit, very self-confident and loud, and that did not go down well with anyone.

Hazel listened to her on tape, in oral histories recorded with women mountaineers in the 90s. At first she disliked Doreen, with her private schoolgirl vowels and supreme self-confidence, and almost left her out.

Then she saw how much Doreen had coped with. Doreen married a man who said she could keep climbing, because back then women stopped as soon as they married. After the wedding, it was all off. Her life was very difficult, and she was a very lonely person. In the letters Mavis Davidson kept, now in the Hocken Collections at the University of Otago, Hazel saw how differently the other women talked to Doreen and about her. She softened, and came to see her as a bit of an icon. Doreen's fiction scene, with the club men going home to their mums to brag while the women did the big climbs, is one of her favourites.

### Betsy bluffs her way in

Everyone loved Betsy Blunden. In the 1920s she bluffed her way into a guiding job at the Hermitage, because it sounded like great fun. She'd never climbed a mountain. Asked if she'd been up mountains, she said yes, and didn't mention it was on horseback. A friend gave her an ice axe for the train ride from Christchurch, and she tucked it under the seat in case anyone asked technical questions.

She started as a picnic guide, leading people out on what's now the [Hooker Valley Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) to the Stocking Glacier and boiling the billies. The male guides got the bigger climbs, so Betsy waited until they were all booked up, sold bigger climbs to guests herself and became the only guide available. That's how she got into high guiding, and she ended up doing all the high peaks.

> "I think that's such a life lesson to us, like go out and charge in and kind of claim the terrain."

The job never ended: after guiding came the gramophone at dinner and piles of mending. After a couple of years, she and Rosamond tried to set up their own guiding business in Arthur's Pass, until the park board sent a nasty little letter saying they had no permit.

## How women learned to climb without the clubs {#learning-to-climb}

Mavis Davidson, who led the 1953 climb of Aoraki Mount Cook, was a Tararua Tramping Club member. She applied for a trip one Christmas and was turned down because she was a woman and there might be some climbing. Incensed, she organised and led her own parties from then on, often with men in them.

In the early days, everything at the Hermitage was guided: the guides cut steps for you and put you on the rope. Even then, two society sisters from Wellington, Maude and Elfie Williams, climbed their own peaks with no guide. They were the baby peaks, like [Mount Wakefield](https://abigailhannah.nz/046-mount-wakefield-jinal-govind/) and Mount Ollivier, up where [Mueller Hut](https://abigailhannah.nz/028-mueller-hut-backyard-travel-family/) is now.

## Micro erasures and alpine rescues {#erasures-and-rescues}

Hazel calls them micro erasures: small moments where a woman gets written out of the story, and they're hard to challenge.

### Lucy Mannering

In 1894, three men made the first ascent of Aoraki Mount Cook and left their camp on the glacier in a hurry. It became fully encased in ice. Years later, Lucy Mannering spotted it and chipped it out. It was her discovery, but her husband, Guy Mannering, later framed it in the passive voice, and in one article claimed it for himself. Hazel gives him some grace: Lucy, the love of his life, died in the Aratiatia Rapids, and he was distraught.

### Ruth Adams

In 1948, Ruth Adams climbed the previously unclimbed south-east ridge of Mount Cook with Ed Hillary and a couple of guides. Then, on La Perouse, she had an accident. Her rescue, one of the last big ones before helicopters, took a week, and she was stretchered out to the West Coast down the Cook River.

The men's accounts said she slipped and the rope snapped. Hazel says Ruth was told to jump and the rope would hold her, and the old manila rope snapped. Ruth's daughter has since said she was glad the correct version was in print. Ruth never climbed again.

Her father, Ernest Adams of fruitcake fame, went straight to the Hermitage, got in a plane and dropped fruitcakes out the window to the rescuers.

### Ida Corry and Katie Gardiner

In 1933, Ida Corry was making the first ascent of Mount Goldsmith from the west with just her guide when he broke his leg on the descent. Ida splinted it, then cut steps and belayed him down them, all the way to [Almer Hut](https://abigailhannah.nz/chancellor-hut-almer-hut/). For a woman then to manage the rescue of her own guide was deeply unusual.

Katie Gardiner was on Mount Tasman with two guides when a storm hit, and they camped in a crevasse. They all thought they would die, and Katie, the client, rewrote her will to provide for her guides' wives. When the storm broke, they walked down to safety, fuelled by a boiled egg each that the chief guide had saved.

## Stories still turning up {#stories-turning-up}

The research keeps going after a book is out. Hazel is keen to hear from anyone who knows about women mountaineers.

Great Hearts is small and flexibound, so it slips into your pack, and Hazel will donate copies to huts and lodges.

## Women in the mountains today, starting on Ruapehu {#women-today}

We can thank these women that we don't climb in long skirts and motoring veils. But confidence is still a big challenge, and readers of Solo tell Hazel their experience is reflected in it. Hazel is short and has to walk at a fast cadence to keep up with long legs, which she thinks is partly why she goes solo so often: alone, there's no pace to worry about.

Women are well represented on beginner snowcraft courses. On intermediate courses, when the rope comes out, there are far fewer, and it's hard to find people to go with or know which routes are appropriate. Hazel planned a women's mountaineering development weekend this year, though two attempts were called off by bad weather on Ruapehu. For it, she's put together an alpine prescription: 15 Ruapehu routes for someone who's just done basic snowcraft.

Years ago she started a Facebook group, Secret Women's Mountain Business, so she could ask stupid questions without getting ripped to bits. It's still going, and any woman keen on mountaineering can request to join.

My safest space in the outdoors was a women-only kayaking skills weekend before [our big expedition from Cape Reinga](https://abigailhannah.nz/182-finishing-a-two-month-slow-paddle-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/), where there were no silly questions. A lot of my tramping is with girlfriends or my husband, who always walks behind me so I set the pace. In some groups I'm at the back, in others the front, so pace is relative. We tramp and [rock climb](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/), and mountaineering feels like a natural next step.

Hazel agrees, and says Ruapehu is the place to start:

> "Ruapehu is the ideal place actually to get into mountaineering … You can do pretty much everything on ice axe and crampons without going to ropes."

It's a fantastic place to learn to walk on ice and build a big base of experience in challenging conditions, then progress to South Island climbs.

Hazel hopes Great Hearts does for other women what her friend's Milford trip did for her.

> "I want women to see that a path has already been carved out for them by these very tough, tenacious women and that we have this really long unknown history of women."
