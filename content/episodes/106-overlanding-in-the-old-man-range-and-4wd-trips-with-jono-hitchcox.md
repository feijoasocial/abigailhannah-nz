When my husband and I first got our [four-wheel drive van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/), there were all these words I didn't understand. Locking hubs? What even is that? So I was really really looking forward to this chat with Jono Hitchcox.

Jono is from Timaru, and he runs the YouTube channel Lost Sheep Adventures, which has taken him on some epic trips across New Zealand. He grew up tramping, got his licence and never really looked back. He's also taught himself to fix pretty much everything on his truck (I was strongly advised not to try that with our transmission).

The heart of this one is Operation Overland 2022, a 14-hour day crossing the Old Man Range in knee-deep snow, with stuck trucks, a daisy chain of snatch straps and a tough call near the top. And it's soooo good.

Then we get practical: how to find four-wheel drive tracks, the apps Jono uses, driving in snow, his tips for beginners (spoiler: learn to read a river) and what he says to people who think four-wheel driving wrecks the land.

## Growing up tramping and learning to fix his truck {#jono-story}

Jono describes Timaru as a quiet, sleepy town with easy access to the mountains. His family got him tramping very young, and growing up was all tramping and camping, including a cursed tent that rained every time they put it up. A big map on the wall marked off every road trip, until pretty much every road in the South Island was ticked.

The odd bit of four-wheel driving in the family truck lit a spark: he could drive to the places he'd been walking to. Once he got his licence, the tramping club took a back seat, and that hasn't really stopped.

Jono has no professional mechanical training. He's taught himself from YouTube, with a few mistakes along the way. Mechanics were expensive, and when you break something almost every trip, that adds up, so he thought, how hard can it be? He started with small services and replacing a hub, and worked up to pulling the whole engine apart and adding a turbo for his reliability build. He hasn't tackled a transmission yet, but these days:

> "I can quite happily rebuild my truck on the side of a track if I have to. Let's hope I don't have to."

I said the same thing when our van needed its transmission rebuilt: surely it can't be that hard, Isaac? We were strongly advised against it. Jono's advice to start simple, with oil changes, might be where I begin.

## Starting Lost Sheep Adventures {#lost-sheep-adventures}

When Jono was young, he got hold of an Australian four-wheel drive magazine with a DVD of four-wheel drive trips in Australia, and it looked like the best job ever. Later he realised YouTube was right there, so why wait for a TV show to pick him up? He bought a Jeep, put a bit of extra cash into a camera and a computer to edit on, and gave it a go, thinking it would be cool if something happened. Then something happened.

Growing up, his family didn't know where to go four-wheel driving. There was no community or social media for it, so they didn't do a lot because they didn't know a lot. That's part of why one of his early videos took off: a tiny stock route tucked in the back of Akaroa that he'd heard about from locals while he was living there. People had never heard of it, and he thinks a lot of the views were Christchurch people looking for somewhere local, with Akaroa only an hour away.

Since then he's aimed for a video a month, which can be hard work with breakages and real life in the way. Locals always know the best spots, he says, and he knows heaps of places around Queenstown now.

## Finding four-wheel drive tracks in New Zealand {#finding-tracks}

Back in the day it was books. Jono still uses his copy of Overlanding New Zealand, picked up when he was very young, with the corners rolled off from living in the truck. His channel shows what to expect on each trail, and he says three apps between them cover most tracks in New Zealand:

* **Overland Navigator** covers a bit of both islands.
* **Wikiloc** isn't used much here, but it's international, so it works for trips almost anywhere in the world.
* **Adventure Guide** is mainly the South Island, with a thousand-plus trails.

That's enough trails for the rest of your life. There are still plenty kept quiet, because they're epic, hardcore or hard to get access to.

## Operation Overland on the Old Man Range in snow {#old-man-range}

Operation Overland is an annual trip Jono hosts through his channel, inviting sponsors and supporters to spend five days camping and four-wheel driving a region together. 2022 was the first, with a small convoy, and when we recorded he was about to head off with 11 vehicles.

Jono works in tourism, so summer and winter are busy and his big trips happen in the shoulder seasons. This one was in October. They crossed into Central Otago, spent day two in the Serpentine and met up late at Piano Flat.

### Day three: a snow-filled creek

The plan was to climb the Waikaia Bush Road onto the plateau, cross the Old Man Range almost to the Obelisk, then drop down to Fruitlands and Alexandra. There hadn't been any snow, so they expected an easy run and started at 9.30.

Higher up, snow sat on the track where it hadn't melted. The tops are a very delicate ecosystem, so they stuck to the track, smashing through knee-deep snow in places. At a small snow-filled creek around 11am, Jono shot across without breaking the surface, the second truck broke through, and the third, a Prado, hit it hard, stopped dead and bellied out.

Getting it out took hours. They turned the trucks in front around on the narrow track, edged a second vehicle around as an anchor, and Jono dug out what felt like a ton of snow from underneath. A double line pull on the winch, with one heavy truck anchored to another, finally dragged it across. People were starting to worry about how much time they'd lost.

### Daisy-chained to Potter's Hut

Next came a 500-metre run of knee-deep snow, so they daisy-chained everyone together with snatch straps, a technique one of the group had learned at his four-wheel drive club. If one truck goes down, the ones in front drag it out. When two or three got stuck, Jono hooked on from the far end and they gunned it through. A very epic recovery, he says.

That only got them to Potter's Hut, at the top of the Waikaia Bush Road. Along the ridgeline at 1,600 metres, the snow thinned, apart from massive drifts filling the hollows with fence posts poking out the top. With the weather closing in to near whiteout, one truck at a time crossed each drift and gave the all good on the radio.

### Turning back at Hyde Rock

At the Hyde Rock turn-off, another vehicle went down, and they spent a couple more hours leapfrogging it to a dry spot on Maxtrax. Then the team huddled. With kilometres still to go to the gate, it was push on and potentially become a statistic, or drive everything they'd done back to Piano Flat. At 6pm, with cloud coming in hard and darkness close, they turned around.

They rolled into Piano Flat at 11.30pm after 14 hours on the track, the longest day any of them had spent in low range, and were very happy to start a fire and sit down with a beer. Some of the group had been getting distressed about the conditions and having to stay the night, so Jono is sure it was the right call.

I'm so glad they turned around. Whether you're tramping or in the alpine, you've got to think about whether everyone in the group is comfortable with the decisions being made.

For Jono, the highlight was the isolation. New Zealand is small, and usually he's a day's walk from a road. Up there:

> "being up there in those conditions, the temperatures dropping, night falling, snow drifts all over the place, you really felt isolated"

You can't park up and walk out, he says, because many people died on that hill in the gold mining era trying to do exactly that. He wants to go back and do it again. He's crossed it plenty of times in summer, when it's still a grade four track, and now he's planning a longer shoulder-season trip from Piano Flat to Duffers Saddle on the Old Woman Range, across both ranges and down to Cromwell, with a bigger convoy kitted out for the cold.

## Four-wheel driving in snow and camping from the truck {#snow-and-camping}

Jono drives in snow a lot in winter, up to Macetown and Skippers, so he doesn't see it as a bigger risk than other terrain. The thing to know is that each kind of snow handles differently:

* **Fresh powder** is the easiest. It's grippy, and you can smash through it.
* **Slushy snow turning to ice** is the hardest, early and late in the day and after a vehicle has driven over it. Chains are recommended.

If you get stuck, you can dig back down to solid ground and drive out, but it takes a lot of time, and the temperature can drop fast. He also thinks about a club convoy that went out unprepared, got stuck in a snowstorm and made the news. His group goes out in trucks built for that territory, with the gear to stay the night if they have to. And GPS units and [PLBs](https://abigailhannah.nz/plb-safety/) are easy to hire or borrow now.

When we had snowfall at [Mount Cook](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) in August, I learned there are different types of snow: that snow rolled into a snowman like in the movies, which I've never seen on [Ruapehu](https://abigailhannah.nz/032-ruapehu-solo-hazel-phillips/), where it's a lot more icy and compact.

Huts were the thing when Jono was tramping, but his truck is now his personal hut. He's done ground tents and the swag life, and he's currently on a rooftop tent, with a fridge, a 12-volt system and a little oven on board. He can't remember the last time he slept in a hut he'd driven to.

## Getting started in four-wheel driving {#getting-started}

Jono says it's a much easier hobby to get into than people think. There's some stigma about vehicles, tyres and lifts, but when we recorded you could spend about five grand on an Isuzu Bighorn or an Isuzu Mu and start on local trails. He started on the Opihi and Pareora rivers outside Timaru. Just don't drive into a big hole.

His tips:

* **Learn to read a river.** A lot of four-wheel drive tracks are riverbeds, particularly in the South Island. Learn what an eddy, an eddy line, a weir and a standing wave are.
* **Fit a snorkel first.** Raising the air intake is the cheapest insurance for your engine.
* **Download the apps** and get out there.
* **Join a local four-wheel drive club.** They generally run monthly trips and will tell you what vehicle and recovery gear you need.

The New Zealand Four Wheel Drive Association website lists the clubs around the country. Go to a social meeting, then do a trip: day trips, long weekends, multi-day trips to the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/). Joining also gives the Association a bigger voice to keep tracks open, and you'll probably get addicted really quickly.

## Four-wheel driving and the landscape {#landscape-impact}

I asked Jono what he says to people who think four-wheel driving destroys the landscape. He says it doesn't, and that the tracks you can access fall into three categories:

* **The Queen's Chain**, which means riverbeds. They generally reset with the next flood, so the impact is minimal, apart from when endangered birds are nesting, which DOC usually signposts.
* **Paper roads**, public roads anyone can use, which become four-wheel drive tracks because nobody maintains them.
* **Department of Conservation tracks**, the easiest to damage, often leading into national parks.

Damage comes from going off track. On the Old Man Range, people go wide around a mud hole, the next person goes wider, and soon a 100-square-metre patch is damaged. Jono puts it down to negligence, a lack of education and chasing TikTok likes. As the hobby grows, he sees more people coming in with the right mindset:

> "we're here to enjoy the environment, we're here to enjoy the birds, the rivers, the views, the mountains … we're also here to make sure that we can leave without really leaving any sign that we've been there."

By his reckoning, that's 90% of the four-wheel drive community in New Zealand. It's the other 10% who head out there because there's no cops around.

## Fitting it around work and what's next {#whats-next}

It's all weekends for Jono, and moving to a nine-day fortnight changed the game: every second weekend is a three-day weekend. Not everyone can come out on the extra day, though. Usually he films one weekend and edits for the next two or three.

With a thousand-plus South Island trails, he tries a different track every month, travelling further as he ticks off the ones nearby. He's loving expedition-style trips, and Operation Overland is the highlight of his year: five days of wheeling, wild camping and good mates.

He'd love to get back to the North Island too. On a 2021 trip he did Thompson's Track and the sand dunes at Poutō in [Northland](https://abigailhannah.nz/176-a-locals-guide-to-northland-and-building-hiking-communities-with-hannah-whittaker/), which absolutely blew his mind: a different style of wheeling, a different environment, and hot as hell.

I've done the Poutō track too, as passenger princess in [Bri](https://abigailhannah.nz/006-4wd-land-cruiser-bri-woolnough/)'s truck on the way to the lighthouse. On one massive dune she braked at a drop she hadn't seen and everything in the back went smash. Nothing broke, and it was such a great day out.
