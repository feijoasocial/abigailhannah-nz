Bronson and Chelsea are the family of five behind Adventures with Rosy, and this was their first ever podcast (no pressure). They took fifteen months to live in a caravan in the South Island with their son and twin girls, while working from the road.

I love asking guests where their love of travel started, and these two both grew up on the road in one way or another. So we get into that, and then the real stuff: the first week, when they nearly turned around and went home, what other people thought, and how they worked mornings and explored every afternoon.

Plus their trip within a trip to Stewart Island with three little kids (the ferry crossing, oof), why they came home, how they fit adventures around normal life now, and their advice for families who want to give it a go. Spoiler: pack less. Soooo much less.

## From a bus to a family caravan {#rosy-story}

Their first Rosy was a Mitsubishi Rosa [bus](https://abigailhannah.nz/058-bus-nz-wayna/), Rosy the Rosa, bought when Chelsea was pregnant with their son, back when it was the two of them doing weekend trips. When he came along, they took the dining table out to fit the car seat, and he slept in a basket on the bench top.

Having everything shoved in the bus got annoying, so they bought an ex-rental motorhome. Then they planned one more baby, hoping for a girl, and twins came along. So they went shopping again and bought a caravan to fit everyone in: Rosy 3, the one they went full time in.

They'd never really been to the South Island, and with kids they knew they'd travel slower. They both worked from home already, so why not see if they could work on the road and explore the South properly? The idea formed, they picked a date, and they spent fifteen months there.

## Growing up on the road {#growing-up-on-the-road}

Bronson lived in a big old house truck as a kid. His family was living in Auckland, and his parents didn't like the vibe there for kids, biking down the footpath of a busy road. So they bought a house truck, left Auckland and travelled around the North Island, staying in spots a bit longer, for a number of years.

His core memory is his bedroom, behind the cab, about the size of a dining table, with a foam mattress, a little shelf and a little window. He really wanted a desk for his birthday, so the end of his mattress was cut off and a little desk built in. A slightly shorter bed, but he could sit at the end of it, and he was really stoked. Mostly they played outside and built forts.

That's partly where their idea of fewer screens for their own kids comes from. They'd travelled together a fair bit, to Europe a few times, and a camper meant they could keep travelling with kids, with a little home base that came with them.

## The first weeks of full-time caravan life {#first-weeks}

They nearly turned around and went home after the first week. Bronson was working mornings, starting at about 7.30am, and they hadn't figured out how to make that work with meetings while three kids were around their feet and breakfast was on the go. Five of them in a small space meant a very tense first week or two, with lots of arguments and getting on each other's nerves.

Once they'd sorted the logistics, given each other space and learned to pick up on the cues when someone was getting frustrated, it made a big difference. They were surprised by the mental load, too. Somewhere new is exciting, but you don't know where the supermarket or the petrol station is, and with a caravan you're also finding water, dumping your grey water and emptying your toilet.

They started with a spreadsheet: six months mapped out, down to the cafés they'd heard good things about. It was out the window pretty quickly. The plan was one clockwise loop of the South Island. Instead they zigzagged all over the place, chasing the weather and events.

You absolutely have to chase the weather in New Zealand. It's one of my favourite ways to travel, especially with wheels: if it's tossing it down where you are and there's sunshine on the other side of the mountains, just drive.

The girls loved it. The caravan was fairly childproof, and an east-west bed gave them a play space with only one edge to fall off. A couple of months in, they'd forgotten they had ever lived in a house, so coming home was a massive adjustment. Even in the wet, they'd put their rain gear on and go puddle jumping for the day. The family explored somewhere every afternoon, so the kids were well exercised and slept really well. They could turn the caravan upside down with toys in seconds, and it took only a few minutes to tidy.

The flip side is that the kids are so young they don't remember any of it.

## Family, friends and other people's opinions {#other-opinions}

Bronson and Chelsea are still figuring this one out. Some friends and family really struggled with the idea for quite a while. Bronson's parents understood, having done the same thing, but it was still bittersweet to watch the grandkids drive away. The videos helped, though a lot of people saw it as one big vacation, and they had to keep explaining they still had work and laundry. On the flip side, lots of people told them they'd love to do something similar.

They also realised quickly that they hadn't moved to another country. When they needed to get home once, they booked a ferry and drove for a day. They got stuck at a really nice campground on a weekly rate in one of the lockdowns, and while family rang telling them to come home, there were no ferries, so they stayed put.

Family and friends came down to travel with them for a week or two at a time in rental vehicles, having their own mini adventures, which was really fun.

Their favourites were the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/), where they've been back a few times, and Stewart Island.

## Stewart Island with three kids {#stewart-island}

[Stewart Island](https://abigailhannah.nz/194-summer-as-a-doc-ranger-on-stewart-island-and-women-in-trades-with-brooke-thompson/) was one of the highlights, and a trip within their trip, because it took planning. With the girls on their backs and their son only able to walk so far, they couldn't do much hiking, so they needed a car over there, which meant three car seats.

### Getting to Stewart Island

I assumed there was a car ferry, and so did they. There isn't really: you fly, or take the passenger ferry. They took the ferry on the roughest day they could have had. Every wave lifted everyone out of their seats, the kids threw up on them, other passengers were sick, and it was chaos.

### Where they stayed

There isn't a lot of accommodation, with a backpackers and a lot of Airbnb-style places. They found a little motel that comes with a car for each unit. The owners picked them up at the wharf and handed over the keys, so they could drive all over the island. They took one car seat over on the boat, and that made it a lot easier. The kids were super excited to have real furniture: a couch, a TV and running water nobody had to worry about.

### Ulva Island and the birds

They spent four days on the island, hiking a bit and visiting the beaches, which were beautiful and pristine. There's one hotel and a few little shops, and not much else to do.

The highlight was taking the ferry to Ulva Island. Nobody lives there, and it's somewhere you can see kiwi running around on the paths in the day, along with kākā and saddlebacks. Even with the kids making all the noise in the world:

> "the bird life on the island is incredible"

A kākā came to their motel window every morning, tapping for some apple. At night, the motel units had torches with red lights, so you could head into the bush right outside and watch kiwi running around.

They reckon Stewart Island would be a lot easier without kids. There are night tours for stargazing and bird spotting, and a big loop track right around the island that looks pretty amazing.

I haven't been yet, and it's definitely on the bucket list.

## Working from the road {#working-on-the-road}

Bronson works for an IT company, and he set up his office in the back seats of the car, with a table on his lap for the keyboard and his tablet propped up on the seats. He worked mornings, while the kids got up and had a run-around, and finished at lunchtime, leaving the afternoons for exploring and travelling.

His work was really supportive. They'd asked if he could cut his hours back for a year, and his team shuffled meetings to the mornings. Renting out their house at home and earning on the road meant they weren't burning through savings, so they could keep going.

## Coming home and adventuring around normal life {#normal-life}

They didn't really have an end date. They'd thought about keeping their son out of school for a year. But towards the end, he was missing his friends and cousins. On his birthday on the West Coast, in pouring rain, they'd filled the caravan with balloons, and as he unwrapped his presents he asked when his friends were going to show up.

He'd been video calling his friends and sending them postcards, and he was keen to start school with them (one ended up in his class). It felt like they'd be doing him a disservice to keep him away any longer, even though he had the best time travelling.

Now they're based in Taupō with Rosy 4, a more off-road friendly caravan that carries all their bikes, and they're getting into biking with the kids. With school, it's weekends and school holidays, and it's North Island time: the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/) in summer, back up to Port Jackson (their first trip together, before kids), and maybe the [East Cape](https://abigailhannah.nz/056-vanlife-the-east-cape-and-life-done-differently-with-lisa-jansen/) again through Gisborne. They're also hoping to do an RV trip in the States with the kids.

People picture it as either stepping away from normal life for one big thing or doing nothing. As they put it:

> "you can actually fit it in around normal routine quite easily and still still get to explore"

## Their advice for families hitting the road {#family-advice}

**Don't overpack.** Less is more. There's nothing worse than too much stuff and nowhere to put it. A fold-up clothes line never came out of its locker, and they came home to a double garage jam packed with boxes they hadn't needed in fifteen months. The trickiest bit was the season swap, sending summer things home and getting a winter box sent down. They took so many books and toys, and then op shopping for new books became a favourite rainy-day activity. Anything you miss, you can buy on the road.

**Do a few longer trips first.** The trips that taught them most were a week, ten days or two weeks: long enough that you still have to do the laundry on the road. Figure out what you really have to have, and try not to add much to it.

**Start with what you've got.** You don't need a flash caravan and a truck. Borrow a neighbour's tent, or put a mattress in the back of a [station wagon](https://abigailhannah.nz/044-nz-station-wagon-road-trip/). There are so many DOC campgrounds, and most people have a walk or a national park within a few hours' drive. There's a season for everything, too: what works now might not work a year from now.

**Pick a date and tell everyone.** Until they'd set a date, they'd have kept finding reasons not to leave yet. They told everyone so they couldn't keep pushing it back:

> "We had to tell everyone this is our date so that we couldn't just keep pushing it back because there would be no perfect time."

**Don't leave it too late.** On the road, they met a lot of retired people who wished they'd done it ten years earlier, when they could still get on the bike every day.

I'm with them on packing light. We're on [van number three](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/), redoing the inside, and I'm being really pedantic about having a place for everything we need and no random cupboards filling up with random stuff.

Our own trial run was twenty days in the South Island in the middle of winter, with no insulation, scraping ice off the inside of the windows most mornings. I'm a really cold person, so if I can do it, anyone can (hot water bottles are the answer). I figured if I hated vanlife by the end, we'd never do it again. Two vans later, here we are.
