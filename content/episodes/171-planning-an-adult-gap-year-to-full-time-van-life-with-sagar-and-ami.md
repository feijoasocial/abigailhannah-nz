Adult gap year. I'd never heard the term before this chat, and now I think we need to make it a thing.

Sagar and Ami both grew up in India and moved to New Zealand 10 years ago. They travelled on weekends and long weekends around their full-time jobs for years. Then in March 2024 they left on a one-way ticket for what was meant to be one year of seeing how big the world is. They came home in August 2025 and moved into Wanda Bee, their tiny van, to roam Aotearoa until the bank account says stop.

And there are so so many similarities between their story and mine. We both arrived in 2016, and we both had our minds blown by how much is on our doorstep here.

So this post covers how they planned the gap year step by step (including the safety nets), what nobody tells you about the first few months of full-time travel, why they cancelled a working holiday in Finland, their favourite corners of Nelson Tasman and [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/), and some real talk on rainy days and showers in a van.

## How Sagar and Ami got into travel {#travel-beginnings}

Sagar and Ami both grew up travelling, in two very different styles.

Sagar's family were all about getting out. Every weekend they'd take the car wherever they wanted, to sit by the beach or a riverbank and have lunch or a barbecue, and every year they'd head away for 15 or 20 days. Ami's favourite part is their random road trips, where they'd pack up a tiny little Alto and go. Sagar always knew that whenever he could, he wanted to travel.

I learned to drive in an Alto in Bangalore, and when we moved to New Zealand we had one for about a year and a half in Auckland.

Their verdict on the Alto: a very tiny car, very reliable, and you can hit 500,000 kilometres and it'll still run smoothly.

Ami's family travelled on set-plan trips organised by a company. She never enjoyed those, and she didn't understand pack-your-bags-and-go travel until she met Sagar here in New Zealand. When they started travelling together, her mind was blown, and that's when she fell in love with it.

Growing up in India, they say, a gap year between high school and university wasn't something you could even think about. In New Zealand the goal was jobs, settling in and weekend trips. But they'd been following Kara and Nate, big travel creators on YouTube, and Ami had an idea brewing.

## First camping trip at Lake Waikaremoana {#first-camping-trip}

Their first Christmas in New Zealand, in December 2016, was also their first camping trip ever. They bought a tent and all the camping gear, practised putting the tent up with the help of YouTube videos, and headed for the holiday park at [Waikaremoana](https://abigailhannah.nz/175-starting-tramping-in-her-40s-and-lake-waikaremoana-as-a-family-with-lois-baldwin/), which they describe as the middle of nowhere. They stayed five or six days, did some kayaking, and it blew their minds.

I love that attitude so much. Everything is figureoutable.

For me, after arriving in 2016, it was the smaller escapes first. Driving 45 minutes to Piha and finding an incredible beach, then the rest of Auckland's coasts, then [Rotorua](https://abigailhannah.nz/038-drone-photography-rotorua-petra-leary/), Tauranga, the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/) and Whangārei, and my first South Island trip in 2020.

## Planning an adult gap year {#planning-gap-year}

Ami loves that people ask about the planning. In her words:

> "I'm getting sick of people thinking you can just leave and just start traveling full time because there is just so much that goes behind that."

It takes planning on logistics, health and finances, and for them the transition was gradual.

### Step one: understanding the lifestyle

Ami first brought the idea to Sagar in 2017. Growing up in India, travel meant holidays, and it couldn't be your job. Sagar thought she was out of her mind: nobody they knew talked about travelling full time, and they'd come to New Zealand for a reason they weren't ready to risk.

So Ami started sneakily sending him videos of cool travelling couples. The more they watched, the more they understood. Sagar is all about numbers, so he started looking at the spending, the earning and the lifestyle, and for the first time he thought about it positively. He loved his job, Ami says, but he loved freedom and travel more.

### Step two: research and a number

Next came books, blogs and YouTube videos on what you need to travel full time. Then they found Nomadic Matt, a big name among travel bloggers, and his book on travelling for $50 USD a day. That gave them a starting figure. They increased it a bit, set a goal, and decided that once they hit it, they were leaving.

### Step three: doing it without risking everything

They describe their mindset, growing up in India, as always having a backup plan if you're doing something risky. So they built in safety nets:

* **Citizenship first.** New Zealand is home, and they're never going to leave, so they became New Zealand citizens before they went. It pushed their plans out, and it was necessary.
* **Two pots of savings.** One amount for the gap year, and a separate amount in investments that was untouchable, so they'd never be starting from zero.
* **The house.** In 2019 they bought a house, partly so that if they came back, they'd have somewhere to go.

Our own journey to full time in our [van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/) looked similar: we bought the van in 2023 and only kitted it out the way we wanted last year. These things take a lot of saving, planning and time.

## A one-way ticket to Hanoi {#one-way-ticket}

Friends and family knew they were planning something crazy, and nobody took them seriously at first. They'd learnt how to share big news gradually, so they framed it as one year to themselves to travel, and they'd see. Their advice for anyone in the same spot: after a certain age, part of it is helping the people around you understand, because they come from a different generation.

Sagar's citizenship came through in February 2024 (he paid extra for a fast passport), and they left in March. The date had been set six months before. Once they booked a one-way ticket to Hanoi, it started hitting everyone, and when family came over to a house emptied of everything they'd sold, it was finally real.

Telling people about the crazy thing builds your own confidence too, because now you have to follow through. For Isaac and me, that was our [kayak expedition](https://abigailhannah.nz/182-finishing-a-two-month-slow-paddle-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/). Everyone said "oh, that's cool" until we had a date, February 2026. People don't believe you until they see you do it, and there's so much power in saying it out loud to the people who'll hold you accountable.

Vietnam was planned: seven days in Hanoi, then Sapa, then Ho Chi Minh. After that, the cheapest flights decided. Their hack is Skyscanner's search for anywhere, any time, which turns up some crazy deals (they used [trip.com](http://trip.com) as well).

## Full-time travel and content creation {#travel-and-content}

Choosing countries by the cheapest flight was fun. It was also, they say, the wrong approach for growing an account.

The bigger shock came after they left. They'd been talking and breathing full-time travel for years. Then it happened, and they were clueless. Now what? Nobody prepares you for that, and the first one to three months, in Asia, were really hard. Europe was a blessing in a way: things were similar from country to country, and they changed countries by train or bus instead of constantly sorting flights.

About a year in, they could see the mistake. They'd started full-time travel and content creation at the same time. The logistics, the finances and a new culture are all running in the back of your mind, and on top of that they were vlogging, editing and posting on YouTube. It was their dream, and for a while it didn't feel like it. If they could, they'd write a book or start a podcast about what happens when you start full-time travel, because a lot happens and nobody prepared them for it.

### Being honest about the goal

They admit they needed more honesty, with each other and with the plan. They'd called it an adult gap year, but underneath they were chasing freedom. They'd watched the creator industry grow and were trying the vlog style Kara and Nate had used five years earlier, when algorithms and audiences had moved on.

About six months before we recorded, they started breaking it down and treating it as a business, and saying so out loud. That changed how they think. They invested in a course they'd never have paid for two years earlier, and Sagar came around to Instagram as their main platform.

I had my own reality check. When I bought my first camera in 2017, I was sure I'd have 10k followers by the end of our India trip and drop out of uni to be a travel influencer. Nine years later I've built a creative business, and it took a lot longer than I thought.

## Swapping Finland for van life in New Zealand {#finland-to-vanlife}

Coming back wasn't the original plan. In April 2025 they'd secured a one-year working holiday visa for Finland, booked flights and a month in Helsinki, with the idea of working there for five months and then travelling. They knew the money was running out, and they didn't want to go back to their old jobs.

Finland excited them as individuals. As new citizens they could access it, and they wanted to experience something wild. Then they asked whether it would help the business. A new country, a new language and job hunting from zero all said no. Changing their minds cost them money, and they were sure New Zealand would earn it back, which it did. They were excited, too: they'd never travelled New Zealand deeply, slowly and intentionally.

### Why a van

New Zealand had got expensive. They looked at options like [house sitting](https://abigailhannah.nz/151-house-sitting-across-new-zealand-with-travel-writer-jessica-lockhart/) but weren't confident about it. What they did have was a van, bought in March 2022, and they'd loved a two-week trip in it in 2023.

So they gave themselves six months, all in on the van and all in on Instagram, to answer three questions: could they make Instagram work, could they earn enough to sustain it, and did they love [van life](https://abigailhannah.nz/121-avoiding-vanlife-mistakes-and-life-on-the-road-in-nz-with-tiff-and-nathan/)? Ami had her doubts, because she has a thing about public toilets.

I'm on your side, Ami.

The answers: yes on Instagram, with lots still to learn about monetising, and a firm yes on van life in New Zealand. Since September 2025 they've been travelling around the South Island.

Van life still looks like a very white activity to me, so I love seeing them out there doing it loudly and showing people what's possible. It's important to see representation, and it brings more diversity to the travel space.

Sagar and Ami call travel a privilege too. They've been to 26 countries, and they know slow travel around New Zealand is a dream for so many people. They love meeting like-minded creators on the road too: other crazy people, as Ami puts it, and she loves having that community.

## Nelson Tasman favourites, from Wharariki to Rawhiti Cave {#nelson-tasman}

Abel Tasman wasn't on the plan. In Napier they met an older couple who'd been doing caravan trips for a long, long time, and Tōtaranui campground was one of their favourite campsites, so Sagar and Ami had to go.

Ami is in love with the Mackenzie region and wanted to get there quickly. She thought they'd see everything in two days and suggested three or four nights. Spoiler: no. Abel Tasman has everything, in their words: beaches, blue springs, [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/), kayaking on the Tasman Sea and a Great Walk. Once Ami started researching, she realised they'd need days on end, so they dropped pins on the map and went around them slowly.

A lot of visitors skip the top of the South for the Mackenzie, Queenstown, Wānaka and the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/), which is one of the reasons I love it: fewer people. A couple of summers ago Isaac and I spent [six weeks in the South Island](https://abigailhannah.nz/192-six-week-road-trip-in-the-top-of-the-south-island-with-abigail-isaac/) and got no further south than [St Arnaud](https://abigailhannah.nz/108-fastpacking-from-st-arnaud-to-arthurs-pass-in-8-days-with-alice-brice/), between Marlborough, Abel Tasman and a hike that finished near [Karamea](https://abigailhannah.nz/133-tramping-the-heaphy-track-from-karamea-to-collingwood-with-jacq-nesbit/). We could have spent all six weeks there.

### Wharariki Beach

Sagar's favourite beach in New Zealand is Wharariki Beach. They first went in the afternoon, in harsh light, and knew straight away they'd be back for sunset, even though they had no plan to stay anywhere nearby. When they came back, it was only the two of them and the whole beach.

Sagar's theory:

> "in New Zealand, there are certain places … that can make you giggle like you are a child."

At Wharariki they kept smiling and giggling at each other as if they'd seen the most beautiful sight in the world.

They never pay for campsites (broke college kids, they say), and Tōtaranui is quite far from Wharariki, so they made an exception at a holiday park five minutes from the beach. When they stayed it was $25 a night per adult, and they rate it: a really nice campsite with everything you need.

### Farewell Spit

Near the northernmost point of the South Island, Farewell Spit was amazing too.

### Te Waikoropupū Springs

At Te Waikoropupū Springs the water is so clear that first-timers will think it can't be real. They were amazed.

New Zealand is constantly out here looking like AI.

I remember the Blue Spring in the North Island before everyone knew about it, when a fountain let you drink straight from the spring. That was my favourite part, and they took it out. The Nelson Tasman springs are even deeper and clearer.

### Rawhiti Cave

Not many people go to Rawhiti Cave, just outside Tākaka. The walk isn't so hard, they say, but it's steep and constantly uphill, and the payoff keeps getting bigger. They felt a certain energy there, something mystical about the formations.

## Via ferrata on Tākaka Hill {#via-ferrata}

They'd never known the via ferrata on Tākaka Hill existed. It's the first in New Zealand, and when we recorded, a second section had opened just after Christmas.

Sagar compares it to a circus act, someone walking a thin rope. He was scared for his life, and he loved the thrill so much he did it twice. Ami didn't go. She wants to build her upper body strength and get out of that fear zone, and when Sagar, who never admits to being scared, said it got scary for him too, she felt she'd made the right call. The second section is her goal for this year.

I'd love a whole podcast on the via ferrata. Some friends of ours took their gear to Europe and did a whole bunch of these routes there. I rock climb, and this was different: you're on rungs, and the little bridges were a bit scary. I pep-talked myself the whole way and was the most scared in our group, and I finished it. The second section has ladders facing away from the cliff, with nothing below you, and a bench where you can dangle your legs over Tākaka Hill.

It's free, and you can bring your own gear or hire it, but following the right procedures and safety measures is still your responsibility. There are a few emergency exits if you've had enough halfway. If you're scared, it's okay to not give it a go.

## Abel Tasman Great Walk and kayaking to Adele Island {#abel-tasman}

If you don't have time for the whole Abel Tasman [Great Walk](https://abigailhannah.nz/10-great-walks-in-10-days/), Sagar and Ami's tip is to do part of it as a day hike.

* **Water taxi day walks.** Boats have pick-up and drop-off points, so you choose a section for your fitness and time. They picked the longest one for the day, and it gave them a true sense of a Great Walk. They're thinking about walking the whole thing.
* **Kayaking to Adele Island.** They hired a kayak for the day and paddled about two hours out to Adele Island, with seals up close. The wind picked up later, which was a bit scary, and they still got a perfect sunny day.

My first ever [sea kayak](https://abigailhannah.nz/114-sea-kayaking-for-beginners-and-the-best-of-the-hauraki-gulf-with-bennett-lim/) trip was four slow days in Abel Tasman: paddle in the morning, sit on a beach all afternoon, paddle to camp. I fell in love with kayaking and the park. Our next trip there got rained out over New Year, so we kayaked in and walked out. I've done the Great Walk too. I love Abel Tasman so much.

## Van life questions: rain, showers and what's next {#vanlife-questions}

I put some listener questions to them.

### Do you feel cooped up on rainy days?

Yes, rainy days are the toughest, and part of it is acceptance: life has good days and bad days. They check the weather daily and plan for the wet ones:

* **Simple meals.** One-pot rice, because their van isn't a high-roof model, they can't stand up inside, and they don't want to cook outside in the rain.
* **Work days.** They fill those days with work that needs full focus, so the weather doesn't affect them as much.

Same for me: rain meant a work day, sun meant an adventure day.

### What's the hardest part?

Showers, for Ami. They have a camp shower and dip in rivers and lakes, and she's still someone who needs a shower.

Her other challenge is the flip side of the freedom. Because you can go anywhere, it's tempting to hop around, and she'd have missed Nelson Tasman if they'd rushed off to the Mackenzie. As they put it:

> "it's a blessing that we have a flexibility to go anywhere. But in my mind, it's like we jump around a lot because we can go anywhere."

### What's next?

On paper their base is Auckland. They're early in their content creation journey, so plans keep changing. When we recorded, the rough plan was to finish this stretch of van life in March, depending on how the business side goes, and they were thinking about a slightly bigger campervan to be back on the road around September or October.

Starting travel and a business at the same time sounds so hard to me. I spent about a year and a half in Auckland focused on the business before we went into van life. When we recorded I was back there for a month making sure it would keep running, then we were off kayaking. Still no base. Life is interesting when you do it a bit non-standard.
