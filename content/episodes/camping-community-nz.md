Camping is such a key part of Kiwi culture, and so many of us grew up doing it. So I was really excited to sit down with someone who has made it his whole thing.

Jono grew up on an orchard in Hawke's Bay, far enough out of town that he made his own fun, building huts and climbing trees. After moving to Wellington for work, he found himself yearning to get back into camping properly. Then lockdown came, and he started a Facebook group that has grown into a camping community of well over 100,000 people.

So we get into how that happened, his favourite place to camp (which, funnily enough, I'd walked into the weekend before), a soggy group camp where nobody complained, his answers to the most common camping questions, how to get started without spending a fortune, and the night the aurora turned up over his tent. I was grinning the whole way through.

## How Jono got into camping {#jono-story}

Jono remembers his first camping trip as clear as today. He was pretty young, sleeping in a Star Wars A-frame tent among a row of trees in the family orchard, with no lights. He can still picture that tent, and would pay a fair bit to buy it again.

It's not so different now. The weekend before we recorded, he slept outside under a tarp with the fly open: the sky above, the cold air, no luxuries, and the same feelings as back then.

## Starting a camping community in lockdown {#camping-community}

When COVID came and everyone was asked to stay home, Jono did not enjoy being shut inside. Everything he does is outdoors. He figured that if he felt like that, other people would too, and he worried some might come out the other side and decide they didn't need to go outside anymore. So he made it his mission to encourage as many New Zealanders as he could to get outdoors once we all could.

He coaxed a couple of friends and some family into a little Facebook group. Within a couple of years it had well over 100,000 members. He calls it a community now, because people help each other and share ideas, from brand new campers to very experienced ones who are still learning new ways of doing things. Whatever camping means to you, glamping, a powered site or wild camping, they try to show it. And it's grown past camping, with paddle boarding groups and outdoor cooking groups too.

### Taking first-time campers out

One of the things Jono's team does is take first-time families camping, with their own gear. Part of it is environmental: people buy cheap tents, don't enjoy it, and the tents often end up in landfill. So they remove the barriers, like setting up the tent (he reckons that one ends a few relationships), and spend the weekend with families having their very first marshmallows over the fire and their first night sleeping outside, or a parent having a quiet beer.

## Camping at Hot Water Beach on Lake Tarawera {#lake-tarawera}

Jono gets excited before every camp (he says there's probably something not quite right with him), but one of his favourite places is Hot Water Beach on the edge of Lake Tarawera. You can walk in, a five-hour hike, so not many people do. Otherwise you need to get across the water somehow.

His crew does a six-day camp there, all off jet skis, towing their gear behind and sometimes sending big items over on the water taxi so they can luxury camp. Waking up on the edge of that thermal lake to watch the sun rise and putting your feet in the hot sand is pretty magical.

He prefers it in winter. Pitch your tent and the ground is hot, so the tent is naturally heated, and you can swim in the thermal water all year round, with a lot more steam coming off the water. It's also a DOC campsite, so it's far cheaper than a commercial campground, and affordable for most people.

I was there the weekend before we recorded. We did the five-hour walk in and planned to walk out, but some friends were catching the water taxi, so we took the easy way out. I was surprised how many campsites there were, and it sounds like it's popular with boaties in summer. In May, it was pretty empty. It had been on my list for years, so if there's an adventure sitting on yours, this is your nudge to go and do it.

## A rainy group camp and why getting outside matters {#rainy-group-camp}

One of the community's supporters, a New Zealand camping company, sold a whole lot of tipi tents in a sale, and they became a bit of a craze. So Jono's team gathered everyone who'd bought one for a three-day camp together, all those brand new tents of the same shape grouped in one place.

Then, on the day, it bucketed down. Normally most people would turn back. They couldn't drive to the campsite, so everything had to be carried across a field and over a walking bridge. Everybody came anyway, set up in the rain, got drenched, and helped each other get their tents up. At the end, Jono realised:

> "not a single one of these people complained"

He's heard people complain in perfect conditions about music, tents too close and other people's kids. This time they had every reason, and they were laughing about how much it rained, even when it turned cold.

### Kids sleep better outside

For Jono, getting outside is a break from the pressures of life:

> "getting people outdoors is such a good space for them to forget the pressures of life. You know there's no bills when you're walking a track in the middle of a forest."

The most common feedback after group camps is that the kids slept so well, and that they're often better behaved and eat better. On a group tramp to a hut the weekend before we recorded, three young boys were sword fighting on the lawn in the pouring rain while their mums tried to get them inside. Jono called out to put their hoods back on, and they did straight away, to the mums' astonishment.

He loves that even the most experienced people keep learning. The owner of an outdoor business that's been going for over 40 years told him he loves walking around campgrounds, because he always bumps into some older person who knows a trick he doesn't, a different way to pitch a tent or tie a knot.

## Camping tips: staying warm and packing up {#camping-tips}

These are the questions that come up most in the community. Jono's team doesn't sell any products, and he'll only talk about something he's used and believes works.

### Staying warm in a tent

As the weather gets colder, the big question is how to stay warm.

* **Take a hot water bottle.** We forget it exists, and boiling water is easy outdoors, over a fire, on a gas cooker or with a proper jug if you have power. It doesn't weigh much either.
* **Heat packs** are an option if you want to spend a bit more.
* **Swap the blue single-chamber air bed.** It's great up north in summer, but cold in winter. An insulated air mat is a much better choice.

When I'm tramping, I fill a Nalgene bottle with boiling water to take to bed, in a hut or a tent, because I'm a super cold sleeper.

### Getting the tent back in the bag

The other big one is packing a tent. Tents come in so many shapes and sizes, and Kiwis want the biggest tent on the campsite (Jono doesn't think they need it). It will always go back in its bag, because that's how it started. It's about knowing the folding technique, and there are lots of tutorials in the group (and if yours won't go back in, they'll take up the challenge).

My husband can always get a sleeping bag so much smaller than I can, while I'm still trying to shove mine into its tiny bag.

### Stage your pack-up

Nobody likes packing up at the end of an amazing trip, including Jono. His advice:

* **Do it in segments.** One person takes the inside, another the outside, and swap who's looking after the kids.
* **Take breaks.** You don't have to smash it all out at once. After a hard job, like getting the sleeping bag back in, sit down, have a laugh and do something easy.
* **Keep a treat in the car.** A can of Coke, a chocolate bar or a coffee, whatever yours is, as a reward.

Unpacking at home is the bit I dread most. Jono dreads it too, but you've got to look after your gear, because it isn't cheap.

## Getting started with camping on a budget {#camping-on-a-budget}

Jono's first suggestion is their page, where you can ask questions. Then ask friends, family and colleagues, because he'd be baffled if most people couldn't borrow a tent. Kmart and The Warehouse sell affordable gear, but he also suggests asking on your local Facebook community page whether anyone has camping gear they don't use. Kiwis love to buy something, quickly upgrade (he's guilty of it), and keep the old one in the garage, so plenty of people have five tents and would happily part with one. There's probably someone in your area with gear they never use who would love to hand it over to a family wanting to start. New Zealand also has some world-leading tent and outdoor gear designers.

And you don't need much:

> "you don't actually need a lot to get outdoors"

You need some shelter and something to sleep under. In summer, despite what people think, a blanket on the ground can be just as nice as a big thick mattress if you've got a good campsite.

We got started with gear from friends and family. Then an outdoor store was closing down and we got a tent at a big discount, and these days our go-to is Facebook Marketplace, where you can find good deals on secondhand gear that's been used once or twice.

### From a trailer full to a school bag

Jono started out wanting everything he could get his hands on: a big trailer, a big car, a fridge. These days his goal is to fit everything into a school bag, and anything he takes has to do at least three things. For him it's about being minimalist: one sharp knife, no cutlery or can opener, one little blanket. Members may be surprised, because the setups they see from the team can be elaborate, but that's to show all the different ways you can camp.

Most of the time when I'm camping, I'm tramping too, so I'm on the lightweight journey myself, because I don't want to carry a heavy tent for hours. And with consumerism and climate change, I'm all for being conscious of what we buy and throw away, so we can look after the outdoors for the generations to come.

## Bucket list campsites, the aurora and what's next {#bucket-list-campsites}

Jono doesn't have one bucket list campsite. It's the one he doesn't know about yet. They'll head down a path, step off it and find a spot: a little waterfall, or a stream curling around a cliff face. They'll cook an amazing meal, the stars will come out, and they'll think it's the best site they've ever had, until the next one.

### The night of the aurora

On a personal camp, work kept them home longer than planned, so they didn't make their original campsite, which was deep in a dark valley. Instead they camped at a regional park close to home in the North Island. Jono wanted to camp down by the river, but it was freezing, so he went way up high to find the sun for his solar panel. He'd normally never camp up there. That night, cooking dinner, the sky looked red. He snapped a photo, and on the camera screen it was the [aurora](https://abigailhannah.nz/027-astrophotography-larryn-rae/), looking a whole lot more impressive than it did to the eye. In that deep, dark valley, he doesn't know if they'd have seen it, so they're pretty thankful their plans changed.

### Coming up

When we recorded, Jono was about to take his son camping. He has three kids and takes each of them camping one-on-one, so they get to do whatever matters to them. Then a group tramp with first-time trampers, tent camping in a beautiful forest. They've been getting to know each other in a group chat beforehand. They'll have to carry all their own gear, and there might be some grumbles along the way, but he knows they'll feel pretty accomplished by the end, and there's something special in store for them at the campsite.
