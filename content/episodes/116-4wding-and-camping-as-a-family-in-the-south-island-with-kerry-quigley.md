I've had a few four-wheel driving episodes on the podcast, but they're usually with singles or couples. So I was really excited to chat with Kerry, because he does it all with his wife and two young kids in tow.

Kerry lives in Dunedin and runs a four-wheel drive shop there, but his real passion is four-wheel driving, [overlanding](https://abigailhannah.nz/overlanding-nz/), camping and getting outdoors with the family. And he didn't start with a fancy rig. He started with a Honda Civic, a big tent and a chilly bin on the back seat.

We talk about how he got that itch, what a typical family weekend away looks like (spoiler: they always forget something), a wild stormy night near the Hopkins, and how to know when to call a trip off. Plus his must-have gear for camping with kids, why you should borrow before you buy, and where to find your people.

## How Kerry got into overlanding {#kerry-story}

Kerry grew up in Invercargill and moved around a lot. They had enough to get by, but there wasn't money for gear. Even for school camps, he had to borrow it.

The biggest trigger for his love of the outdoors was his time in the army. You're working, but you're still out there camping, and he learned a lot of skills and a real sense of preparedness. Later in life, once he was able to, he could explore that itch.

He also used to do quite a bit of hunting, and his four-wheel driving stemmed from that. He and his wife started very basic: the Honda Civic, a big tent, the chilly bin, and the Labrador they had at the time. They spent their first wedding anniversary camping at Mavora Lakes, doing walks for a few days.

From there they built on it, buying more and more capable vehicles as they could, and getting amongst like-minded people.

People see what Kerry has now and assume you need all that to start. He knows he's been fortunate to get into a business with access to that gear. But:

> "you don't need a lot. … And we didn't start off with a lot."

When you're starting out, you don't know if you'll enjoy it, so there's no point sinking heaps of money into gear first.

## Taking young kids four-wheel driving {#kids-overlanding}

They've taken both kids out from as early as they could. Their first family camping trip with their youngest was a tent at the campground in Hanmer Springs. They're both full-time workers, so it comes down to weekends and holidays, but wherever they can, they get out as a family.

It means sacrifices around home, but for them it's a reset. He sees a sense of calm come over the kids outdoors. On a beach trip the weekend before we recorded, his son was in the waves and the water at every opportunity (lucky they took plenty of spare clothes).

He finds kids outside make their own fun:

> "When they get outside, they just make their own fun. They don't need all that stuff."

It resets the adults too. Getting somewhere with no cell coverage, where nothing can interrupt you, is about the most relaxing thing he can think of. When he turned 40, he skipped the big party and went to a beach camp with a couple of friends, cooking a lamb roast over the campfire.

That sounds like the best way to turn 40. I hope I'm doing the same when I get there.

## A typical family overlanding weekend {#typical-trip}

Every trip looks different, and sometimes the morning before is absolute chaos.

What helps is that a lot of their gear stays in the truck. So it's pack the tent, the sleeping gear, some clothes and food, and away they go. For an overnighter, they'll leave on the Saturday morning with the kids and the dog. They try to plan overnighters so the dog can come, because a lot of places in New Zealand don't allow dogs. For anything longer, she goes to kennels, because it's not fair on her sitting in the back with the kids.

Kerry's other tip is go bins. After a trip, once everything has aired out, put your gear in bins ready to throw in the car.

And they always forget something. In the school holidays they spent a couple of nights at the campground in Riverton, exploring Gemstone Beach and fossicking for precious stones with the kids. They forgot so many things they had to go to the supermarket. That's part of getting four people out of a house.

We're [van life](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/) people, so most of our camping gear stays in the van all the time. The worst is when you take something out for something else, forget to put it back, and it gets left behind.

## St Bathans, the Omarama Saddle and a stormy night {#st-bathans-storm}

One of Kerry's most memorable trips was one of their first with a group of families, all with kids of different ages and vehicles at different stages of build.

They went out the back of St Bathans. With a capable vehicle, you can drive up either the West Branch or the East Branch of the Manuherikia River, and both take you up to the Omarama Saddle, with epic views over the Mackenzie Basin. They went past Boundary Creek Hut and stayed the night at Top Hut, in warm weather under clear skies.

The next day they went over the saddle through to Omarama, planning to reach Monument Hut in the Hopkins. Wind and rain started coming through, and when they got to the river, it was completely impassable. With families on board, you make the safe decision. The dads were having a yarn about it, and Kerry could see the wives shaking their heads.

With family in the car, he never takes unnecessary risks, because you never want them having to bail out of a vehicle in a river. Your decisions are a lot more careful than if you were out with a group of mates, pushing things.

### The night at Temple Campsite

They pulled back to Temple Campsite, a DOC campsite about halfway back down the road with a couple of tramping tracks and a wee shelter. As they set up, the wind got more and more extreme. A couple of awnings went, and they made a windbreak with the vehicles for the families with smaller adventure tents.

Through the night came more rain and lightning crashing just down the valley. They could hear the wind coming through the trees like a freight train, a rumble, and then the tent would shake. The kids were zonked out all night, completely oblivious. The parents kept waking up and checking they were still in the tent. One of the other mums got hit in the face by her tent wall, the wind was pushing it in that much.

Everyone still enjoyed it, and the kids were jumping in puddles the next morning. It's one of those trips where you look back and say you've camped in worse. As long as the kids have warm gear and good sleeping bags, and hot water bottles on cold nights, you can still enjoy it, even while you're wondering if your tent will survive the night.

## When to call off a trip in bad weather {#bad-weather-calls}

Knowing when to call it quits comes with experience. A few weeks before we recorded, after their Riverton stay, they'd planned a couple of days at some lakes. But the rain kept coming and the temperature dropped to about four degrees, so Kerry and his wife talked it through. You can handle being wet and warm. Wet and cold, trapped in a tent, is harder to manage, especially for kids, who don't always know to put more clothes on.

In a group, everyone is part of the decision. If anyone isn't comfortable, they back out and go home. It's the same with any obstacle in four-wheel driving: you decide as a collective, and you respect the person or group who isn't comfortable, because otherwise someone's vehicle gets damaged or someone gets hurt, and you don't want that on your conscience.

> "The thing about getting out outdoors is it's supposed to be fun, and if not everybody's having fun, then what's the point?"

## Back to Monument Hut via the Hawkdun Range {#monument-hut}

That was the last night of the trip, and these days most Waitangi weekends they do a trip. The last one, they all met up in Ranfurly and went over the Kyeburn and into the Hawkdun Range, then up the other branch to the Omarama Saddle. They went more than 24 hours without touching tar seal before they hit Omarama.

And this time they made it to Monument Hut. The wind was blowing down the valley so hard they could see dust coming off the river, but they found a sheltered spot in the trees, and the kids made up adventures in the forest.

Kerry loves taking the kids to places he hasn't been before. When they're grumpy, it's hard for them to grasp, but some of their friends will never stand at 1,700 metres looking out over the Maniototo for miles and miles.

## Gear for camping with kids, and borrowing first {#gear-for-families}

Kerry's must-haves for families:

* **Warm gear.** Layers that are easy to put on and take off, especially for kids.
* **First aid kits.** They carry several in the truck.
* **A** [**PLB**](https://abigailhannah.nz/plb-safety/) at minimum. You never know, and kids don't always make the soundest decisions.
* **A good chilly bin.** It doesn't have to be an expensive fridge. A good chilly bin will keep food cold for a couple of days.
* **Gear that lasts.** They've had the same Jetboil for 13 years, and it still boils the water for their coffee and the kids' hot water bottles.

I wish I'd had that chilly bin advice sooner. When we did 10 days around Northland, our ice would melt within hours and our fresh veggies would end up sitting in cold water. We ditched the chilly bin and went shopping more often. Listen to Kerry, not us.

His bigger point is to borrow someone's gear first, to see if you really want to get into it. Camping gear isn't cheap, and he hates seeing people buy really cheap gear that breaks on the first use and gets dumped. Borrow good gear, then start buying once you know.

I'm so glad he brought this up. When we started camping, it was in a little borrowed pup tent with a tiny borrowed stove, and there's no way we'd have got started otherwise. So much good gear sits in garages, and we can all be better at lending it, and at asking. The answer will most likely be yes.

### Fishing as a family

Kerry gave up hunting a couple of years ago because he had too many hobbies, but he's recently got back into fishing. His wife did a women's fishing course through Fish & Game, his son caught his first fish at a take-a-kid-fishing day at one of Dunedin's stocked reservoirs, and the youngest is always catching something with a little plastic fish on the end of the line. If they're out for a few days, the fishing rods come too.

## Finding your overlanding community {#community-next}

What Kerry wishes he'd learned sooner is that you don't need much to get out there. And you've got to realise how awesome a place we live in. A visitor from Australia they took up to Old Woman Hut was blown away to be that high and still have mountains higher around them.

To find your people, try the camping and overlanding Facebook groups and clubs. A lot of Kerry's group came through mountain biking. If you know someone who does it, ask if you can tag along.

Kerry recently did Operation Overlanding with [Jono](https://abigailhannah.nz/106-overlanding-in-the-old-man-range-and-4wd-trips-with-jono-hitchcox/). Next on the list is a trip after Christmas, along the Rainbow Road through [Nelson Lakes](https://abigailhannah.nz/016-nelson-lakes-mel-white/) and down the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/), on back roads wherever possible. The family keeps a list on a blackboard of places they want to visit, and they tick them off as they go.
