Daniel and I met on the road this summer, and it was soooo cool to connect in real life. And when he started telling me about the places he keeps going back to, I noticed a theme. Lighthouses. Lots of lighthouses.

Daniel is German, a mechanical engineer turned [full-time van life](https://abigailhannah.nz/171-planning-an-adult-gap-year-to-full-time-van-life-with-sagar-and-ami/) traveller, and a photographer who first came to New Zealand in 2015. He goes back to his favourite spots again and again, and I've been reflecting on that. I'll often visit somewhere once and figure I've seen it, or go back and leave my camera behind because I won't top last time's photo. This chat made me want to slow right down.

So we cover how he ended up here, the mattress test he uses when buying a car, and his hidden gems: Cape Palliser, Castle Point and French Pass. There's a sea elephant, a pod of hunting dolphins and a sunrise on Mount Stokes with nobody else around. Then we get into how he went from full-time work to living in a van, what he loves about it, and the plans that keep pulling him overseas.

## From Germany to India and back {#daniel-story}

Daniel grew up in Germany. His first trip overseas was to the United States when he was four. After that, most summers were spent on Borkum, a small island in Germany, where lots of other families came every year too. For a kid it was the best holiday ever: buddies to hang out with, volleyball and cycling around the island.

That sounded a lot like a classic New Zealand summer to me. Over New Year's we stayed at a campsite in [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/), and there were so many kids on bikes, cycling around and around the whole campsite. They probably come back every year and meet the same friends.

Later, a family trip to the US took him to a retirement community in Charlotte, North Carolina, where people in their 60s move for the community. It's in a green city with a really nice climate, on a big property where deer come by regularly. Daniel loved it so much that the next year he took half a year of unpaid leave from work in Germany and went back to volunteer there. He calls it the best time of his life.

Volunteering became a bit of a habit. In 2016 he spent time at an orphanage and school in a small town in India, teaching English with no teaching experience at all. He'd never been a fan of spicy food, but India changed that. These days he looks for authentic dum biryani wherever he eats Indian. Restaurants often swear theirs is the real thing, and then it comes out and it definitely isn't.

He also spent a month volunteering at a kids' camp in the Bahamas, which was so humid that even the ocean wasn't refreshing. His tip for the Caribbean: go in the New Zealand summer months, which is their winter.

## How Daniel ended up in New Zealand {#moving-to-nz}

Back in Germany, Daniel was working as a mechanical engineer and project manager. The company hit financial trouble and needed to lay people off, and because he was young, with few responsibilities, they asked if he'd leave and offered him redundancy. He took it.

Instead of looking for a job straight away, he took a sabbatical. He'd already planned a month in New Zealand with a friend, so he stayed longer: three months in 2015, travelling around and making friends he still has today. That sabbatical also took in India, the US and the Bahamas.

Towards the end of his time in India, he had to decide what was next. He had two job offers in Germany without even applying. But he kept thinking about New Zealand, the work-life balance and all the outdoor opportunities, because he's very outdoorsy. He told himself he was a bit crazy and tried to push the thought away. It kept popping up. In the end he asked himself what he had to lose: if it didn't work, he could go back to Germany. So he turned down both offers and decided to go to New Zealand with nothing lined up.

Then his old company, which had gone through bankruptcy and been bought, asked him back. He agreed to join until he left for New Zealand. Funnily enough, they'd just started a project with a company in New Zealand and put him in charge of it. That company offered him a job, and six months later he was here, working for them. He's still here.

## Weekend trips from Hamilton and car camping {#car-camping}

Daniel worked in Te Rapa, Hamilton, for that company for five years. Plenty of people don't like Hamilton, but he rates it because it's so central. He spent a lot of weekends in Rotorua, the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/) and Raglan.

From the start, he bought a car and planned to sleep in it. The first was an old Honda Odyssey, and he built a wooden frame so a mattress would fit in the back. When he upgraded to a newer Odyssey, he turned up to the viewing with a mattress. If it fitted, the car passed. It fitted, so he bought it.

When Isaac and I bought our first car, he wanted a station wagon so we could sleep in the back, so at every viewing he'd fold the seats down and lie in there to check he fitted. I'm a big advocate of adventuring in whatever car you have, and if you want to car camp, that's the tip: check you fit, and check the seats go down flat.

## Cape Palliser lighthouse and seal colony {#cape-palliser}

Daniel's favourite places are quiet spots, where he's had an interesting experience or an amazing sunset or sunrise. Queenstown and Wānaka aren't on the list.

Cape Palliser is the first. It's the southernmost point of the North Island, not too far from [Wellington](https://abigailhannah.nz/021-wellington-gems-local-travel-tilleigh/). You drive from Wellington towards Masterton, over the range, then turn off and follow the coastline along a beautiful, beautiful road until it dead-ends at the cape.

Fun fact: I always forget how angled New Zealand is, and Cape Palliser is further south than the northern point of the South Island. That blows my mind.

### The lighthouse stairs

At the end there's a beautiful lighthouse, up a very long wooden staircase that goes more or less straight up (run it and you'll get a really, really good workout). At the top, a platform looks over the bays, with the lighthouse right in front of you.

Daniel went up to photograph the sunset on his first visit, on a day that wasn't windy. It can be super windy there. On his second visit it was so windy that, when he tried to fly his [FPV drone](https://abigailhannah.nz/038-drone-photography-rotorua-petra-leary/), the wind almost pushed it into the lighthouse. His third visit was stormy, with some of the roads flooded. He wondered if he should turn around, kept going, and was rewarded with an absolutely amazing rainbow. The wild weather brings cool atmosphere, but it can be rough.

When Isaac and I went up a couple of summers ago, it was so so windy at the top that I thought I'd get blown off. We've got funny videos of the wind filling up our clothes and turning us into the Michelin man.

### Falling next to a sea elephant

Close to the cape there's a big seal colony you can walk right into, as long as you watch how the wildlife reacts to you being there. On his first visit, Daniel found a massive sea elephant lying in a little pond. He'd never seen anything like it. He got as close as he could, watched its reaction and tried to make friends.

Next to the pond was a little hill with a steep cliff, so he climbed up to photograph it from above. Then he slipped, lost his balance and fell down the cliff, landing right next to the sea elephant. He sliced his little finger on the rock trying to stop himself (the scar is still there) and smashed his phone. He got up and ran out as fast as he could. Luckily, maybe because they'd made friends, it didn't really care. He's never seen one again.

### Reading the wildlife

Daniel always watches how animals react to him. Sea lions make it easy: they open their mouths and give a little warning, and that's your cue to back up. The seal pups are curious and come to you, and on his third visit a pup came over and sniffed his hand.

At [Kaikōura](https://abigailhannah.nz/054-kaikoura-to-aoraki/), there's a little fence you're not supposed to cross, and the seals cross it all the time, so one will suddenly run across the path in front of you. And in Abel Tasman, I mindlessly stepped over a ring of driftwood someone had built around a nesting bird, and it squawked at me until I went around. Animals are good at telling you when you're too close.

## Castle Point lighthouse and lagoons {#castle-point}

Castle Point is another of Daniel's favourites. From Masterton you turn off and drive for another hour to reach it. It's super rugged, with rocks that form a couple of lagoons. It can be rough and windy there too, so check the wind forecast.

On his first visit he wanted to take a photo like one he'd seen, but it was super cloudy all the way there. Just as he arrived, the clouds opened up and the sun came through, and he took one of his all-time favourite pictures. That's maybe why the place became so special to him. There's a hill you can walk up for a view down to the lighthouse, the ocean and the cliffs on the other side, and he's surfed and swum there too.

There's a dead end at Castle Point where, when we recorded, you were allowed to freedom camp. Space is tight, so you're a bit crammed in with others, especially in a longer van.

We had a beautiful sunset there on a windy day with huge waves, but someone told me recently they'd swum there on a flat, sunny day, so it depends on the weather.

## Busy hotspots and sharing remote places {#sharing-remote-places}

Daniel first saw most of these places in 2015, so I asked what's changed in ten years. His take: the popular spots are getting busier, and the lesser-known places are the same, because most tour companies go to the famous spots.

He sometimes gets hate on Instagram for exposing remote places, but he sees it differently. These places take effort to reach, and tours don't go there, so they won't turn into hotspots. As he puts it:

> "if people really want to go there, they have to take an effort. they need to plan it and it will be worth going there."

For me there's a fine line between protecting places and making them accessible. Cape Palliser and Castle Point take effort to reach, but with hidden spots closer to the usual tourist trail, I think there's a responsibility to keep them pristine.

## French Pass in the Marlborough Sounds {#french-pass}

In the South Island, Daniel's pick is French Pass in the Marlborough Sounds. It's remote and hard to get to, with a long gravel road in, and it's the most peaceful place he's found in all of New Zealand.

The drive alone is worth it. You're driving along the ridgeline at the top of the Sounds, and later in the day, closer to sunset, everything turns golden. He reckons it might be one of the most beautiful drives in the whole country, more beautiful than driving into [Mount Cook](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/). Controversial.

We drove French Pass at sunset too, and the whole hillside turned golden, so I can picture exactly what he means.

Daniel first went in 2015 with a friend from Germany, with no time to stay the night. There's a DOC campground right at the bay, where you can park up at the water, jump out of your van into the ocean and enjoy life. He promised himself he'd come back, and he's since been back twice and stayed a few nights each time.

What to expect:

* **Internet reception**, which is crazy for somewhere that remote. If you work remotely, you can even work there.
* **A pier**, with a lot of fishing going on.
* **Stingrays**, pretty big ones, swimming up and down near the beach. They're curious, so if you sit with your feet in the water, they'll swim around you and move on.

Then there was the morning he woke in the van to a noise that kept going. He went out to look, and a massive group of dolphins was hunting in the bay, jumping and splashing as they chased the fish. It's one of his favourite van life mornings.

## Going full-time in a Ford Transit van {#full-time-vanlife}

After five years in Hamilton, Daniel had already cut back to four days a week, which made long weekends of travel possible. When the project he ran reached a milestone where he was comfortable handing it over, he quit his job and his apartment, put his things in storage and travelled overseas for half a year: Australia, Hawaii, San Francisco, then Thailand and the Philippines.

He came back to New Zealand in December and asked himself, what now? He'd always enjoyed travelling by car and being flexible, so he decided to give van life a go. From the Philippines, he was already looking for vans on Trade Me. One Ford Transit had a really bad ad, and he figured nobody would buy it, but the photos showed a really nice wooden interior. It was in Tauranga, so he checked it out, liked it and bought it.

He set no timeline for how long he'd do it. He changed the electrics to make the van better for staying off-grid for longer stints, started van life and hasn't really stopped. These days he does van life with his partner while they're in New Zealand, and they mostly travel overseas over the winter months. After three and a half months in a bigger motorhome for a collab, a trip overseas and some [house sitting](https://abigailhannah.nz/151-house-sitting-across-new-zealand-with-travel-writer-jessica-lockhart/), they were back in the van, which is a bit small for two. When we recorded, they were planning to sell it and find something else.

I get it. [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/) and I started out in a small, compact van, and [our van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/) now is a little bigger. But after a collab in some massive VW vans, with a bed that drops from the ceiling and a whole shower and toilet unit, ours felt small again. You get used to more space so quickly. I do love that I can sit in the middle of our van and reach everything, though.

Daniel likes that you carry less stuff. You have to choose what comes with you and what stays in storage.

## Van life highs and Mount Stokes at sunrise {#mount-stokes-sunrise}

What Daniel loves most about van life is the flexibility, and where it lets you wake up:

> "I love the flexibility. … being able to park up in the most beautiful piece of nature and right next to the ocean and just open the back doors and hear the sound of the ocean."

He sits at his desk to work, cooks outside and enjoys the scenery. There are plenty of things he doesn't like, but he says that makes up for all of them.

> "just being out there, one with nature, it's just the best feeling ever."

The flexibility is also the challenge, because every day you're deciding where to park up that night. Sometimes he checks out a few spots in an area, then goes back to the first one because it was the nicest all along.

### Mount Stokes in the dark

A listener asked for his favourite van life morning. The French Pass dolphins are one. The other is a sunrise on Mount Stokes, which van life made possible. They parked the van not far from the start, so it was a short drive in the dark to where you park, which is on the road and on quite a slope. They set an early alarm and hiked through the dark, totally alone, through forest (you can get lost on the way up, so take care).

At the top it was already blue hour, the light before sunrise, and then the sun came up over a 360-degree view of the Marlborough Sounds. He rates it the most beautiful view in Marlborough. I asked if it beat Roys Peak: similar, he says, with far fewer people. They had the top to themselves, and it gave them such a boost.

And if his van could talk? It would call its owners very spontaneous, Daniel reckons. It never knows where it's going tomorrow.

## Big plans and a Fiordland bucket list {#fiordland-bucket-list}

There's no cut-off date for Daniel's van life. In some form, he thinks travelling in a van will always be part of them. They don't plan more than about a year ahead, and they'd been weighing up a tiny house or some kind of base against more travel. More travel won. When we recorded, the plan was two and a half months in Africa, several months in Europe and Germany, a possible job with a travel company leading a 38-day tour in Southeast Asia, and maybe a road trip in the US.

In New Zealand, his partner was getting ready to walk the South Island section of [Te Araroa](https://abigailhannah.nz/173-section-walking-te-araroa-over-multiple-seasons-and-battling-cancer-with-te-aroha-hohaia/), which he hasn't done himself. He's a bit jealous, but he needs to keep working.

His own bucket list is Fiordland by water. He's been to Doubtful Sound but never done an overnight cruise there, and he'd love a longer cruise around Fiordland, out to Dusky Sound and the remote places you can only reach by helicopter or boat. Some kind of expedition down there, at some stage.

This summer, Isaac and I tried to get our [kayaks](https://abigailhannah.nz/110-a-sea-kayaking-expedition-along-the-fiordland-coast-with-mark-jones/) over to Doubtful Sound, but the logistics were a bit hectic and we didn't get a good weather window. I'm less keen on the sandflies (imagine trying to cook while they maul you), so maybe Isaac can have his kayak expedition and I'll wave from the cruise.
