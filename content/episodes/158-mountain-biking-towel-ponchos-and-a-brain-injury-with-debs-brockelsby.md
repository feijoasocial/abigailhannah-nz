Debs and I had been talking about doing this episode for a long time. She opened by telling me she didn't feel cool enough to be on here. Then she spent the next hour proving exactly why she is. We work together on social media, so I already knew how much energy she brings, and I think you'll feel it too.

Debs owns Stoked NZ, the towel poncho business she started after recovering from a brain injury. She grew up on a farm in the Waikato, spent a stint in Canada, landed her dream job at Cycling New Zealand and has been [mountain biking](https://abigailhannah.nz/047-mountainbiking-nz-laura-goldsworthy/) ever since she bought her first bike at uni. And she makes it all sound soooo fun.

So this one is part beginner's guide, part story. We cover the different kinds of mountain biking, picking a bike, learning skills safely, finding trails and riding buddies, and her favourite places to ride, from [Rotorua](https://abigailhannah.nz/038-drone-photography-rotorua-petra-leary/) to Castle Hill. Then the crash, the long recovery, and how a towel poncho turned into a business. I came away really really keen to ride with her (on the easy trails, please).

## From a Waikato farm to mountain bikes {#debs-story}

Debs has always been outdoorsy. She grew up on a farm in the Waikato, really close to Hobbiton, and spent her childhood on little missions around the farm with her dogs. She learned to drive a tractor at about 11.

Like most kids, she had a bike, but she was rubbish at it, and it wasn't really her thing. Her weekends went on racing cars, which she did for 11 years, and water skiing. She calls it the coolest family time, and she knows not everyone gets that opportunity.

At uni, she started working at R&R Sport, back before it became Torpedo 7, and got her first mountain bike there. She's been riding ever since, and loves to hike too, though her partner is more of a "why would you hike when you can ride it" person.

## Riding and swimming around Rotorua {#rotorua-life}

Debs and her partner moved to Rotorua because they were already coming every weekend to ride in the Whakarewarewa Forest, which is incredible for hiking and riding (and horse riding, which isn't her thing).

Everyone knows about the Blue Lake and the main lake, and living there, she's found so many hidden local spots too. In summer she rides or swims, or both, every day, and weekends add hiking and water skiing whenever they can rope in friends with boats.

They've recently moved rural, and they can ride straight from home into the forest. She reckons it'll be faster to bike to the lake in summer than to drive and sit in traffic, and there are hot pools right there too. As Debs puts it:

> "living in New Zealand I think we don't know how lucky we are … we have so much goodness on our doorstep"

## Canada and a dream job at Cycling New Zealand {#canada-cycling-nz}

Debs was a straight-A student, a full-blown nerd by her own description. At the end of uni, she bought a one-way ticket to Canada, with no job, no plan and no worries, and had the time of her life.

She worked the graveyard shift at a Denny's in Revelstoke three nights a week, so she could snowboard every day. The truckies on the main highway loved a yarn with someone with an accent, and she saved her tips to buy an F-150 for shuttling bikes in summer. Then her visa ran out, and she came home.

Back home, she started a master's, then landed her dream job: a high performance internship across Rowing New Zealand and Cycling New Zealand, which led to a full-time role at Cycling New Zealand as a performance and technique analyst. She travelled to places like Colombia, Poland and LA with the Olympic track cycling team.

It was a dream job, and it wasn't always as glamorous as it sounds. That comes back into the story later.

## Types of mountain biking and choosing a bike {#types-and-bikes}

There are a few different flavours of mountain biking, and Debs likes a bit of everything:

* **E-biking** is its own little subset. Debs rides an e-bike (judge her if you like) because she can ride in an hour what would usually take her four, and still get to the lake.
* **Downhill** riders love riding downhill, on a downhill-specific bike. Those bikes aren't made to be climbed. They're made to go on the back of a van and be shuttled.
* **Enduro** is timed over the whole thing, and then there's **trail riding**.
* **Cross-country** is for the super fit, charging around the forest in Lycra. Cross-country bikes are lighter, some have no suspension, and the tighter head angle means you climb far more efficiently.

Cross-country isn't her cup of tea. Debs rides the ups so she can do the downs, and she's there for the views. She loves scenic riding like the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/), though she hasn't ridden the whole thing yet: their trips south in the van tend to mean parking up, riding and coming back to the van. With six weeks down south planned for January and February, maybe this is the year.

On foot, the Old Ghost Road takes me five days. On a bike, it only takes two.

### Picking your first bike

Some of Debs's friends deliberate over bike specs for months. Her approach is to get on different bikes and see how much fun you have, because wheel size, suspension and angles can get overwhelming fast. Hire a bunch of bikes, or borrow your mates', and try a few types. One might climb better, another might be more fun downhill, and it all makes more sense on the trail.

Her simplest starting question: do you like going up hills, or do you like going down hills more?

## Learning mountain bike skills safely {#learning-skills}

How do you progress, and do it safely? Debs's advice:

* **Get coaching.** There are great coaches around, including one in Rotorua she rates as the kindest human ever, who teaches everyone from total beginners to World Cup racers.
* **Relearn the basics.** If you haven't ridden in ages, the gears and brakes can be quite scary. Gears work differently on different bikes, and in some parts of the world the front and back brakes are set up the other way round, so check before you get on.
* **Start on flat grass** before you head into the forest.
* **Use the trail grades.** The tracks are all graded, so there's something for everyone. In the Whakarewarewa Forest, the Forest Loop goes right around the outside of the forest, down past the Blue Lake and the Green Lake. It's long, so don't do the whole thing straight off the bat (sore butt), but you can ride parts of it.

There's not much gravel in the forest. Part of it is tar-sealed, and most of it is dirt, quite rooty, with a lot of pumice, so in lots of places it dries really fast. Debs mostly rides really rooty, steep tracks. Think tramping tracks like Urchin, near Tūrangi, and steeper.

I nearly went up Urchin a month before we recorded. It was 6am, it was cosy and warm in the van, and I decided I didn't need to see the sunrise. I'd been up it a couple of years earlier, and it's a really cool track.

I got super into biking in my final year at boarding school in India, up in the hills, riding back roads through tea fields and rice paddies. Once a year we'd ride about three hours downhill, all switchbacks, and one year my chain broke right as the last little uphill started. My bike went in the ute and I got a ride up. Pretty stoked.

## Finding trails, rail trails and riding buddies {#trails-and-community}

### Trailforks and council websites

Debs's top tool for finding where you can ride is Trailforks, an app with offline maps. It covers paid parks and DOC tracks, it has grades, so you can see which trails suit your level, and it's global. There's a free version and a paid one.

For rail trails and river trails, she'd look on council websites. Around Alexandra and Clyde, the tracks are mostly gravel and grade one to two, which means wide and quite flat, and the Waikato has plenty now too. Debs rode the Dunstan Trail on her last trip south and says it's well worth doing for the scenery.

### Riding solo or with a crew

You can totally ride on your own. Debs has a first response sticker on the back of her helmet, so people can get help if there's an accident. She rides alone, with her partner, on girls' rides on Tuesdays and Wednesdays and with lots of mates. There's always someone keen.

For finding community, there are so many women's groups, all welcoming women of all ages and riding abilities:

* **Dirt Town Queens**, around Queenstown.
* **Mud Maidens**, based in Rotorua, with riders coming from Tauranga, Taupō and all over for their events, plus social rides.
* **Sunday social rides** for women, run by two of her friends.

Mountain bike clubs, like the Rotorua Mountain Bike Club, run rides too, and joining one helps fund trail maintenance and new trails. Mountain bikers are often climbers and skiers as well, so meet a few people somewhere new and you've got friends for all the activities.

There are clubs for literally anything you want to do: [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/), hiking, [jet boating](https://abigailhannah.nz/125-jetboating-27-wild-rivers-on-a-solo-trip-with-ollie-sharpe/), [four-wheel driving](https://abigailhannah.nz/128-4wd-adventures-across-the-length-of-the-south-island-with-rich-soult/) and [surfing](https://abigailhannah.nz/learn-to-surf-new-zealand/). I love that you can always find other people out there doing your thing. And it's been forever since I've had someone on to talk mountain biking. Bikepackers, yes, from the ones riding [the whole length of the country](https://abigailhannah.nz/188-bikepacking-3-000km-in-new-zealand-on-tour-aotearoa-with-laura-bell/) to the ones cycling to [every single ski field](https://abigailhannah.nz/117-bikepacking-to-all-23-new-zealand-ski-fields-with-tara-and-naomi/).

## Best places to mountain bike in New Zealand {#best-places}

* **Whakarewarewa Forest, Rotorua.** World-class, in Debs's words.
* **Castle Hill and Craigieburn.** Probably her favourite place in New Zealand to ride, about an hour out of Christchurch. She loves it because hardly anyone goes, and the riding is technical, varied and quite different to the North Island. Sitting up on Helicopter Hill and looking out is mind-blowing.
* **Kaiteriteri.** Bike parks are popping up everywhere. Kaiteriteri is a smaller spot than Rotorua, and they spent an afternoon there on their last trip south and loved it.
* **Queenstown.** One of the classics.
* **Alexandra.** Rocky, and like riding on the moon.
* **Wellington.** There are five or six different places to ride within an hour of the city.

Alexandra was a real mind game for Debs. The first time, she didn't trust the rocks to be grippy, and things she'd hit on dirt felt off-putting. A few days later she was hitting all of it, and loved that sense of achievement.

## The mental side of mountain biking {#mental-side}

The bit Debs loves is following a mate down, hanging on to their line in a flow state, then reaching the bottom absolutely amping.

Her technique tips are to look through the corner, and to look ahead of where you want to go, never at your front wheel.

She can be a bit of a chicken, so she'd rather follow someone over a feature than stop and look at it. From the top it looks gnarly, then you hit it and it's fine. In Alexandra, she went back and rode one feature seven more times.

### Building confidence on technical tracks

A listener asked how to build confidence for more technical tracks. Debs's answer is a supportive group: the women's groups and clubs, plus the coaches lots of clubs bring in. She finds friends at a similar level, or a little better, and follows them. Her disclaimer: make sure they're only a bit better than you, and not Olympians sending it off crazy features.

### Why the variables make it fun

Track cycling, where Debs used to work, is a really controlled environment: an oval, the same temperature, time after time. Downhill has so many variables, like sun that turns to pouring rain in ten minutes. That's what makes it fun.

## Debs's brain injury and recovery {#brain-injury}

Debs hit her head in 2015, riding with friends on little tracks through a backyard at Ōkere Falls, a big [whitewater kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/white-water-kayaking-nz/) spot near Rotorua. She went over a drop no bigger than she'd normally ride, with people on either side heckling and encouraging each other. She doesn't know if she looked away, but she went over her bars and landed really heavily on her left ear.

She was wearing a helmet. She landed so hard that her ear was caked full of mud, and her brain was shaken forwards and backwards. At first she thought she'd broken a rib. She drove herself home that night, feeling rubbish.

The next day, a GP suggested a massage. Debs knew that didn't seem right. Still feeling rubbish a few days later, her partner took her into work, where the doctor who worked with the Cycling New Zealand athletes, who had known her for years, took one look and said she wasn't okay. By then she was seeing stars. An MRI showed bruising on her brain.

That was the start of a long recovery. Delayed signs are quite common with concussion and traumatic brain injury.

* She spent months in a dark room.
* The part of her brain she hit affected her depth perception, so she couldn't tell how far away a car was at a road.
* Landing on her ear affected her vestibular system and her balance, so even a walk outside had her all over the show.
* Her speech was mumbled, and her emotions swung from super happy to super low.

The hardest thing, for Debs, is that nobody can see it:

> "the worst thing about it is you can't see it."

Break a collarbone, and the doctor tells you six weeks. Hit your head, and you look the same, but you can't cross a road. Every brain injury is different, so all they could tell her was that they hoped she'd be herself again. She knew she wasn't. She lost friends and family over that time, because some people couldn't handle the new Debs.

Her partner pushed for her to see a new neurologist, and things levelled out after that. Months in, an occupational therapist suggested kids' colouring books and Lego. One day her partner came home to find her in floods of tears, sure Lego had left pieces out of her Lego monkey. She'd used the wrong piece. She laughs about it now, and it was the darkest time of her life.

When we recorded, it was ten years since the crash. Her head-iversary.

So many people at Cycling New Zealand, Debs included, had tried to create change and couldn't, and hitting her head was a wake-up call:

> "what if you don't get any more tomorrows? Cause at some point our tomorrow's run out. … Was I doing something today that truly set my soul on fire? And I wasn't."

## Starting Stoked NZ and what's next {#stoked-nz}

### From towel ponchos for mates to a business

Debs knew she didn't want to go back. She started making towel ponchos for herself and her mates. Then a wholesale order came in for a thousand, so she flew to Turkey, because she loves Turkish cotton. Turkish towels dry quickly and pack up small, and she'd always travelled with one.

She came up with the name sitting in the stands with a good friend, not long after she'd gone back to work. Her favourite places to travel were Revelstoke and New Zealand, "stoked" was a word she said all the time, and she was stoked to still be here.

Her towel ponchos double as a portable changing room, so she uses one before and after mountain biking. A friend bikepacked across China using one every day as her changing room, towel and pillow.

I've got so many now, for day hikes, beaches, lake swims and the van. They pack down as small as a microfibre towel, and you get a full-size towel.

### Scott Base and a call from Antarctica

Stoked ships all over the world, and Scott Base stocks them after reaching out to Debs.

One Christmas, Debs got a call from a really weird number. It was [Mike Dawson](https://abigailhannah.nz/south-pole-expedition-2/), who lives at Ōkere Falls, in the middle of his Antarctic expedition. He'd forgotten it was Christmas and asked her to send his wife a towel. So she did.

### Raising money for the Breast Cancer Foundation

In October, Stoked raises money for the Breast Cancer Foundation. The year before we recorded, breast cancer survivors drew their boobs on towels and towel ponchos, raising $39,000, with a photo shoot Debs calls the most humbling, empowering, beautiful day.

When we recorded, the next step was a topless swim at Lake Rotoiti on 11 October, with most of each ticket going to the foundation. You didn't have to be a survivor, and you didn't have to go topless for any of it. The fundraising carried on all month.

### Unhinged and Debs's bucket list

There's a track called Unhinged near St Arnaud, by the other Lake Rotoiti. Everyone says don't ride it when it's wet. Debs rode it when it was wet, had a bad time and was so rubbish, so she needs to go back when it's dry.

Beyond that, after a big year of moving rural, there are no mega plans. They're planting a bunch of natives and building tracks on their own land, so they won't even need to leave the backyard.
