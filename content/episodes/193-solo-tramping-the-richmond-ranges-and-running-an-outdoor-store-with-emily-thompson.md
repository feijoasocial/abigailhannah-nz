Emily runs MD Outdoors in Nelson, an all-woman outdoor shop, and she's spent a long time getting other people geared up and kitted out for their adventures. She's been living and exploring Nelson Tasman for over 15 years. So when it came to the Richmond Range, I wanted to hear it from someone who knows it AND sees every Te Araroa walker come through her door.

And this one is a big solo trip. Five days, alone, on one of the tougher sections of the trail, with just enough food (and a bit of trading for noodles along the way). I loved it.

But we cover so much more than the track. How Emily has reinvented her life more than once, the time she got lost on Mount Owen and had to press a borrowed PLB, what it's like being a woman who owns an outdoor store, and the three bits of gear she'd tell any new tramper to invest in. I'm the one in the group who turns up and lets someone else do the planning, so the PLB story hit home. Hard.

So here's the Richmond Range, hut by hut, and everything else we got through.

## Growing up outdoors on the West Coast {#emily-story}

Emily spent a lot of her childhood in the country. From about the age of six she lived on the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/) of the South Island, riding horses, eeling and riding motorbikes, and mostly hanging out with the boys. Being outside was her happy place.

The other side of her family lived in the country too, up in the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/), so she spent a lot of time walking and at the beaches there. She's always preferred to be outside.

Looking back, she doesn't think she appreciated the ruggedness of the West Coast as much as she could have, with all the hiking, climbing and rafting people do down there. But growing up in the country, with the outdoors as your entertainment, is pretty special.

## Reinventing a life in Nelson {#reinventing-life}

Emily first moved to Nelson to study, and she's lived quite a few different lives there since. In her 30s came a real "who am I?" moment and some big changes, and that's when she decided the outdoors was what she loved most, and started gearing her life towards it.

Her CV is a long one: dental assistant, film producer, bookkeeper and software specialist, and she still runs film festivals. She reckons she's reinvented herself about five times, and she thinks anything's possible.

If you're looking for your direction, her advice starts with self-awareness: knowing what excites you and makes you feel alive. She's learned that money isn't what makes her happy, and that sometimes we chase what we've decided life is about, get there and find it isn't as fulfilling as we thought. So she keeps asking herself whether she wakes up excited and passionate about her life. Emily likes the 80-20 rule: 80% of your work should be enjoyable, and the other 20% you suck up and get done. The trap is what she calls the treadmill, doing what you have to without thinking, and the reassessing can come in your 20s, 30s or 40s.

## Planning a solo Richmond Range tramp {#planning-richmond-range}

Working at MD Outdoors, Emily kept hearing about the Richmond Range from the [Te Araroa](https://abigailhannah.nz/164-five-months-sobo-on-te-araroa-and-finding-community-with-deni-estivill/) walkers coming through the shop, because it's one of the more challenging sections. She'd been up to Starveall, but she hadn't walked the whole thing, and she knew she had to.

Early in the business she was working six days a week, so getting away for five days took some doing. A student living with her was meant to come, then pulled out at the last minute. Emily was going regardless, so it became her biggest solo trip yet.

### Pack and food

She went as lightweight as she could, with a pack that was relatively small for five days.

Food was simple. She used Radix meals, because you can count up the calories:

* **Breakfast:** a 400-calorie meal.
* **Lunch:** another 400.
* **Dinner:** a 600-calorie meal.
* **Tailwind:** one for morning tea and one for afternoon tea.
* **A few bars** to top it up.

She didn't take enough food for the output, but it worked out. Eating the same thing every day, though, she wouldn't recommend.

## Richmond Range, hut by hut {#richmond-range-huts}

Emily didn't start at the Pelorus end. She went in from the Aniseed Valley, at the Hacket car park, and her route ran Slaty Hut, Mount Rintoul Hut, Mid Wairoa Hut, Hunters Hut and Red Hills Hut, then out to the road. She'd hoped to do it in four days, and came out on the fifth, which was totally fine.

I've walked to the first hut from the Pelorus end, on a little overnight trip that came up on the [six-week road trip](https://abigailhannah.nz/192-six-week-road-trip-in-the-top-of-the-south-island-with-abigail-isaac/) episode. It's so cool being on trail with Te Araroa walkers and hearing their stories.

### Hacket to Slaty Hut to Rintoul Hut

From the start you're heading up, and over the first two days you traverse the ridgelines of the range, with amazing views. Emily rates the first day as moderate exposure. The second day, heading into Rintoul, is quite exposed. If the wind is high or there's rain, it can get sketchy. It isn't rock climbing, but there are sections where you have to be really careful where you put your feet. When Emily walked it, they were in a bit of a drought, so it was really slippy and gravelly.

Heading up Mount Rintoul, one of the harder sections, she kept seeing a little head bob up ahead, crouched behind a rock. It turned out to be an English tourist who'd come straight from a festival and randomly decided to do the Richmond Range, in a full cotton outfit (the big no-no) and really cheap shoes. He was really struggling, so she told him to get in behind her and led him over the mountain. Once he stopped freaking out, he was quite fit and cool to talk to. They parted at Mid Wairoa, and she never saw him again.

### Mid Wairoa to Hunters Hut

From the bottom of the valley at Mid Wairoa Hut, you walk up beside the river the whole way to Top Wairoa Hut. This is Emily's favourite part of the whole trip, which seems weird with so many high points on the track. She loves rivers, and through there it's like a travel show, with incredible waterfalls and bluey green water. It's still one of the most beautiful trails she's ever walked.

When Emily did it the track wasn't too bad, though people have told her recently that it's a little scrambly. Mid Wairoa Hut itself is so beautiful, and there's a shortcut that she doesn't think a lot of people know about: a DOC track from the Wairoa Gorge Mountain Bike Park goes straight to it, and it's also a shorter way up Rintoul.

Above Top Wairoa Hut you climb onto the saddle, where the hills turn really, really red. It feels like being on Mars, with amazing rock formations.

This was the day that ended at Hunters Hut, the hardest of the trip. At the bottom of the final climb there's a sign giving a time to the hut, and it lies: the climb is longer, and really steep. At the hut, a Te Araroa walker from Prague asked Emily if she'd sworn on the way up. Yes. So had she. Emily's advice: ignore that sign.

The same thing got me on my first solo hike, five days on the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/). On the last day, the map says the trail finishes where it meets a four-wheel drive track, and then you have to keep walking. That's where I sat down and had a big cry before I got up and kept going. It's brutal when the maps lie to you.

### Hunters Hut to Red Hills Hut and out

From Hunters to Red Hills, it's scrambly, up and down river valleys, sidling around different hills, with quite a few short, really sharp climbs over river rock. Emily remembers beautiful waterfalls, and even though she was on a mission, she took the time for the odd swim.

She'd hoped to get right out. But there were some really lovely ladies at Red Hills Hut, so she stayed. On the fifth morning she walked the last little section, cutting through a mountain bike park onto the main road, then hitchhiked to St Arnaud, like a lot of walkers do.

There was some food trading in the huts too: her Radix meals for other trampers' plain noodles, and everyone was stoked. The walker from Prague, an orienteer who was tearing through the track, finished ahead of her, and they met up again at the lodge at St Arnaud, which for Emily is the best way to end any hike in the area.

### Breaking it up

Emily's days were long, and it's tough tramping, with a lot of up and down on technical terrain. Lots of people break it into shorter days, and with so many huts there are plenty of options, like stopping at Old Man Hut between Slaty and Rintoul. Her one rule: take a lot of food.

There's nowhere to resupply in the Richmond Range, so food is a big factor. When I talked to [Billy Meredith](https://abigailhannah.nz/157-setting-the-fastest-self-supported-record-on-te-araroa-with-billy-meredith/), who set the fastest known self-supported time on Te Araroa, he'd underestimated his food too, and did the last day or two through the Richmond Range on a calorie deficit. That did not sound fun.

## Getting through the hard days solo {#solo-mental-strategies}

Micro goals are Emily's big one. She also runs [ultras](https://abigailhannah.nz/187-from-beginner-trail-running-to-setting-course-records-with-david-zebedee/), with a 100k last year, and on a hike it's the same: get to this point, and that's all you think about. Think too far ahead and it stresses you out.

Her other strategies:

* **Eat consistently.** Even on minimal food, she'd have some Tailwind or half a bar every 45 minutes, so her blood sugar and energy didn't drop too far.
* **Take regular breaks, on a timer.** She'd set a timer, stop for five minutes, and never let herself stop for longer than that.
* **Keep reminding yourself you can do this.**

There were beautiful moments too. On the saddle above Top Wairoa Hut, it wasn't a busy time of year for that section, and she couldn't see anyone. Being out there completely alone is still one of her favourite feelings: independence, and pride in having made it that far.

Walking out on the last day, she felt so proud of herself. As Emily puts it:

> "Trips like that, you just grow as a person so much because you realise you're capable of so much more than you ever thought."

She'd like to walk the whole thing on her own every year, as a bit of a pilgrimage.

When I did my first solo hike, I was very generous with my breaks early on, because I could stop whenever I wanted. Then I realised I still had to get to the end of the trail, so I love the idea of a timer.

## Mount Owen, a borrowed PLB and a helicopter {#mount-owen-plb}

Being alone doesn't faze Emily. She moved to Australia pretty much on her own at 16, and she's more of a leader. What she has to make sure of is the knowledge.

Before the Richmond Range she'd done one other solo mission: Mount Owen. Every group has a planner and someone who turns up, and Emily was the one who turns up. A friend she's walked the Queen Charlotte Track with did all the planning, sometimes even the food.

Me too, Emily. I've never booked a Great Walk: someone else books it and I show up.

Physically, Mount Owen was easily within her reach. Navigation was another story, and she was a little blasé (she's a glass-full person).

She got to the hut at two, with heaps of summer daylight left, and decided to go for the summit that day. A few people at the hut had just come back down, said the weather was looking great, and asked if she had a [PLB](https://abigailhannah.nz/plb-safety/). She didn't: her friends always had them. So one woman gave Emily hers, and insisted she take it.

She reached the top easily. Coming down, there are no signs, only cairns, and everything looks the same up there, so she followed the wrong one. With no topo map on her phone, she ended up about 10 kilometres off the track, trying ridgeline after ridgeline until she knew she was lost.

She pressed the PLB, and the helicopter came. The crew told her they wouldn't have been able to come in any later, because the weather then turned really bad.

It was humiliating, and humbling: she'd never thought anything like that would happen to her. It was a big life lesson in being prepared, and she's glad she went through it, because now she's a stickler for all the safety rules. MD Outdoors hires out PLBs, and her case for carrying one goes beyond your own safety:

> "it's about your safety, but it's also about if anything happens, it just makes it so much easier for people to find you."

Think of the helicopter and the search and rescue teams' time. She'd add topo maps on your phone, because you can GPS yourself so easily.

We only got our own PLB this year, and before that we always rented one for the backcountry. My first solo hike taught me I was responsible for everything in my bag, because I was the whole group. So do something solo, and it doesn't have to be the Richmond Range for your first one.

## Running MD Outdoors in Nelson {#md-outdoors}

MD Outdoors was already established, in a little container, when Emily bought it. She'd been casually filling in for a guy she knew, and the energy hooked her. Film is intense, accounting is draining, and the outdoor industry is people's happy place: they spend their money there because they want to, and you hear about their cool adventures all the time.

She brought her background in advertising and media to the shop, added the marketing, and moved location, which made a big difference. When we recorded, the store was in Nelson city, next to Macpac, with a second store about to open in Wakefield.

They stock gear they believe in, test a lot of it themselves, and never sell anyone something they don't think is fit for purpose.

The shop does a lot with the community:

* **An Adventure Film Festival** every year, which was Emily's idea. People film their adventures on GoPros or even an iPhone, and the best end up on the big screen, with lots of prizes from the sponsors.
* **Intro to hiking workshops** and **safety workshops**.
* **Community events**, including quiz nights.
* **A location of the week**, a blog about a different place to go hiking, which is hugely popular.

It's ended up being all women, which Emily never planned.

## Owning an outdoor store as a woman {#women-outdoor-store}

At events, people have asked her if she's the receptionist, or the admin who's come along for the day.

She's done a lot of business already, so she's decided never to think of being a woman as a thing, and she's never let it be intimidating.

She does think she brings something. She's not a purely money-driven person, and she sees discounting at some outdoor businesses that shows they don't value the brands, or what things are worth. Whether that's because she's a woman or her own ethos in business, she suspects it's a bit of both. What she wants is for MD Outdoors to add value to people's lives, in an industry that isn't harming anyone and makes people's lives cool.

I resonate with all of that. It's why I do what I do: I want to see more people out there, and make the outdoors accessible.

## Three pieces of gear for new trampers {#beginner-gear}

Emily's three for anyone brand new to tramping:

* **A really good pack.** At MD Outdoors they stock brands like Osprey and Deuter, but you have to try packs on to see what's right. Spending a bit more on a brand that specialises in packs is worth it.
* **The right footwear.** There's no reason you need blisters or sore feet. Altra, La Sportiva and Topo are among the many brands of shoes and boots that will make your feet happy.
* **Hiking poles.** If you want to do this for the rest of your life, look after your body. Poles protect your knees and get you up hills easier, because you're using your whole body.

Packs and shoes have to be a personal choice. Packs fit everybody's bodies differently, and we all have different feet, so try on a couple of things to find what works for you.

## What's next for Emily {#whats-next}

The last two years have been ultra-focused for Emily, with a lot of training for the 100k, and Backyard Ultras with her partner. Now she's reframing things and stepping back from running a bit, because she's realised what she loves most:

> "as much as I love that, I truly love hiking more. … I don't want nature for me to be a racetrack."

For her, it's more spiritual, the place she goes to refresh herself and do epic adventures.

When we recorded, she was about to ride the West Coast Wilderness Trail for her birthday, in the rain, to test some new gear. With lots on at work, she's added a list of smaller missions, and there's so much around Nelson to hit whenever you want.

The bigger one on her radar is the whole South Island section of Te Araroa, walked fast, as an early autumn adventure. She won't be a purist about it, and will probably hitchhike some of the boring bits.

My husband wants to do Te Araroa, and I'm not too keen, but I do want to do the Richmond Range. And maybe that will inspire me to do more [sections](https://abigailhannah.nz/173-section-walking-te-araroa-over-multiple-seasons-and-battling-cancer-with-te-aroha-hohaia/) later.
