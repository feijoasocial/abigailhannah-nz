I did the via ferrata on Tākaka Hill last summer. It was the first time I'd ever come across one, and I've been really really curious about it ever since: who builds a steel wire across a cliff, and how do you get permission to do it?

So I asked the man himself. Till Middelhauve is the founder of the project behind New Zealand's first public via ferrata. He's originally from Germany, started [paragliding](https://abigailhannah.nz/paragliding-new-zealand/) the moment he was legally allowed to, and spent his summers climbing via ferratas around the European Alps. Then he moved to New Zealand in 2020 and found nowhere to climb one. So he built one (with a lot of paperwork and a lot of help).

This post covers what a via ferrata is, the two and a half years of bureaucracy, how you plan a route on a crumbly cliff, and what it's like to climb. Plus the practical bits: gear hire, cost, who can do it, how to stay safe, and when to go (he has a very good tip for last-minute people like me).

## From paragliding to via ferrata in the Alps {#till-story}

Till got very passionate about paragliding at 16 and was a little bit addicted for years, flying competitions in the European Alps and using it as an excuse to travel the world. Like every outdoor pursuit, he says, you can do it safely or in a crazy way. He's been flying for 32 years now, though he doesn't paraglide much these days.

A pilot friend introduced him to [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/), but it never became a big thing for him. He was more into tramping in the Alps. Then a tramping mate took him on his first via ferrata, at Kandersteg in Switzerland, a climb up a waterfall line with all sorts of bridges. It's still one of his favourites. He liked having a reassuring wire to clip into, which is a lot nicer on the anxious part of your brain.

### A family hobby in a camper van

When his kids were young, the family travelled the European Alps in a camper van and turned via ferrata into a family hobby, stopping at one every other day. They started on the easy routes and worked up the grades. In Europe there's a series of five guidebooks describing 1,100 routes, so you pick the book for your region and choose the grades that suit you. They spent whole summers climbing.

## What is a via ferrata? {#what-is-via-ferrata}

Via ferrata is Italian for iron way. It's a steel wire attached to a cliff face along a climbing route, and Till says it's so strong you could hang a truck off it. You clip yourself into the wire, and as long as you're clipped in, you can take a fall and slide along the cable. On Tākaka Hill, they haven't had any accidents over thousands and thousands of climbs.

Via ferratas were invented in World War I, to reach alpine peaks for the fighting. When the war ended, the routes stayed, and people started climbing them for fun. Some old routes remain, especially in the Dolomites (they wouldn't pass the engineers' tests here), but most via ferratas today were built purely for recreation.

## Bringing via ferrata to New Zealand {#via-ferrata-nz}

When Till's family moved to New Zealand in 2020, they found no via ferratas apart from one in Wānaka, a commercial and pretty expensive tourist attraction aimed at overseas visitors. He wanted to climb one, so he started looking into why there were none, assuming some liability issue was in the way.

The chance to dig into it came during COVID. After a trip back to Germany, the four of them spent two weeks in a managed isolation hotel room, and Till wrote his first concept there. A lawyer contributed free time, and together they worked out there isn't a big liability issue in New Zealand, as long as the project is non-commercial. That fitted his idea perfectly: a free, public facility.

### Finding the cliff

Then he went looking for a cliff, and found one on Tākaka Hill. He'd paraglided there every now and then: there's good lift in front of it, so you see that cliff a lot.

Anyone who's driven to Tākaka knows the hill: over from the Motueka and Nelson side, and you've arrived in Golden Bay. The cliff sits at the very high point of the road, and you climb to a pre-peak with 360-degree views.

## Paperwork, permits and a charitable society {#paperwork-permits}

For a long time it was a lonely project: Till in the office with his laptop and the telephone, doing two and a half years of purely bureaucratic work. He worked with the landowners and the QEII National Trust, which has a covenant protecting the land, plus the council, building permits and surveys, including biological and plant surveys. A big part of the job was constantly reminding people to please respond. It was painstakingly slow, but he persevered.

I used to be an urban planner, so I felt his pain.

Once Till knew it was going to happen, even with the paperwork unfinished, he invited his new friends round to set up a charitable society (a bit of a party), so there was an entity to own and run the via ferrata. Many of his helpers were cavers, who know very well how to access a vertical rock face.

They built two routes in two years, and the first, the easier Cliff Traverse, opened in December. Putting anchors into the cliff is physical, and it's the fun part. The long part was the bureaucracy.

## Planning a via ferrata route {#planning-the-route}

The Cliff Traverse goes along the cliff, so it was much easier to plan than the second route, which goes up a very high one. The process goes like this:

- **Look up.** Stand at the bottom and study the cliff.
- **Fly the drone.** Take pictures and a video fly-by, then look closely at possible lines on the computer.
- **Find solid rock.** The rock up there is crumbly in places, so you connect the good areas of solid rock into a route.
- **Build a bridge.** Where they couldn't cross something, they sometimes built a bridge over it.

Till loves those bridges.

When I climbed it last summer, I did not like the bridges. I wanted to close my eyes crossing them, which is not very helpful.

Till's friend Andrew, a very careful engineer, signs off the route. Till would rig ropes into the cliff and dummy a route: drill 3mm holes, put a marker in each and connect them with a line. Then he'd call Andrew, who would stop by and say either that it was great, or that it was horrible and they'd never drill there, but they could go over there. That's how the route evolved.

## Making a crumbly cliff safe {#crumbly-cliff}

Apart from the bureaucracy, the big challenge was geology. The cliff is unstable in places and full of loose rock, which is why it was never developed for rock climbing. That was lucky, because if climbers had claimed it, Till couldn't have run a wire through all their climbs.

So the team spent ages scaling the cliff: abseiling down from the top and kicking off everything lying loose. They threw down thousands of rocks, until the engineer said they'd scaled enough. Rocks too big to throw down would have destroyed the highway, so they glued wire into those rocks and then into the bedrock, fridge-sized boulders included. As Till puts it:

> "we tied most rocks to the mountain, so they're not going to come down."

Short of an earthquake while someone's on the cliff, it's super stable.

### The team behind it

A small core team did most of the work, with Andrew and Till's son putting in the most. Till says it always comes down to a few people able and willing to put in real hours. Most of the track network that connects everything was built by an 84-year-old volunteer, up there every nice day building steps and stairs. Till wants to be like him.

The biggest win was receiving the last document they needed. Till saved it to his Google Drive and called it a day.

## Climbing the via ferrata on Tākaka Hill {#climbing-takaka-hill}

What Till likes most is that a random visitor can progress from easy to a bit more challenging to challenging:

- **The skill zone.** A little training area near the car park, where you see how the equipment works and understand the concept.
- **The Cliff Traverse.** Walk another 10 minutes to the first real route, aimed at families and the everyday person, achievable in normal physical condition, with a bit of exposure. It has three emergency exits, so if you feel too challenged, you drop out, no problem.
- **The Cliff Conqueror.** Another 10 minutes on, the more challenging route goes pretty much straight up, with much more exposure.

On the Cliff Conqueror is what they call the sky bench, a park bench 65 metres above the base of the cliff and 85 metres above the state highway. You dangle your feet in the air, and Till says it's a great place for lunch.

On my climb, we stopped there for chocolate chip cookies. Very memorable.

Seeing so many people have a great day up there is rewarding for Till. Sometimes climbers ask him if he's ever tried the climb. His answer:

> "I know every anchor personally."

### My first via ferrata

When I did it last summer, we rented our gear from the shop, which is super affordable. I have a rock climbing background, so at the start I was holding on to the rocks and trying to climb, while my husband kept telling me I could hold the wire. In rock climbing you don't touch the bolts, so it took me a few minutes to trust it.

Till's take: you could even grab a tree if you like.

I'm usually somewhat okay with heights, but I always get butterflies. I loved the Cliff Traverse, and on the higher, more exposed new section I freaked out on every bridge. My favourite bits were the bridges, the bench and the ladder, which leans out away from the cliff over Tākaka and Golden Bay. It feels so not normal.

Till says a ladder is a great way around an overhang, where holding the wire would be very hard on your arms.

We were the first people on the wall that day, and by the time we finished, the car park was full of families and so many different types of people. I couldn't believe infrastructure like this was free.

## Opening day and the best time to climb {#best-time-to-climb}

When Till proposed the project to the QEII National Trust, they asked where people would park. He pointed to the walkway car park and guessed two cars a day. They opened the first route in December 2024, put it on Facebook and never spent a dollar on marketing. Then 150 climbers showed up. The car park was jammed and people parked along the state highway, which Till says NZTA wouldn't like to see.

They've had a crowding problem ever since. They doubled the car park with 20-tonne diggers and brought in a slot system for hire gear: when we recorded, eight sets every 30 minutes, to spread people over the day.

### Till's tip: go in the afternoon

> "Go in the afternoon. Everyone, I don't know for what reason, wants to climb in the morning."

The morning is colder and the sun isn't on the cliff. In the afternoon there are very few people, the sun is on the cliff face, and you can return hire gear into a box any time, even after dark.

When we recorded, the online reservation system only took bookings for morning gear pick-ups, from 9.30 to 11.30. From around midday, gear comes back and it's first come, first served. Till's advice is to get there before three.

I clearly needed that tip, because we were first on the cliff. I'm always a last-minute person and I hate booking things in advance, so now I know to go in the afternoon.

## Gear hire, ability and safety {#gear-and-safety}

You need a climbing harness, a helmet and lanyards: two carabiners connected to your harness, with a shock absorber in between. If you fell a few metres down a vertical section, being stopped suddenly could break a bone, so the shock absorber rips open to smooth the stop. It's single use. Over the 17,000 climbs they'd logged when we recorded, there were 12 deployments, and no one was injured.

### Hiring gear at Ngarua Caves

You can hire the helmet, harness and lanyards from the Ngarua Caves shop. The society partners with the business, which has run cave tours there for decades. It's a five-minute drive from the via ferrata car park, on the way in from the Nelson side. When we recorded, hire cost $10 for four hours, plenty for both routes: a $7 service fee for the business and $3 for the society to replace worn gear. It was free for anyone under 18.

### Who can climb it

It's designed as a family activity. You don't need to be a rock climber: reasonable fitness is enough for at least the first route. Exposure challenges some people more than others, and if it doesn't work out, you drop out and enjoy the views on a walk. The suggested direction is up. Climbing down isn't forbidden, but you'd meet a lot of climbers coming the other way, and up is much more fun.

### Weather and risk

- **Rain.** Don't climb when it's wet. Clipped in, you still can't fall off, but you're far more likely to slip. Hire gear isn't given out when it's obviously wet.
- **Thunderstorms.** A very bad idea: lightning would be highly attracted to a steel wire.
- **Everything else.** They can't eliminate every risk, and there are plenty of signs on site. A stone could still fall on you. It's your own decision.

Multiple engineers assessed the risk mathematically and rated it the same as swimming or riding a bike. Till's message: take responsibility for your own actions.

## New via ferratas and Till's other adventures {#whats-next}

The biggest help is a donation towards new routes. The Tākaka Hill via ferrata is built from stainless steel, so it won't rust and costs little to keep running. Local help with maintenance is welcome, especially on the track network, where steps fail and rain washes the track away.

Till meant this as a showcase that it can be done here, and the society's mission is to help anyone in New Zealand who wants to build one. They have a couple of cliffs scouted, and he won't say where until they have permission, which he's learned is the hardest bit. Their main costs are steel and glue. The construction is all volunteer work, and they've never paid anyone a dollar, for legal reasons and for the idea of the project. If you're good on ropes and can hold a drill, it's great fun.

### A via ferrata in the North Island?

So far, Till only knows of South Island locations. He encourages any North Islander, or tourist, who finds a great cliff in the North Island to let them know. The society will help, but a local needs to take the lead: Till spent half a year up on Tākaka Hill.

On our two-month [sea kayak expedition](https://abigailhannah.nz/182-finishing-a-two-month-slow-paddle-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/), I paddled down the [Northland](https://abigailhannah.nz/176-a-locals-guide-to-northland-and-building-hiking-communities-with-hannah-whittaker/) coastline, and there are a lot of cliffs up there.

A sea cliff is possible in stainless steel, Till says. They've looked into it, but haven't found one yet.

### Bush planes and caves

Till admits to way too many hobbies, and his two biggest right now are flying and caving. He owns a little two-seater bush plane, like a 4x4 plane with really big tyres, that can land on beaches, riverbeds and farm fertiliser airstrips. The day before we recorded, he flew a round of the Top of the South. There's still so much to find in New Zealand, so he combines the two, flying into remote places to find caves. He also goes tramping all the time, with a little fishing and diving. His advice is to have a bit of focus and get good at something, so you achieve more and do it safely.

As I said to Till at the end of our chat, making the outdoors accessible is something I'm really passionate about, and via ferrata is such a cool way for families and people new to the outdoors to try something new. Maybe it leads them into climbing, or caving, or who knows what.
