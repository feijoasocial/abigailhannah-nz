I have never surfed. I wanted to when I was a teenager in India, watching people on the beach and thinking it looked like so much fun. And here, the water is really cold. So I loved getting the inside story from two people who are in it all year round.

Cody and Callie Chase are into surfing, rock climbing, skydiving, snowboarding, wing foiling, anything that gets them outdoors and into nature, and they love doing it together. Cody grew up in Auckland. Callie grew up in Texas, and didn't discover extreme sports until she came to New Zealand.

We talk about how a lost surfboard brought them together (Cody's version is a lot more dramatic), why surfing keeps pulling them back, their tips for beginners, Cody's path from a joinery job to a skydiving diploma and a wingsuit licence, and the biggest adventure coming up next. Plus my own very spontaneous skydive.

## How Cody and Callie got into adventure sports {#getting-into-adventure}

Cody grew up in Auckland, loving the beach, being outdoors and climbing from a young age. He realised he could go bigger and better, and then higher, so he started [skydiving](https://abigailhannah.nz/154-working-as-a-sky-diving-instructor-in-wnaka-with-anne-bonfert/), and found he could do quite a lot if he wanted to.

The sports build on each other, too. Knowing how to read the wind and understand the waves for the surf reports carries across.

Callie grew up in Texas and focused on ball sports. It wasn't until she came to New Zealand, in her early 20s, that she discovered extreme sports. She'd started dabbling in [surfing](https://abigailhannah.nz/learn-to-surf-new-zealand/) before she met Cody, and he took her [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/) properly for the first time. She calls him a huge stepping stone, pushing her into it and showing her the way.

I love hearing the ages people start different pursuits on this podcast. It's never too late.

## How a lost surfboard brought them together {#how-they-met}

Callie's version is short and sweet. She was surfing at Tāwharanui, up north, which she reckons is the most beautiful beach in New Zealand, on a really big swell from an April cyclone. She was still a beginner on her little foamie, so she stayed on the inside, caught a couple of waves and went to sit on the shore while her friends finished. She sat down away from the crowds, next to a lone surfboard leaning against the rope fence. About 15 minutes later, a surfer came up out of the water. She asked how his surf was, and he said he'd nearly drowned.

That was Cody. It was a big day and he was a bit over his head. Out the back, a rogue set came through, bigger than the rest and breaking further out, and he tried to paddle out towards it. He didn't make it. The wave dumped straight on top of him, his leg rope snapped and his board pinged off while he was still under the water. He surfaced, another wave hit him, and when he finally came up again and tried to swim in, a strong current kept dragging him out to sea. Another surfer gave him a tow in on his board.

Walking up the beach, he saw his surfboard against a fence post, with a girl sitting right next to it.

They got chatting, and she invited him to come along with her friends for another surf that evening. He had the best pickup line, too. His truck, an old '92 Toyota Land Cruiser Prado, had a busted starter motor, so he asked Callie to help him start it: she turned the ignition while he whacked it under the bonnet. She laughed, jumped in, and they hung out every weekend from there.

Cody definitely downplayed it at the time. As a beginner, Callie didn't realise how gnarly it was until later. Since then she's taken a wave on the head and snapped two boards in the space of two weeks, so she gets it now.

## First surfs at Muriwai and Raglan {#first-surfs}

Cody's first surf was at Muriwai, off Rimmers Road, with big, gnarly West Coast waves. He started older than most people do. A friend handed him a surfboard and told him to have fun, and he got slammed by wave after wave and had a terrible time. He's glad he persisted.

Callie's first surf was in Raglan, on her first trip to New Zealand. All she knew was that it was an amazing surf beach, and back then it was much quieter than it is now. She rented a massive surfboard and a wetsuit and went out by herself for two hours.

I'd like to learn somewhere warmer first, then come back to New Zealand with a bit more confidence. I was out at Piha the weekend before we recorded, and it blows my mind that there are people in the water all year round.

## Why surfing keeps them coming back {#why-surfing}

For Cody, surfing is a really easy flow trigger. You click into flow, become one with the ocean, read it and ride it. It's addictive. The ocean is always different, clean green waves one day and stormy, lumpy, windy chop the next, and you have to learn to read each wave and where it's breaking. It feels like conquering the elements. When we recorded, it was coming into winter and he was still in his 3/2 wetsuit, with a few holes in it.

Callie has had to build up her cold tolerance over the years. When she started, she wanted the thickest wetsuit going.

For Callie, surfing is a mission with herself as much as with the waves. As a beginner she wanted to be really good, and she'd get down on herself when she fell off or missed a wave, with that internal dialogue telling her she wasn't getting any better. She's always been a beach lover, and when she's surfing, time slows down and all her worries go away. Over the last couple of years she's learned to be kinder to herself and to surf for the enjoyment of it, since everyone's on their own journey. Asked for one word to describe surfing:

> "it's just it's fun like I want if I was going to explain to people what surfing is I want them to know. It's fun."

I get that inner dialogue with rock climbing at the gym, where people can see you and you can see them, and there's always a little bit of needing to be better. Then I remember I'm there to have fun and to move.

## Surfing tips for beginners and beyond {#surf-tips}

Their tips for beginners:

* **East is easier.** West is best, but east is easier. They both started on the West Coast, and it's gnarly and more difficult. The East Coast usually has kinder waves.
* **Choose the right board.** Research lengths and shapes. Don't jump on a small board thinking you can suddenly shred, because you'll get pounded. Start on a longer board so you catch more waves, and more waves is better.
* **Surf with people who are better than you**, or take lessons. Their surfing really progressed on a two-week trip to Hawaii, surfing with someone who'd surfed his whole life and taught them how to read the waves and where to sit.
* **Be prepared to get wiped out.** Some sessions will suck. There's always another one around the corner, and every session gets you closer to being better.

### Breathwork and staying calm

Taking it further, part of their work has been surf development, training like a gym trainer for surf athletes. Gym work helps your pop-up and your paddling endurance. Even more important is breathwork and mental training. The more you build your CO2 tolerance, the longer you can hold your breath and the calmer you stay when you're getting pounded.

Panicking uses up more of your oxygen, so it helps to have a happy place to sink into in your mind. Even big waves in places like Hawaii only hold you under for 10 or 15 seconds. It feels like forever, but 10 seconds isn't that long, and your wetsuit and board will bring you back up (unless your leg rope snaps, speaking from experience). The goal is to be comfortable in uncomfortable situations.

And you're in control of when you head in. If you're flustered, there's no shame in going in.

## Skydiving and wingsuits with Cody {#skydiving}

Cody did a skydive, fell in love with it, and told his instructor he'd be back. Sure enough, he went back to his local drop zone at Parakai, on the west coast of Auckland near Muriwai, and took his time getting his licence through advanced freefall training.

He was working a joinery job at the time, 7.30 to 5, and got sick of it. So he quit and did the six-month diploma in skydiving at Parakai. He got 200 skydives in, made some epic friends and did some epic jumps, including onto beaches. He's jumped all around New Zealand, from the top of the Bay of Islands down to the [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/) and the South Island, and now he has his wingsuit licence.

Wingsuit flying is the ultimate flow trigger for him: peaceful and relaxing, and high intensity. As Cody puts it:

> "You have to be very very present the whole time and aware of your whole body and what you're doing."

Situational awareness is huge, with all your emergency procedures running through your head.

For most people, a skydive is a bucket list thing. Doing it all the time, the excitement changes. On your first couple of jumps, so much is happening that your brain can't process it. A skydive is only about 45 seconds long, and the more you do it, the more you learn to slow it down.

### Where to skydive in New Zealand

* **Bay of Islands.** Very tropical, with all the islands below you.
* **Parakai.** Muriwai Beach is right there, and you can see Ruapehu and Mount Taranaki, all the way down the North Island.
* **Taupō.** Cody's first wingsuit jump was there, and he has a lot of fun memories. It's his pick. Callie has done tandems around the country, and Taupō is her special one too: the mountains, the lake and, on a clear day, the view.

My one skydive was completely spontaneous. [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/) and I were at Lake Pukaki on a clear, sunny day, and I knew there was a skydive place just off the road in Twizel. If I'd planned it, I would one hundred percent have chickened out. We could see five different lakes, [Aoraki](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) and the whole Southern Alps. When my feet touched the ground my legs were jelly. Isaac said he could do it again. Me, not that day.

Jumping down there with his own rig is high on Cody's bucket list.

## Coaching, woodwork and the next big adventure {#whats-next}

Their adventures have turned into work. When we recorded, they'd both recently started trampoline and freestyle [snowboard](https://abigailhannah.nz/014-snowsports-nz-olympian-anja-barugh/) coaching, teaching kids body awareness, spins and flips on the trampoline so they can take the technique to the snow. Cody also has his own woodworking and wood turning business.

Their next biggest adventure, when we recorded, was having a baby, about a month away. After that, they planned to head to the States, [live in a bus](https://abigailhannah.nz/058-bus-nz-wayna/) and travel around surfing, rock climbing and skydiving. They've already done a big [van trip](https://abigailhannah.nz/008-nz-vanlife-gooseandellen/) around New Zealand with their dog, so they'll bring the baby along the same way. Cody doesn't think it'll change much: he'll have a little adventure buddy, and he's excited to teach her to read the water, the wind and the weather, and to do risky stuff safely. Calculated risk is a really healthy thing.

I've had a few parents on the podcast who keep doing what they love with their kids, and I love it. What you do as a kid can define what you do as an adult.

Their parting advice: if you have a dream, go for it, and don't let fear hold you back from trying something new. Callie never imagined, on her first surf, where she'd end up, or that surfing would help her meet her husband. Her favourite quote:

> "The master has failed more than the beginner has even tried."
