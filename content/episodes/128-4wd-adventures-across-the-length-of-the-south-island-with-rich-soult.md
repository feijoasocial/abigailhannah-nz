I've been on quite a few four-wheel drive trips with friends, and I'm always the passenger princess (which I love). So I was really really looking forward to this chat with Rich Soult, a four-wheel drive guide who runs overlanding trips the length of the South Island.

Rich was born in England and grew up in Christchurch. Then he spent years in the UK and France, before moving back to New Zealand in 2014. Which means nobody can place his accent. And it was one photo of a man in a bathtub up the Macaulay that started the whole thing.

So we go from that bath to how he ended up running 4x4 Explorer Adventures, then stop by stop through his two-week trip from Picton to Macetown. Plus how to read a river before you drive into it, the first upgrades that make the biggest difference to a truck, and what he'd want you to know before you come along. I also asked a soooo selfish question about our own van, so stick around for that.

## From Christchurch to France and back {#rich-story}

Rich was born in England in the late 60s, and his family moved to [Christchurch](https://abigailhannah.nz/141-best-christchurch-and-canterbury-adventures-with-kids-with-jasmine-trafford/) when he was a baby. They moved back to the UK in 1981, and he lived there, then in France, before coming home to New Zealand in 2014.

Nobody can place his accent. In New Zealand, everyone thinks he's a Pom. In the UK, they reckon he sounds like Crocodile Dundee. In France, people ask whereabouts in France he's from.

You'd get on really well with [my husband](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/), Rich. He's half American, half British and grew up in Nepal, and nobody can place his accent either.

Rich remembers family hikes as a kid, and fishing trips to the Harper outlet of Lake Coleridge in a little wooden dinghy (they were absolutely useless at catching anything). These days the Harper is one of his go-to midweek overnight trips, and the drive that felt like forever when he was six or seven is now down the road. He learnt to ski at Mount Hutt, Mount Cheeseman and Porters, and in the 70s everyone could fix their own car. It gave him a love of the outdoors from a pretty young age.

### His first four-wheel drive trip

Just before the family left New Zealand, a primary school mate's family took him along with their Land Rover club, over to the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/). He remembers it as complete wilderness madness, with a great bunch of people.

In the UK, he dreamed of owning a Range Rover. At university he joined the British Army, and spent 10 years being driven around the Salisbury Plain and Sennybridge training areas in Land Rovers (as an officer, he wasn't allowed to drive). He counts that as part of the job, so it doesn't feel like overlanding to him.

He finally bought a 2005 Range Rover in France, where he was living in Burgundy and skiing and hunting a lot. Then a university mate invited Rich and his kids on a Trans Pyrenees trip. The Range Rover was black, with blacked-out windows and low profile tyres (his mates called it the drive-by). The kids loved it.

Years later, his daughter piped up from the back seat to say he'd been right all those times he called it the best vehicle he'd ever had: since the Pyrenees, it had been their holiday home.

## A bath up the Macaulay Valley {#macaulay-bath}

The New Zealand chapter started at the top of Mount John, at Lake Tekapo. Rich hadn't been back for decades when he left freezing cold France one January and spent two weeks on a Kiwi Experience bus trip. At Tekapo, the driver walked up Mount John, and Rich went too. From the top he could see what he now knows as Lilybank Road, a gravel road heading up along Lake Tekapo, and he wondered how far it went up into the mountains.

A couple of months later, back in his dining room in Burgundy, he followed Lilybank Road on Google Maps all the way up the Macaulay. There he found a photo called "bath time": a man in an iron bath outside the hut, with snow-capped mountains behind him, holding up a tin of Speight's.

That was it. Back in New Zealand, Rich bought his parents' Prado, headed straight to Lake Tekapo, stopped for a slab of Speight's, drove up, lit the fire and had a bath in the mountains. That's where it all started.

New Zealand has an unusual number of bathtubs in really remote places. I've been up the Macaulay and the [Godley](https://abigailhannah.nz/overlanding-nz/), and I love that Rich can pinpoint the whole thing to one image of someone in a bath.

Sadly, the Macaulay bath has been taken away, because it was deemed a fire risk. When Rich was at Stag Hut up the Godley a few months before we recorded, there was a bath there.

## Starting a 4WD guiding business {#starting-guiding}

After that, Rich did more and more four-wheel driving and got involved with a route planning website. He and his partner were living in Auckland, not enjoying their jobs, so they sold up to move to the South Island. First they spent months in Europe, with another four-wheel drive, travelling the French and Italian Alps, the Pyrenees, Corsica and Sardinia.

Back in Christchurch, on a clagged-in trip to Potts Hut, Rich met two vehicles at a junction heading the same way, so they convoyed down together. One of them was a four-wheel drive guide who'd written a guidebook Rich owned, and Rich was a bit awestruck.

Rich was building a name of his own too. He writes for NZ4WD magazine, and people kept recognising his articles: at an air show in Blenheim, they parked next to a Land Cruiser with the same rooftop shower he'd written about building from plumbing kit, and the owner had read it.

So Rich met the guide for a coffee and suggested they put their names together. The guide told him he could just have the business if he wanted. That's how Rich started 4x4 Explorer Adventures, and he hasn't looked back, even with a rough start in the COVID madness.

He's keen to point out none of this was a grand plan:

> "it sounds like it was strategically planned, but it wasn't. It was just like another opportunity came up"

## Why the South Island is world class for overlanding {#world-class}

Rich's trips are South Island only. North Island tracks are very limited, with too few for a proper overlanding tour, and the South Island, he says, is world class.

When people in France told him France was beautiful because it has beaches, mountains and rivers, his answer was: well, so have we. Start at the [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/) and you've got some of the best beaches in the world. Then there's the Main Divide and the Southern Alps, great braided rivers, Central Otago (completely different again) and [Fiordland](https://abigailhannah.nz/186-packrafting-and-tramping-48-days-through-fiordland-with-penzy-dinsdale/), where the scenery is awesome.

Even on a road trip, you can see that diversity. It's all right there, so compact and so diverse, and I love that about the South Island so much.

## Two weeks of 4WD tracks from Picton to Macetown {#two-week-trip}

The two-week trip had sat on Rich's website for a couple of years, with feedback that it was too long. Then some clients who'd become great friends opened a diary during a rugby game and found dates that worked. He ran it for them, and 12 vehicles signed up. It's run every year since, and when we recorded, he was planning a south to north version for 2026.

### Picton to Tekapo

Most of his guests come from the North Island, so the trip starts in Picton, a convenient place to meet. The first afternoon is a chill one at Mill Flat, a campsite on the northern bank of the Wairau River, with a barbecue to get to know each other.

Then it's up the Leatham Valley, down the Molesworth into Edwards Valley, on to Hanmer Springs and down to Lake Tekapo and the Macaulay.

### The Hopkins to the Old Man Range

From there it's down to Ōhau and up the Hopkins, then over the saddle to Homestead Campsite for a couple of nights. Then down to Alexandra and up the [Old Man Range](https://abigailhannah.nz/106-overlanding-in-the-old-man-range-and-4wd-trips-with-jono-hitchcox/), across to the Old Woman Range. Between the two is one of Rich's really cool campsites, near a stream with a great swimming hole.

### The Nevis to Macetown

Next comes the Nevis, then Mavora Lakes and a stop in Te Anau, where you need to buy bug spray (or a shotgun, says Rich). Then down Borland Road to Lake Manapōuri, and the last night together in Macetown.

So many of these valleys and routes are icons in New Zealand. Linking them all up sounds like the most epic adventure.

### His favourite spots

Rich has a lot of absolute favourite campsites and tracks, many tied to memories. Three that came up:

* **Monument Hut, up the Hopkins.** On a clear night with a full moon, you've got the moon on the river, all the stars and a campfire.
* **Mill Flat, on the Wairau.** A real classic, always immaculately maintained, in the middle of the beech forest, with amazing birdsong and a great river to swim in.
* **Macaulay Hut**, or the Macaulay Hilton, where he's had some epic, random nights meeting people.

### The end of the trip

After two weeks together, people become really good friends, so the end is bittersweet: no more chatter on the radio. Once everyone drives away, his shoulders drop and he'll often have an afternoon nap, because at the end of the day, he's responsible for people's lives.

One North Island guest told him he'd driven around the South Island loads of times, looked up at the mountains and wondered what was up there, and Rich had shown them so much. Rich puts it simply:

> "for me, that's worth more than the money. … at the end of the day, I'm a people person and I enjoy sharing what we've got."

## River crossings and reading the terrain {#river-crossings}

Rich gives plenty of tips on his trips, because a lot of guests have just bought a Ford Ranger and want to see what it can do.

On the two-week trip, the terrain builds. Up the Leatham there are a couple of river crossings, normally pretty low. Edwards is rough, but nothing really challenging. Then the Main Divide river section brings more river crossings.

### How Rich runs a crossing

* **He explains his choices.** Why he's crossing at this point, and why not somewhere else.
* **One truck in the river at a time.** There's only one thing worse than a stuck truck, and that's two stuck trucks.
* **He plans the convoy.** Rich leads, and a less capable vehicle or driver gets someone more capable in front and behind.
* **No snorkel needed.** People ask if they need one. He has one, but:

> "if a river is so high that you need a snorkel, I'm not guiding people through it."

### Reading the river

All of this comes with experience, and Rich passes on as much as he can:

* **Check the colour.** Crystal clear is great. Milky or blue means snowmelt. Brown means it's flooding.
* **Know the catchment.** Even if it's not raining where you are, it could be pouring way up in the mountains.
* **Use a telltale stone.** If you stop, put a stone at the water level and watch whether the river is coming up or going down.
* **Go early when there's snowmelt.** Get up early and be out before the sun gets on the snow.
* **Watch for standing waves.** They mean a boulder, and on the downstream side, the water going over the top creates a hole.

## Who the 4WD trips are for {#who-its-for}

You either bring your own vehicle or hire one, and when we recorded, his website listed rental partners for that. A lot of his clients are long on money and short on time, so they fly from Auckland to Christchurch, pick up a truck and go.

You don't need any experience to come, because it's an overlanding trip, with safety in numbers. He always has a plan B, C, D and E. On the Hopkins, for example, you can get to Monument Hut without crossing the river.

His best example came on the Old Man Range in January. They got up to the last gate below the obelisk to find six inches of snow, a blizzard, and no sign of the obelisk. So he took everyone back to the bottom for lunch, and they went to the Serpentine Church in the afternoon instead, lower down, with no snow at all.

Local knowledge and watching the weather are what guiding is about, and Rich reckons that's why a lot of people come away with him: they know they're in a safe pair of hands.

For people who used to be keen campers and trampers but aren't anymore, he also runs a wilderness comfort trip: out into the wilderness during the day, back to civilisation at night. When we recorded, he was also running a Southern Wilderness Camping Trip from Sheffield down to Macetown.

## Tyres, lift and recovery gear for your 4WD {#setting-up}

Then came my very personally motivated question. [Our van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/) is a four-wheel drive Hiace with locking hubs, without a lot of clearance. We've only really taken it on the beach, and I have to use four-wheel drive to get off the grass sometimes.

Rich's answer starts with the same thing he tells anyone building a vehicle for overlanding: it's about the tyres first.

### Tyres

* **All-terrain tyres.** In Europe, his Range Rover ran Pirelli P Zero low profile road tyres, and he got a puncture on every trip.
* **LT rated.** It stands for light truck, and it means the sidewalls are armoured. A sidewall tear means the tyre goes in the bin.

After tyres, it's mostly about ground clearance.

### Rich's own truck

That purple Prado he bought from his parents was a funny one. They don't have Prados in France, so he didn't know what it was, and he told his dad he wanted a proper four-wheel drive. His dad said it was one, and he was right: it's a really capable vehicle.

Then, coming out of Macetown, the chassis cracked, and it was a write-off. All the bits he'd added were worth way more than the truck, so he bought another Prado, a diesel, painted battleship grey, and left both with a four-wheel drive shop to turn one into the other while he was in Europe.

So these days he drives a 1996 [Land Cruiser Prado](https://abigailhannah.nz/006-4wd-land-cruiser-bri-woolnough/). It has no diff locks, but it does have bash plates, rock sliders, a winch, a bar and a snorkel, because he's virtually living out of it six months of the year.

### The first upgrades to make

For off-road capability, Rich's order:

1. **Tyres**, as above.
2. **Suspension.** A 2-inch or 50mm lift gives you more ground clearance.
3. **Recovery gear.** A snatch strap and some shackles.
4. **Recovery points.** At least one rated recovery point at the front and one at the back.

And never, ever use a tow ball as a recovery point. Decent tyres, a bit of a lift and some recovery gear, and you're good to go. You can start small and build up over time.
