Kurt is a listener. He reached out to me to be on the show, and I'm so glad he did, because we haven't talked about much North Island tramping on the podcast. And I really really wanted to pick his brain.

Kurt grew up in a military home, moving all over the world, and he's now lived in Hastings for 25 years, five times longer than anywhere else. He started tramping properly about five years ago, mostly in the North Island, and he's since walked the whole of [Te Araroa](https://abigailhannah.nz/115-quitting-his-corporate-job-and-preparing-for-five-months-of-thru-hiking-te-araroa-with-anurag-sharma/).

So this one covers the huts and tracks of Whirinaki and the Kawekas, his solo loop in Whirinaki where pretty much everything went wrong, a wild day over Mount Crawford, his advice for new trampers, and (very selfishly) his tips for Round the Mountain, which I was about to walk.

## How Kurt got into tramping {#kurt-story}

Kurt came to New Zealand in 1999 to help start a church in Hastings, and these days he works as a counsellor in Hawke's Bay. Apart from a three-day hike as a teenager, tramping came late. In 2018 he walked the Camino. Then in 2019, on a visit to the States, he kept crossing the Appalachian Trail and started listening to podcasts about it. Back home, he started finding out about everything New Zealand has.

His first Great Walk was around [Lake Waikaremoana](https://abigailhannah.nz/175-starting-tramping-in-her-40s-and-lake-waikaremoana-as-a-family-with-lois-baldwin/) in 2019. He went completely unprepared, and it was a disaster. He said never again. About two weeks later, he was thinking that with better gear and a bit more preparation, he'd give it another go.

## Huts and tracks in Whirinaki and the Kawekas {#whirinaki-kaweka-huts}

The Kawekas are Kurt's backyard: head west from Hastings and Napier and you run right into them. [Whirinaki](https://abigailhannah.nz/145-solo-tramping-the-mangamate-loop-in-whirinaki-forest-park-with-sharon-evans/) is further north, on the east side between Taupō and Rotorua.

### Whirinaki Forest Park

Most people start at River Road car park, or you can get a shuttle from Rotorua. The most common hut is Central Whirinaki Hut, a big, well-stocked hut with plenty of tenting space. There's also a longer five-day loop.

Further in, the huts get more basic, like hunters' huts. Kurt loves Rogers Hut, which has a stained glass window and axe marks inside where the logs were hewn by hand. It sits right off a bike track (a rough one he wouldn't want to ride).

### Kaweka Forest Park

The Kawekas have an amazing hut network. Kurt's favourite, a beautiful hut, has three names, one of them Venison Tops, though he likes calling it the Kelvinator. Every hut he's stayed in, in both parks, has been well maintained and well supplied.

The catch is that everything in the Kawekas goes straight up or straight down. At Kaweka J, the highest point, Kurt's tramping club has a cairn for its World War I and World War II veterans. After the cyclone, volunteers were in quickly, clearing and rerouting washed-out tracks.

When I first turned on the DOC huts layer in Topo Maps, I couldn't believe how many huts there were in the [Kawekas](https://abigailhannah.nz/kaweka-ranges/). We still hadn't been when we recorded.

### How North Island tramping compares

Ranges like the Ruahines and the Tararuas aren't as high as the South Island ones Kurt walked on Te Araroa, so it's a long way through the trees before you get above the bushline. Nobody believes in switchbacks, and best of all, there are far fewer sandflies.

## Solo on an 80 km loop in Whirinaki {#whirinaki-solo-loop}

Kurt's most memorable trip was his first big solo hike, an 80-kilometre loop in Whirinaki. It came from a three-day club trip there the year before: he loved the big podocarps, the whio, the caves and the river crossings. Nobody else had the free time for the long loop, so he went alone, with his PLB.

### Where it went wrong

The first day and a half went fine. Then, at lunchtime on day two, he reached the hut he'd planned to stop at and decided it was too early. A terrible mistake, he says.

Up to there, the track had been wide, clear and flat. The next stretch was all uphill, on a section that hadn't been maintained in a while. Ferns covered the track and the orange markers were hit or miss. Realising he'd be late, he rushed, slipped off the track and slid down towards a creek. He lost his Garmin watch, his main navigation, and carried on with his phone. (He went back two months later and found it.)

He reached the hut wiped out. The next day was all downhill, leaving him with rubber legs, and on the walk out he twisted his knee. The last day was a long one on a knee that wouldn't bend.

About two hours after hurting his knee, he reached Rogers Hut. He had it to himself, in a beautiful setting, and sat reading about its history. For a while, all the worry left his head.

It was September, early in the season, and apart from three trail runners one night, he had the huts to himself. He enjoys the quiet: as a counsellor, he's talking to people all the time.

### What it taught him

Kurt could have set off his PLB, but he figured that if he could still move, even slowly, he'd get out. It showed him how resilient you can be with no other option. His biggest lesson is don't rush, and look hard at the topography before you push on.

> "I needed to remind myself why I was out there. It was not to complete the trail, it was to enjoy the trail."

One of his favourite memories is a club trip in the Kawekas with a day in hand, spent at the hut restocking the firewood, swapping stories and napping.

## Te Araroa and a wild day over Mount Crawford {#mount-crawford-day}

That Whirinaki loop was meant as training for Te Araroa. Afterwards, Kurt cancelled his walk for that year and did a postgraduate certificate in counselling. The trail kept bugging him, so he walked it the year before we recorded. His favourite moment was Stag Saddle, for how beautiful it was. One of his scariest was in the Tararuas.

After Christmas at home, Kurt and the friend he was walking with went back on at Levin, with about four days left in the Tararuas: mud, roots and goblin forest, up and down even on the tops. On the third day the rain came in.

In the hut below Mount Crawford was a group of three who'd tried to set off and turned back. Kurt was ready to get out of the Tararuas, so he told them he'd been out in winds up to 90 kilometres an hour with his tramping club, and if it wasn't blowing that hard, they'd give it a go. They heard that he was an expert. Leading them along the top in rain and low visibility, he was thinking that if they only knew how little he knew.

They had a ten-second celebration on Mount Crawford, then headed down a spur into the trees. At Waitewaewae Hut, a big, nice hut, the five of them got a fire going and laughed about their miserable day.

> "Certainly one of the scariest and hardest days on the TA, but man, it was … such an experience that I never want to forget that one."

My first ever tramp in New Zealand was a three-day loop on Pirongia, in the Waikato. My friends said it was three or four hours of walking a day. It took us nine to ten. It was so muddy, and I haven't been back.

Kurt says the Tararuas are Pirongia day after day after day. So it wasn't only me.

## Advice for new trampers {#new-tramper-advice}

Kurt's first tip: don't go tramping with my friends. Fair. The rest:

* **Start with a** [**Great Walk**](https://abigailhannah.nz/10-great-walks-in-10-days/). They're usually well maintained, with good huts.
* **Join a local tramping club.** The wealth of knowledge in them is amazing, and it's what he did after Waikaremoana.
* **Watch YouTube videos of a track** before you go, so you know if you've bitten off more than you can chew.
* **Go with someone experienced**, and look for river safety courses.
* **Take a** [**PLB**](https://abigailhannah.nz/plb-safety/) **or satellite messenger.** New Zealand has no dangerous animals, but the land and the weather are another matter.

> "It's a beautiful country, but it's a rugged country as well."

University tramping clubs are another option, and anyone can join. A couple of years ago I tagged along on my husband's club trip around [Tongariro](https://abigailhannah.nz/138-an-obsession-with-the-mountains-of-tongariro-national-park-with-hazel-phillips/). When we recorded, we didn't own a PLB, so we'd rent one from Bivouac.

## Round the Mountain on Ruapehu and what's next {#round-the-mountain}

Round the Mountain on [Ruapehu](https://abigailhannah.nz/032-ruapehu-solo-hazel-phillips/) is Kurt's favourite hike in New Zealand, and I was about to start it from Whakapapa Village the week after we recorded. So I asked for everything.

* **Go anticlockwise.** Early on, you walk up between two waterfalls coming down side by side, and Kurt has never seen anything like it.
* **Don't keep left at the waterfall.** You'll get cliffed out and have to backtrack. Go up the middle, with lots of boulder hopping.
* **Enjoy the desert** on the east side. Some people find it weird. Kurt thought it was beautiful.
* **Take your time.** He did it in four days in January, which pushed it. My five days is a sane amount, he says.
* **Use poles.** He barely used his on the Camino and his knees were shot. On Te Araroa, three times longer, he used them every day and his knees were fine.

I take my trusty hiking poles everywhere.

Next for Kurt: the north-south track in the Kaimai Range, another go at a long Kaweka trip he pulled out of after getting badly dehydrated, Around the Mountain on [Taranaki](https://abigailhannah.nz/156-taranaki-three-peaks-traverse-and-supporting-families-in-the-outdoors-with-jenn-julian/), where a big slip had closed part of the track when we recorded, and the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/) in the South Island.

The Old Ghost Road was my first solo hike, and I absolutely loved it.
