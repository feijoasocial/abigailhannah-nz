It took me 99 episodes to convince my husband, Isaac, to come on the podcast. Ninety-nine! And I obviously know everything about him already (we are married, after all), so this one was all about the things other people always want to know.

Isaac grew up in Kathmandu, trekking in the mountains of Nepal, and came to New Zealand at the start of 2017. Since then he's done a degree in outdoor education and thrown himself into whitewater kayaking, rock climbing, sea kayaking and diving. Every time I finally get the hang of one, there's something else he wants to try.

So we cover trekking in Nepal and how it compares to tramping here, how he found his community through uni clubs, and a lot of whitewater kayaking: Murchison, river grades explained, his worst swim and a New Year's overnight on the river. Plus a whale shark, our very first (very smoky) van, and his advice for getting outside without spending a fortune.

## Growing up trekking in Nepal {#isaac-story}

Isaac is half English, half American, and he spent the first 14 years of his life in Kathmandu. At 14 he went to boarding school in South India for high school, which is where we met.

Nepal has two main trekking seasons, spring and autumn, which lined up with the midterm school holidays, so most families would head off into the hills and the mountains. Most years, Isaac's family did at least one or two decent treks of a week or two. He walked to Everest Base Camp when he was five, and he's also tramped in North India, which he describes as crazy beautiful and a whole different scene. Growing up around the mountains is where his passion for the outdoors comes from.

## Moving to New Zealand and studying outdoor education {#moving-to-nz}

As a younger kid, Isaac did heaps outdoors: mountain biking, rock climbing, swimming. At boarding school in India, adventures were more limited, so he lost his passion a little and found other things to enjoy.

In high school, I never pinpointed him as the super outdoorsy one, and somehow he's roped me into all of it since.

After a six-month gap year in America, Isaac flew to New Zealand at the beginning of 2017 and rediscovered that passion. He reckons New Zealand is the best place for it, with mountains, beaches and bush all within a stone's throw in such a compact country.

At first I was about the only person he knew here, so to find some community he joined the Auckland University Rock Climbing Club, the Canoe Club and, for a while, the [Tramping Club](https://abigailhannah.nz/180-hiking-the-cascade-saddle-route-tramping-clubs-and-influencers-with-alice-milne/). He wasn't studying at the time, and he could still join and meet people his age with the same interests.

He'd thought he'd be an engineer, until he spotted a kayak hanging above a stall at a uni open day and his eyes lit up. He'd never thought of the outdoors as something you could study. A couple of years later he started a degree in outdoor education, which he calls probably the least academic-heavy degree you could do: very hands-on, with multi-day sea kayak trips, whitewater trips and tramps. There's also a lot of research and theory about why we do these things, and after every journey you write a reflection, so he's got very good at reflecting on his adventures.

## Learning to whitewater kayak {#learning-whitewater}

Isaac's first time in a whitewater kayak was in Pokhara, a big lake town he calls the adventure capital of Nepal. When he was little, they'd rent whitewater kayaks and he'd be towed behind on the flat lake with no paddle. In a landlocked country, rafting and whitewater kayaking are the main water sports, and he'd always loved the videos of big river expeditions.

In Auckland, he was working at a climbing gym when a coworker in the Auckland Uni Kayak Club kept telling him to come along. In the end a university classmate brought him, and on the club's big O-Week trip at the start of the year he paddled his first rapids. The rest is history.

These days, in the summer months, he's away kayaking pretty much every weekend.

## Whitewater kayaking in Murchison {#murchison-kayaking}

The reason I walked the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/) is that we were down in Murchison, and Isaac was on a kayak trip with a bunch of mates from the club.

Murchison is the kayaking hub of New Zealand. There are so many good rivers right around the town, for all different skill levels, and the New Zealand Kayak School there is world renowned. Whatever the rain is doing, you can find something to hop on. Over summer it's a kayaker's mecca, with hundreds and hundreds of kayakers camped by the river for weeks.

### North Island and South Island rivers

The main difference Isaac found was size. Most North Island rivers aren't super wide or voluminous, with exceptions like the Mohaka, which can get big, and the Waikato. One of the rivers they did around Murchison had far more water than anything he'd paddled before. Near the front of the group, he looked back at a long line of paddlers floating down behind him, half of them strangers to each other, all out on the water having fun.

### Community on the river

Kayaking is individual in the sense that you're alone in your boat, making your own decisions. It's also inherently communal, because most people need a group for safety. And it's fun: driving shuttles, waiting at the takeout, having a yarn on the flat water. Being responsible for each other forges friendships quickly.

When we recorded, Isaac had spent the past year on the team running the kayak club, and was organising a trip for beginners. There's always carnage with beginners, he says, and carnage in a safe environment is normally pretty fun.

### His worst swim

Isaac's worst swim was around Murchison, on a rapid he'd never seen. A couple of people got out to scout the line. He couldn't be bothered, and followed someone who'd only run it for the first time the day before. Error number two. It was a grade three that flushed out pretty well, and he flipped right at the end, tried to roll, then swam, and spent quite a long time underwater.

## Whitewater river grades explained {#river-grades}

A roll is what you do when you flip over: you roll back up and keep paddling. A swim is when you pull off your spray skirt and get out of the boat, at the mercy of the water and hoping your friends are close by to rescue you, your boat and your paddle. How bad that is depends on the grade. Isaac's rundown:

* **Grade one** is flat water. It can be moving, like the lower Waikato, with no whitewater.
* **Grade two** brings some whitewater. It's very safe, and most of the club's beginner trips are on grade two rivers.
* **Grade three** needs more technique and brings more danger. A swim might give you a bit of a rough time.
* **Grade four** is more technical, with waterfalls and rapids where you need to hit the right line.
* **Grade five** is an escalation of that, and you really don't want to swim in it.
* **Grade six** is unrunnable. The chances of survival are so low that people don't run it.

The grades are based loosely on how severe an accident would be, and people can die on any grade. The highest Isaac has run is the Kaituna River in Rotorua, which he calls a soft grade four.

I love the Kaituna too.

## A New Year's overnight on the Upper Maruia {#upper-maruia-overnight}

For Isaac, the highlights are the massive gorges and places you can only reach by whitewater kayak or raft, and the community. Even on a bad day, everyone else makes it a good one.

The trip that stands out was over New Year's in Murchison, while I was on the Old Ghost Road. Most of the group had never done an overnight whitewater trip, so they paddled the Upper Maruia River over two half days with a riverside camp in between. With all the gear jangling around in the back, the boats were far heavier than usual. They set up camp, got a big fire going, and Isaac remembers:

> "spending new years under the stars in the middle of the bush, so far away from anywhere else."

It was chill whitewater and a really good crew, and he says it embodied everything he loves about kayaking and the outdoors.

I was suffering on the Old Ghost Road, obviously. From Ghost Lake Hut, on a super clear day, I could see straight into Murchison and all the rivers and valleys coming together there. If you've never been, it's a small town about halfway between St Arnaud in the [Nelson Lakes](https://abigailhannah.nz/016-nelson-lakes-mel-white/) and the West Coast, with a Four Square, a bakery and real fruit ice cream in summer.

## Tramping, climbing, diving and sea kayaking {#multi-passionate}

### Tramping

It took Isaac a while to adjust to New Zealand tramping. In Nepal, a porter carries your gear and you sleep in a tea house on a mattress with a duvet, with hot showers and a menu. A Kiwi would call it quite boujee, he says. Tramping here is more slogging up hills, and he loves that too.

### Rock climbing

Isaac climbed as a kid, and a Christmas present of climbing harnesses got us both into it here. Soon after, his job at the climbing gym meant he could climb for free and be part of that community. When he had a couple of years off climbing, that's part of what pushed him towards whitewater.

One Christmas we took our climbing gear to Laos, with routes bolted every metre. Some call it overkill, and it's great for learning. Isaac rates the limestone in Southeast Asia for its features, massive cliffs and variety, and his tip is Green Climbers Home in Laos, where he reckons you could spend six months and not repeat a climb.

### Scuba diving

Isaac got his PADI licence two summers ago in Thailand. On his second day, at a big shipwreck called the HTMS Chang, the dive master banged on his tank and made the shark signal. Isaac was looking all around for a reef shark until the dive master pointed up, and there was a baby whale shark cruising right above them. They abandoned the rest of the dive plan to spend the whole time with it.

### Paragliding and sea kayaking

Next on his list is [paragliding](https://abigailhannah.nz/paragliding-new-zealand/), which we tried together a few years ago, when he has a bit more time and money.

His other big pursuit is [sea kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/114-sea-kayaking-for-beginners-and-the-best-of-the-hauraki-gulf-with-bennett-lim/), a very different pace and environment to the river. He got into it through a paper in his degree about the ocean, covering marine biology, conservation and cultural context, with five days paddling from far out in East Auckland back to Takapuna Beach.

I do plenty of sea kayaking with Isaac, and I love it. A flowing river, I'm not so keen on. We've done [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/) and Queen Charlotte together, and a couple of overnighters in the [Hauraki Gulf](https://abigailhannah.nz/025-sailing-stephanie-haden/).

## Why outdoor clubs are the best way in {#outdoor-clubs}

Whatever the sport, Isaac recommends a club. Sea kayak clubs hire out kayaks and run courses and pool sessions where you can learn to roll in a controlled environment, and the same goes for climbing, diving, tramping and whitewater. Gear is a big barrier to entry. When we recorded, hiring all the gear for a whitewater club weekend cost $30, and buying your own, even second-hand, costs a lot more: a big investment if you're not sure you love the sport.

Clubs are also so used to beginners that they aren't elitist. You'll be learning alongside a whole bunch of people with no clue, and the instructors were most probably beginners a couple of years ago. As Isaac puts it:

> "People want to help you to love the sport because then they can have more friends that do it."

## Van life and Isaac's advice for getting outside {#van-life-advice}

Before we got married, Isaac asked if I'd ever want to do campervan trips, and I asked if he was crazy. Now I'm the one asking to go away every weekend.

His first idea of it came from Nepal, watching travellers come through on round-the-world drives in Land Rover Defenders covered in stickers, then from the early Instagram days of #vanlife. What appeals is the freedom: no booking ahead, your home and all your gear with you, so you can chase the good weather, or wherever your mates are kayaking.

Our current one is our third [van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/). The first was a bit of a rust box. Just off the ferry at Picton, smoke started filling up the back. We still did a lap of the South Island, and on the Waikato Expressway on the way home I heard a massive clunk. Isaac said it was fine. We got home and the whole exhaust had fallen off.

Our forever van started as a simple build with a bed and a self-containment certificate, and it lasted us two years. For Isaac, that goes for all of the outdoors: be safe and buy gear that will look after you, then beg, borrow and carpool.

> "Just go do it, because you'll be far happier doing it than having a cupboard full of gear at home and no money to spend actually going on a trip."

His parting advice: if you don't consider yourself outdoorsy, challenge yourself to try something. It doesn't have to be epic. Go for a walk in the bush or sit near the water, and if you like it, see if there's an outdoor club near you. We're blessed in New Zealand to have so much incredible nature, of every flavour.
