Veronika has been on my radar for a really really long time (I remember her brand before the rebrand). And the week we recorded, someone asked on my Instagram stories for [adventures around Auckland with kids](https://abigailhannah.nz/141-best-christchurch-and-canterbury-adventures-with-kids-with-jasmine-trafford/). Perfect timing. I couldn't have planned it better.

Veronika calls herself a modest adventure mum. She's Muslim, she wears hijab, and she moved to New Zealand on her own at 15. She was never outdoorsy growing up in Russia. Then New Zealand made her fall in love with the outdoors. These days she runs a modest adventure wear brand for women like her, and she takes her two boys all over the place.

So this post has her five favourite Auckland spots for kids, from the West Coast to up north. Plus what changes when kids come along, the day her husband carved chopsticks, and the barriers that keep a lot of hijabi women out of the outdoors.

## From Russia to the outdoors in Aotearoa {#russia-to-aotearoa}

Veronika grew up in a small industrial town in Russia. It wasn't very safe roaming around in nature there, and there were always horror stories about ticks, so she never got into it. To get outdoors properly she'd have had to travel out of town. Her idea of the best outdoor day was going to a cafe.

The weather didn't help. Her town had two extremes: hot, dusty and dry in summer, and minus 40 degrees in winter. At least there was central heating. When she moved to New Zealand, the lack of central heating in winter was more of a culture shock than anything, and all she'd brought with her was shorts and t-shirts.

Veronika moved here by herself at 15, to study. It was a blur and she was so stressed, but she knew she didn't really belong in Russia, and she was ready to go. She came on a one-way ticket. The goal was to finish high school, get an education and find a way to stay, and it all worked out. She finished high school, went to film school, made a lot of friends, got married, and the rest was history.

### Getting into the outdoors on a student budget

Her love of the outdoors started because she and her husband were students without much money. What do you do when you want to hang out and you've got no money? You go hiking. And if you want to travel around, you take a tent.

They watched other New Zealanders exploring, and started slow, with a small camping trip in a tent they borrowed from somebody, because they didn't have anything. The idea was to see if they liked it before they invested. Slowly but surely, they fell in love with spending time outdoors in lots of different ways.

I love that as a way to start. Find someone you can borrow gear from and ask all the questions, because when it's your first time, you have no idea what you don't know.

## Adventuring with kids: what changes {#adventuring-with-kids}

Everything changes when you have kids, and for Veronika a lot of it is about how much you have to pack. Kids get dirty easily, so unless you want to be washing clothes the whole time, you need plenty of spares, plus nappies and all the rest.

When her big boy was really little, she had first-mum anxiety and would always overpack and overthink things. She had to learn to let go. Snacks are a big one, because the goal is for the kids to have a good time: if they're having a good time, everybody else is too.

Funnily enough, it got easier when her second boy was born. With your first, you revolve your life around them. The second one already fits into the puzzle and tags along on the journey. So adjusting to adventuring with two kids was easier for her than with one.

Over time they've increased the difficulty level, though she still finds their adventures quite mild. She always chooses kid-friendly trails where there are stops to play. Walking and walking through bush with no sunlight isn't what they're about. She picks adventures the whole family can enjoy.

And she reckons more parents should give it a go. As she puts it:

> "parenting is so much easier outdoors. Like a lot of people are intimidated to take their kids out."

If you have the snacks, kids don't need toys out there. They make up their own stories and games.

## Five Auckland walks for kids {#auckland-walks-kids}

Veronika admits she probably has a very different definition of child friendly. Her family tends to go a little off the beaten path, because she doesn't like dealing with crowds. She also loves a view. Hiking through bush with no views isn't her thing, and scenic walks bring her so much joy. Here are her five.

### Mercer Bay Loop

The Mercer Bay Loop is the one she goes back to again and again because it's so scenic. You have to really watch your kids there, and she knows some people would say that's not very kid friendly, but her kids are okay with it. They stop, enjoy the view and play on the viewing platforms.

It's one of my favourites too. I love going back to Mercer Bay over and over, and I've done it with some of my best friends, so it holds so many memories.

The West Coast walks along the coast are beautiful for this. I'm really sad the Te Henga Walkway closed, all the way from Muriwai to Bethells Beach. It's a full day hike along the clifftops and it was so beautiful, but after the big storm there were too many landslides and it was unsafe. When we recorded, it was still closed. I really hope it reopens.

### Kitekite Falls

Out that way, Veronika rates Kitekite Falls as super child friendly, with a beautiful waterfall at the end. Her tip: if you work on your own schedule, go during the week, when it's always empty. I second that.

### Ti Point

Up north, the family loves Ti Point, near Omaha. It's an easy, short walk that goes steadily up and down along the coastline, without any big climbs or drops, and there's a little beach on the way where they fish. They have a picnic and play, then carry on, so it becomes a whole-day experience, and it's never busy.

The path is narrow and scenic, so it makes you feel like an adventurer, and it ticks every box for them. In summer, when the pōhutukawa bloom, it's gorgeous.

I've only done Ti Point carrying ropes and all our climbing gear, because there's [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/) on the cliffs at the end. It's heavy. I'd love to go back for a casual walk when the pōhutukawa are out.

### Scandrett Regional Park

Scandrett Regional Park was a happy accident. One Saturday, on the way to Ti Point, they got stuck in traffic for the Matakana market. Not wanting to spend 45 minutes going three kilometres, Veronika turned around, opened Apple Maps and found Scandrett.

They go there to fish. At low tide you walk along the coastline to Mullet Point, about 20 to 25 minutes in and out. Her husband fishes, the kids explore the rock pools (starfish, shrimp and all kinds of crabs), and she pops in an audiobook and has a grand old time.

You do need to check the tides before you go, because at high tide you can't get there. They've had a few fun moments fishing until the last possible minute and then rushing back before the tide came in.

### Piha's south end at low tide

Her fifth is back on the West Coast at Piha. At the south end of the beach, you go to the end and around the dunes on a little path. At low tide, there's a tunnel through the rock, which fills up at high tide, and it takes you through to a secret beach on the other side.

She filmed one of the series for her brand there, and calls it Indiana Jones if he was a hijabi. Again, you need to time the tides. The kids took torches into the caves, which go quite deep, and it was very exciting for them.

That's one of my favourite things about Piha: I keep finding new areas. One time at super low tide, we got round the ocean side of Lion Rock to some caves and a tiny little bay. Just keep kids away from the waves, because they can be dangerous.

## When things go wrong with kids outdoors {#when-things-go-wrong}

Veronika is pretty prepared, but she often forgets something. It's never been a crisis, because you become resourceful.

The family likes to have ramen after a hike (same, by the way). One day, she forgot all the utensils. The kids wanted ramen, she wanted ramen, and there was nothing to eat it with. So her husband took his buck knife and made chopsticks. Problem solved.

A lot of it comes down to attitude. As a new mum, forgetting something like a swaddle blanket would have sent her into a panic. Now she's much more laid back: no swaddle blanket? Use a spare hijab.

She also reckons a bit of planning covers most things:

> "If you do your planning right, you'll be just fine if you forget one little thing."

That means doing your research before you head out, like knowing about the tides, and using common sense, especially with little people who depend on you to make smart choices.

## Finding new places and Pokohino Beach {#finding-new-places}

To find somewhere new, Veronika goes to social media and searches things like hikes in Auckland or beaches in Auckland.

A couple of weeks before we recorded, the family went to Pokohino Beach, near Onemana in the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/). It's a secret beach you reach on a little walk. It was all over TikTok and she had a serious case of FOMO, so on a last-minute decision they drove two hours to explore it, and loved it. She made her own video about it, hoping someone else finds it and goes too.

Every time I'd been to that beach it was empty, until last summer, when there were other people on it. Now I know how they found it: TikTok.

## Modest adventure wear and the barriers for hijabi women {#modest-adventure-wear}

What Veronika wears outdoors has changed a lot, and she's dedicated her professional life to solving that problem. She's shared before and after posts, and she describes what she used to wear as ridiculously uncomfortable. She wore it because she wanted to get out there and explore.

A lot of women are held back by that, she says. You want to look good and feel put together. But with layers upon layers, leggings over shorts and long-sleeve tops under t-shirts to make things more modest, you can barely move and you don't feel your best. Outdoors, you need something practical.

Then there's the worry about people staring. New Zealand is multicultural, but you still stick out like a sore thumb in a hijab, and plenty of women decide they don't want to put themselves through it. Swimming at the beach is a big one.

For Veronika, a lot of it comes down to mindset. Being active outdoors brings her so much joy that she wasn't going to give it up over what other people might think. So she got out there however she looked in that season of life. She enjoyed it, but there were challenges, and that's why she changed her entire career path.

Her brand started six years before we recorded as Fit Generation Apparel, which she calls a great learning ground. Once she'd learned more about business and marketing, she rebranded in 2023 to Wommo, all about women, modesty and exploring the outdoors. When we recorded, it was in its second year. The idea is modest adventure wear that covers women properly and works, so they can explore with confidence. She even designed a shirt with Indiana Jones energy, so you can tap into that identity and feel like a badass.

It's like starting a fitness journey, she says: get yourself a couple of cool sets, and you're the person who shows up.

I felt very attacked by that. I bought running shoes last year and have barely used them. Veronika's advice: walk in them first, and stay consistent.

## Stories, assumptions and finding her faith {#stories-and-faith}

So how can the rest of us help break down those barriers? For Veronika, it starts small, with bringing the people around you on board. That's why she started creating content. When she put on the hijab 10 years before we recorded, she found other women sharing their stories, and for her it all comes down to sharing a story. She shows up online to show the lifestyle is possible, hoping it inspires other women.

She also thinks exposure to other cultures changes people. She describes herself as a very sheltered white girl from a small town in Russia. She wasn't born Muslim, and she never thought she'd become Muslim. Then she came here, met awesome Muslim people, looked into the faith and found the path for her. That shifted everything.

Her ask is to let go of ignorance and question the why. People see her hijab and assume she's from the Middle East, where she's never lived, without getting to know her. Respect goes both ways. As she puts it:

> "all beliefs matter, all people matter, all stories matter."

Stories travel. We once met people doing [Te Araroa](https://abigailhannah.nz/043-te-araroa-thru-hike-zoe/) who'd come all the way from Canada for the thru-hike, and that's a story I tell other people now.

Not every culture grows up with the outdoors on its doorstep, either. So find out which of your friends haven't had that experience, and take them with you. I'm so passionate about this because I think there's such a strong connection between the outdoors and our [wellbeing](https://abigailhannah.nz/052-walking-wellbeing/).

## A caravan trip to the snow {#caravan-snow-trip}

The whole goal of this trip was to show her kids snow. After growing up in Russia, Veronika had seen plenty of snow for a lifetime, and she loves that in New Zealand you can drive to the mountain for a day of snow fights and then head back to warmer weather.

Her boys had never seen it, and her big boy kept asking about it, especially when they looked through old family photos full of big fur coats and snowball fights. Why was he in New Zealand, where there's no snow?

So when her husband asked what she wanted for her 30th birthday, she suggested a family trip and a week off. They [hired a caravan](https://abigailhannah.nz/166-caravan-living-as-a-family-and-writing-music-on-the-road-with-emily-charles/), packed everything up, saw the snow, fished and unplugged. It was off-season, so it was quite inexpensive.

Growing a brand from the ground up takes so much out of you, and they'd been in go, go, go mode for the longest time, so they all recharged their batteries.
