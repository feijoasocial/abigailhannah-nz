This one is a bit different. For once I'm the one in the guest seat, and the person asking the questions is my husband, [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/). Weird. And really really fun.

In June the podcast turned four. Four years of a new episode every single week! So after a few months off, it felt like the right time to rewind and tell you how it all started, because I ask my guests about their stories every week and I've never properly told mine.

So we go right back. Growing up in India and going to boarding school, moving to Auckland at 18, my first tramp (I hated it), and the first South Island road trip that made my whole world a little bit bigger. Then quitting my corporate job, building a business from the road, and the last two and a half years with no home address (and how soooo many of our plans changed along the way). Plus the two bits of advice from guests that got us to the start of the Slow Paddle, and a hint of what Isaac wants to do next.

## Four years of the podcast and taking a break {#four-years}

I'd recently had a few months off recording, so that's where Isaac started. Four years of making something every single week fills my cup, and it's also a lot of work, for me and for my team. It can feel like running and running without thinking about which direction you're running in. So [when I announced the break](https://abigailhannah.nz/were-hitting-pause/), the idea was to take my foot off the gas for a second and think about where I'm going.

My last regular recordings were in February, apart from a couple during the [Slow Paddle](https://abigailhannah.nz/182-finishing-a-two-month-slow-paddle-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/), and that trip turned out to be a really good break, a chance to reflect on life and what I want to do with things.

### What the listener survey said

During the break I sent out a survey, and so many of you filled it in, which was so so helpful. The gist: you're doing a great job, we love the show, please don't stop. So not a lot is changing. People did say they wanted to hear more about my adventures, and more from Isaac too, so there will be more of both of us when we have something to talk about.

### How it started, and what I've learnt

One thing people learn about me: when I get an idea, I usually act on it pretty quickly. I had the idea for the podcast at the same time as I started my corporate job, right after a phenomenal South Island road trip. I wanted to bottle that feeling of adventure and being on the road, and the campfire yarns you have with your mates at a beach or by a lake or on a mountain somewhere. So Aotearoa Adventures was born, and it became the thing that gave me joy right through that corporate job.

Hopefully I've become a better interviewer since episode one, with fewer ums and ahs for my editor, and when I listen back to some early episodes I cringe a bit. My favourite moment is listening back to an episode from two or three years ago, thinking "I hope she asks about this next", and then hearing myself ask it. Slay, girl.

I also love the listener segment I started last year, where you send in questions for upcoming guests. The show wouldn't exist without the generosity of my guests, and how open and vulnerable they're willing to be when I ask the hard questions. It's a community effort.

Isaac asked whether it'll be another four years, and I don't have an answer. We talk about moving overseas pretty regularly, and I've wondered whether I'd still tell New Zealand adventure stories if we didn't live here. The stories aren't going to run out, though.

## Growing up in India and boarding school {#growing-up-india}

Isaac knows how I start my episodes, so he asked about my childhood.

I was born in Auckland, at the hospital next to the museum, and the first country I ever visited was the Philippines, as a baby. I took my first steps there.

Then we moved to India, where I had my first birthday, a very round, very bald baby (those photos will never go on the internet). We moved around a lot when I was little. We lived in Ranchi and Hyderabad, and mostly in South India, which is super different to the north. We did live in the north when I was two, and apparently I'd sleep talk in Hindi. Some of my first words were in Hindi, and I can still speak a little.

When I was 10, I went to boarding school in Ooty, in Tamil Nadu. It was an international school, historically British, and getting there from home took 26 hours: 23 hours on a train and three hours in a taxi.

Isaac asked if it was like Hogwarts, and in some ways it was. There was a dining hall with long tables, a train to get to school and uniforms (no capes). It was in a quaint little town up in the hills, which was nice, because it was a lot cooler up there.

I really wanted to go. I'd known for as long as I can remember that I would go there one day, and it's also where I met my husband. It must have been destiny.

Isaac went there too. On a visit when he was five, he asked to be left behind, probably for the trampoline.

## Moving to Auckland at 18 {#moving-to-auckland}

I have a New Zealand passport, and the plan was always that we'd come back to New Zealand. So at 18, after school, I moved to Auckland. My own plan was to do uni, finish in three years and get back out of this boring little place at the bottom of the world.

Growing up, we'd come back to visit every few years, so I knew Auckland a little. Anyone who's been an expat, or grown up in another country, knows what it's like to have your heart in two places. I still feel it. When I go back to India, it's "oh, I'm home". When I come back to Aotearoa, it's "oh, I'm home".

### Isaac's one-way ticket

At graduation I waved goodbye to my high school boyfriend and thought I'd never see that boy again. Plot twist. Isaac spent six months in America, then decided it wasn't where he wanted to be for the next four years. So he came to New Zealand on a working holiday visa, on a one-way ticket, to see how long he'd stay. Nine and a half years later, here we are.

We never thought we'd end up in Auckland this long. We keep trying to leave, clearly unsuccessfully, and I do have a soft spot for it.

### Uni, mental health and rediscovering home

I studied urban planning at Auckland Uni, and it took me five years, because I hit a really bad spot with my mental health. The path to recovery was not linear.

Something that really helped was spending time outside, and rediscovering home. I think I was mourning India a little bit. It's an entire loss of identity at 18, and you have to rebuild your life in a new place. I don't think there are two countries further apart: Auckland is chill and slow, and India is loud, busy, noisy and chaotic, full of colours and flavours. It's an assault on your senses all the time, and it's so rich. In New Zealand I was like, cool, there's some birds.

Turning that around meant being grateful to be here. We have world-class beaches 30 minutes' drive from home, which is why I love Auckland so much, plus incredible hikes and waterfalls, and further afield, mountains, bush and sand dunes that feel out of this world. That's when we both started to think maybe this could be home.

From day one, Isaac and I made an effort to explore Auckland: walks in the Waitākere Ranges, the regional parks, and the ferry over to Rangitoto.

I wanted to see more of the country from the start. At 18 I planned a road trip around [Northland](https://abigailhannah.nz/176-a-locals-guide-to-northland-and-building-hiking-communities-with-hannah-whittaker/), the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/) and the Waikato with Isaac, a friend and Isaac's best mate from Australia. The boys didn't get their visas in time, so it never happened.

## My first tramp: Pirongia and the Pinnacles {#first-tramp}

My first tramp was Pirongia, and I hated it. It was so so muddy. We went on Queen's Birthday weekend, in June, which is pretty wet season, and Pirongia isn't known for being dry even in summer.

Our more experienced tramping friends told us it was three or four hours a day. For us newbies it turned into eight and ten hour days. I kept thinking it must be around the next corner. We haven't been back.

I hold grudges, long grudges. The Pinnacles was another one I held a grudge against for a really long time. Then we went back in January, seven years later, and it redeemed itself. So maybe Pirongia can be redeemed too.

## Our first South Island road trip {#first-road-trip}

Our first South Island road trip was in 2020. Covid had closed the borders, so it was: we can't go overseas, let's go to the South Island.

### From a station wagon to a rust box van

This was peak van life Pinterest. Isaac had been dreaming over people in VW Kombis driving around national parks in America, and thought we should try that in New Zealand. It took him years to warm me up to it. My first thought was: why would I sleep in a van?

Even before the van, we'd spent a couple of nights sleeping in our first car, a Volkswagen Golf station wagon, on a tramping stretcher. At every viewing, Isaac would lie down in the back to see if he fitted lengthways. He didn't fit in any of them. We spent the frostiest night ever on the side of the Desert Road in that Golf. We were so cold. We didn't even know diesel heaters existed back then.

The van was our second vehicle (third if you count our scooters), a 1990 Mitsubishi Delica from Facebook Marketplace. It was a [rust box](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/), and the brakes didn't work. On the drive home, standing on the brake pedal did nothing, and the mechanic found there was no brake pad left. We got the brakes sorted, did a very quick fit-out on the driveway and drove to the South Island. So when we say you can start with nothing, we mean it.

### The West Coast, Aoraki and Kaikōura

On that trip, my whole world got a little bit bigger. You feel it any time you go somewhere new: I didn't know this existed, I didn't know mountains this beautiful were right here. Until then my world was Auckland, Tauranga and Taupō.

We had four days of rain down the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/), and it was still incredible. Then [Aoraki](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) stole my heart: we camped beside Lake Pūkaki and watched the sunset over the mountain, and that's a spot we've been back to so many times, with so many different people.

On the way back to the ferry, we nearly missed it, because we were at the seal colony in [Kaikōura](https://abigailhannah.nz/054-kaikoura-to-aoraki/) and I was losing it over a baby seal. We were one of the last cars on.

The other big change was the people. We met up with other creatives and photographers on that trip, and it opened our eyes to a whole new friend group and community, and a way of life we hadn't really known in Auckland.

## From urban planning to running my own business {#urban-planning-to-business}

I love urban planning. I couldn't imagine studying one subject for three whole years, and urban planning had papers in economics, history, cultural issues, infrastructure and design, and on and on. That was a bit of me.

After uni I worked at one of Australia's largest infrastructure engineering consultancies, with a front-seat view of major projects: roads, trains, wind farms. I threw myself at every opportunity, a giant sponge learning how businesses and client relationships work. It had such a good culture, and I had an amazing team. At the end of the day, though, the creative spark in me wasn't fulfilled.

I wanted a location-independent job, so that when Isaac decides he wants to move to Thailand and be a scuba dive instructor, I can go with him. He's studying outdoor education, so there'll be plenty of interesting places to work. I'd been creating and posting on social media for a long time, and I'd started the podcast at the same time as that job, so I'd already dipped my toes into freelancing. I knew I'd run my own business one day. It was a question of when.

### More mountains on more Thursday mornings

Then came a ten-day South Island trip, squeezed out of two days of annual leave. One Thursday morning, I was at the top of a mountain for sunrise. It was freezing, it had snowed, and there were kea. I thought: what am I doing with my life? I need to be on more mountains on more Thursday mornings. Isaac remembers me saying I wanted more of this, sitting up there in the cold. [I quit](https://abigailhannah.nz/bonus-i-quit-my-corporate-job/) the next Tuesday.

### Feijoa Social and teaching creatives

These days I run an agency called Feijoa Social, doing social media management for brands in the travel and outdoor industry. That's my bread and butter, and I love that part of my job so much.

Last year I also started teaching creatives how to get paid, with a course and community called Pricing and Partnerships Mastery. The feedback has been phenomenal, and seeing other people have those light-bulb moments gives me so much joy.

I'm such an independent girly, and I love being my own boss.

## Two and a half years with no home address {#no-home-address}

After we got married, we rented a flat together for almost five years. Once I'd quit, there wasn't much tying us to that spot, and the irony is that when the rental finished, we thought we only had six months left in Auckland. We planned to move to Central Otago: Wānaka, Queenstown or Cromwell, somewhere down there.

I love a plan. Looking back on the last two and a half years, I've had to let go of so many of them.

### The limbo season

We went overseas for three months, which was a phenomenal trip. Coming back, the plan was to move straight into the van and do [full-time van life](https://abigailhannah.nz/121-avoiding-vanlife-mistakes-and-life-on-the-road-in-nz-with-tiff-and-nathan/), a dream for a really long time.

That was probably the hardest season, because the van build took months longer than we expected. At the start it was always just two more weeks, and it stayed like that the whole time. We couch surfed with friends, then found [house sits](https://abigailhannah.nz/151-house-sitting-across-new-zealand-with-travel-writer-jessica-lockhart/), only short ones, two or three weeks, because we'd have the van back soon. Anyone who's built a house knows delays happen. It was still frustrating.

When we finally got the van, we went south and had an amazing summer. Then we came back to the North Island to start the Slow Paddle, two months of kayaking from [Cape Reinga](https://abigailhannah.nz/162-we-are-kayaking-from-cape-reinga-to-tauranga-to-raise-20-000-/) to Auckland.

Since the paddle, we've been house sitting. We didn't want to live in the van over winter: it's wet and cold, and being stuck in a small space when the weather's bad didn't appeal. It's been so good for me to hustle, get stuck into work and bring the podcast back.

### Holding plans loosely

Moving around in India and boarding school meant I never had the same bed for more than six months, so having no address for two and a half years isn't that wild for me.

There were times I felt shame or disappointment in myself, like we should be doing better. Life has been so cool, though, and it's taken me a while to own that. We have no stable address, and we're also a long way from the full-time global jet-setting a lot of people picture with a non-traditional lifestyle. We're somewhere in the middle: we love being close to friends in Auckland, and we love extended adventures, like the Slow Paddle, a stint in the South Island or a stint overseas.

Isaac sees it the same way: we've learnt to hold our plans more loosely. Life doesn't always go the way you imagine, and that can lead to some beautiful surprises you never saw coming. For him, it's more choice and more freedom to decide how we live. This is our season of life, and who knows, in five or 10 years we might change our minds.

## Living a different life, and how to start {#living-differently}

So many people on the podcast live alternative lifestyles. [Sagar and Ami](https://abigailhannah.nz/171-planning-an-adult-gap-year-to-full-time-van-life-with-sagar-and-ami/) (we caught up with them the weekend before we recorded) did an adult gap year, then sold their house and bought a motorhome. I've talked to families who live in [caravans](https://abigailhannah.nz/166-caravan-living-as-a-family-and-writing-music-on-the-road-with-emily-charles/) or motorhomes. You can live a different life at whatever stage you're at, and the only thing between you and that is the decision itself.

Two pieces of advice from people on the podcast got us to the start of the Slow Paddle:

* **Put a date in the calendar.** Once we decided this was the summer and this was the date we'd start at Cape Reinga, it was happening. Without that, I think it wouldn't have. As Isaac says, pipe dreams stay pipe dreams when they're only in your head.
* **Tell your friends and family.** Then you're accountable, because they keep asking how it's going.

I did both of those on purpose, because I knew that if we didn't, it wouldn't happen.

## What's next: Slow Paddle part two and the Mongol Rally {#whats-next}

I wanted the Slow Paddle to be one and done. It's now part one, because we're going again this summer. Our plans don't always work out. There's a theme here.

Isaac quite likes cars. Old cars and bad cars. Out of the cars and vans we've owned, only one has had windows with a button. There's something called the Mongol Rally, where you start in London and drive a beater of a car to Mongolia, and that's what we'd love to do next year. We're in the thinking and planning stages. Can we just finish the Slow Paddle first?
