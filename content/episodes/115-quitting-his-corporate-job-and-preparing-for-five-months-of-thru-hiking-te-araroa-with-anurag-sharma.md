I've had heaps of guests on to talk about Te Araroa after they've walked it. But a walk like that starts months, even years, before you reach Cape Reinga or Bluff. So I was really really excited to chat to Anurag right before he hits the trail.

Anurag was born in North India and has lived in Aotearoa for more than 20 years, the last 12 in windy Wellington, which is where he fell in love with hiking. He's the first guest to introduce himself in his own language (we're bringing that back). And we have India in common, because I grew up there too.

When we recorded, he'd quit his job and was about to walk Te Araroa northbound, raising money for ten charities along the way. So we talk about how he got into hiking, why he decided to do it now, his training (a walking treadmill under his desk!), his gear, his very nerdy spreadsheet, and his one piece of advice for anyone planning their own walk.

## Early walks in India {#early-walks-india}

Anurag's family moved around a lot when he was growing up, changing schools every year or two, which made it hard to make friends. He likes to think he's travelled India a lot, but mostly the army areas.

His love of hiking came later, in New Zealand, but he has a couple of early memories. One is a walk with his grandpa out to the farm down the road. At five, it felt like at least 10 or 15 kilometres. His dad recently told him it was about two. But he still remembers it as a special walk, just the two of them, with good conversations.

The other is Vaishno Devi, a sacred site above Jammu, which Anurag says is known as the city of temples. It's about 12 kilometres up and back, with about 1,300 metres of climbing, and he walked it with his dad. His dad's advice was to go low and slow and pace yourself, and he still tries to apply that whenever he's out.

These days when he goes back, his cousins all say, Anurag's here, let's go for a walk. Hiking isn't really a big part of the culture outside the north, as he understands it.

India is so diverse, and that's something I really struggle to convey to people here: the languages, the culture, the food. Jammu and Kashmir holds a special place in my heart too. When I was 16, I went trekking there for a summer. I'd never done any exercise or any tramping, and it was so so beautiful. That was my first experience of tramping.

## From two Great Walks to all ten {#great-walks}

Anurag's first multi-day hike was the [Tongariro Northern Circuit](https://abigailhannah.nz/172-hiking-the-tongariro-alpine-crossing-and-tongariro-northern-circuit-with-ben-boland/), counterclockwise. He did it, and the Whanganui River Journey, almost by accident in 2018, because his mates had the huts booked and asked if he wanted to come along. Within six months he'd done two Great Walks on borrowed gear. Walking up the scree near the top of Tongariro was a challenge, but otherwise he wasn't dying, and the landscapes were beautiful. Then he scored a 75 litre backpack on Trade Me and took it as a sign.

The Northern Circuit was my first Great Walk too.

In April this year, he finished his tenth Great Walk, the [Heaphy](https://abigailhannah.nz/133-tramping-the-heaphy-track-from-karamea-to-collingwood-with-jacq-nesbit/). For a long time it was a great excuse to hang out with his mates. He's also spent time in the Tararua Range, just up the road from Wellington, and more recently in the South Island, where he put a single mattress in the back of his car, drove around, hiked where he could and worked remotely where he could. That's been the balance: time with friends, and falling in love with the solitude of hiking. You learn a lot about yourself.

I've only done [one solo hike](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/), which I've talked about a lot, and it's a completely different experience from hiking with friends.

## Why Te Araroa, and why now {#why-te-araroa}

The idea started around lockdown. The weekend before the country shut down, Anurag and some friends did the Jumbo circuit in the Tararuas, a beautiful overnight hike with a sunset from the top of the ranges. A week later, they were in lockdown.

You can only play so many video games, so he ended up on YouTube watching a couple of hikers walk Te Araroa in about 90 days (not what he's planning). That planted the seed. He's also watched [Kurt Fenton](https://abigailhannah.nz/097-accidents-and-beauty-found-in-solo-hiking-whirinaki-conservation-park-with-kurt-fenton/)'s videos to death for the logistics. And as a data analyst, the logistics appealed to him, so he made a little spreadsheet.

He'd always seen it as something for when he was older, maybe at 50, after the classic path of school, uni, job and house. Then three years ago, his mum passed away, very young. It put things in perspective and made him think about his own mortality, and one day turned into let's do it now. Near the end of 2022 he started testing the idea at work, and they were very supportive.

He talks about the safe handbook you're given for how to live, especially as an Eastern child: be a doctor, a lawyer or an accountant. But as he puts it:

> "comfort unfortunately does lead to complacency"

Maybe he could do it at 50, but there are no guarantees. Call it a quarter-life crisis if you want, he says. He doesn't care.

It's so easy to put things off for later. When people pass away, close to you or not, you realise you're not guaranteed that time.

### Northbound from Bluff, a little later than planned

Te Araroa runs from Cape Reinga to Bluff, and most people take three or four months. Anurag is planning on four to five, because he's quit his job and wants to take his sweet time.

His original plan was to walk south from Cape Reinga, starting on 1 October. When we recorded, three weeks after that date, he hadn't started. A lump on his ankle, which he first noticed after the Heaphy, turned out to need minor surgery, booked for the day after we recorded. So he switched to walking [northbound](https://abigailhannah.nz/132-nine-months-of-solitude-walking-northbound-on-te-araroa-with-naomi-arnold/) from Bluff, starting on 18 November.

## Quitting a job to walk Te Araroa {#quitting-job}

Anurag has been with the same big organisation for almost 13 years, moving between analyst roles. When he told the wider business unit he was leaving, the word most people used was brave. He wouldn't call it bravery himself.

A while back, on that South Island road trip, he picked up a Wilderness magazine with a cover line about quitting a job to walk Te Araroa. He still has it in the back of his car, and he took it as a tohu, a sign.

Handing in his notice has also taught him about his self-worth. People respect the leap, and they want to know his why and what he'll do after. One workmate he'd never spoken to before told him about giving tours at a sanctuary one day a week because time outside fills his cup. Anurag would never have had that conversation otherwise.

I quit my job a year ago this month, so this one hit home. I'm still hung up on his coworkers calling him brave. It shouldn't be brave to step out of your comfort zone. If it doesn't work out, you come back.

## Training for Te Araroa {#training}

Anurag made two decisions early that really helped. First, he got an online coach for his programming: the gym three times a week and mobility most days. Second, he bought a walking treadmill for his standing desk.

Long-distance hiking is about time on your feet, he learned early in his research. So during meetings he'd have it at about four kilometres an hour on a slight incline. People got used to it, with a few jokes. He also worked on his eating, which meant fewer pizzas. He's looking forward to trail food, because he has a killer sweet tooth and wants to eat so much chocolate. (A pack of Tim Tams won the weekend before we recorded.)

He knows he'll be walking 25 kilometres a day, so the fitness will come on the trail too.

## Te Araroa gear and logistics {#gear-and-logistics}

Every kilo makes a difference, so Anurag did a lot of research. His tent is a Zpacks Duplex, the kind you pitch with your hiking poles. It's single-walled, so condensation is an issue and he carries a cloth to wipe it down. His pack is a 55 litre Hyperlite Southwest. It's a once-in-a-lifetime adventure, so he decided to invest, saving up and watching for deals. His tip: outdoor stores have great sales over winter, because no one buys hiking gear then. He's already bought most of his dehydrated meals for the South Island on sale, and his food parcels are ready to send.

And yes, there's a spreadsheet. He's trying to be kind to himself on distance, with a five-day rolling average of kilometres: if it goes over 25, that's his sign to slow down. He has friends in Wānaka, Christchurch, Nelson, Palmerston North, Hamilton and Auckland, so he'll be asking them for places to stay. In the South Island, it's mostly DOC huts.

### The Te Araroa trail pass

When we recorded, the Te Araroa Trust had teamed up with DOC on a new trail pass for the season, an alternative to the yearly Backcountry Hut Pass. Check the current details before you go.

He's also keeping a notebook of contact details for trail angels and kaitiaki along the way, so he can give them a heads-up. The FarOut app and the Te Araroa app are both handy, though he's looked at the route so many times he could probably do it without them. Switching from south to north meant redoing the whole spreadsheet.

## Fundraising for ten charities {#fundraising}

Anurag says quitting a job and taking time off to do something like this is a selfish thing to do, so he wanted to make it a little less selfish. He asked on Instagram which charity he should support, and his friends were very responsive. You walk through ten regions on the trail, so he's fundraising for ten charities, including Breast Cancer Foundation NZ, KidsCan, Movember (he'll be growing a mo), Oxfam Aotearoa, Wellington City Mission, Wellington Women's Refuge and the Te Araroa Trust.

The charities have given him a few things to carry to share their message. Before he'd even started, he'd hit about 10% of his target.

### After the trail

People keep asking what he'll do after. One thing he's excited about:

> "I am looking forward to knowing what I'm going to think about when I have nothing to think about."

He likes crunching numbers, so he'll probably end up doing something similar, maybe in a different capacity. As an Indian who loves cricket, sports analytics has always appealed. For now, he's trying not to look too far ahead. All he has to do is eat, sleep and walk.

## Advice for Te Araroa and the outdoors {#advice}

Anurag has one piece of advice for anyone walking Te Araroa this season or next:

> "plans will always fail. Be comfortable with that. I mean, I have planned this thing to death. I really have."

People who finished last season told him the same thing: you'll make the best plans you ever will, and it won't go according to them. If you enjoy planning and that illusion of control, go for it, as he has. He's happy to share his spreadsheet. But be comfortable that it won't go how you planned. He thought he'd start in October, and he's starting in mid-November.

Planning matters for safety too, so have a plan A, B, C, D and E. That's why we have the whole alphabet.

### Migrants and South Asians in the outdoors

I asked Anurag what he'd say to immigrants and South Asians, who are really underrepresented in the outdoors. He talks about the societal expectations that come with an Eastern upbringing, where taking time to learn who you are isn't as much of a focus, alongside thousands of years of beautiful tradition and culture. As a migrant, you're also finding a balance between where you came from and where you're headed.

He was 12 the first time he ever got on a plane, from New Delhi to Auckland, and it was the weirdest feeling to be at school surrounded by people who didn't share his skin colour, and to wonder why everyone was being so nice to him. His encouragement is to go out, try things and say yes. Whether or not hiking turns out to be your thing, give it a try.

When I came to New Zealand on a plane at 18, I had the same moment in reverse: suddenly everyone had the same skin colour as me. The outdoors was my own journey of self-discovery here, learning how my second home was different from India, my first. I think it's such a privilege to live in more than one culture.
