[Aoraki Mount Cook National Park](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) is probably one of my favourite places in New Zealand. So when I heard Justin had put up two new routes there, I really really wanted to hear how you even do that.

Justin was born in Germany and pretty much grew up in New Zealand, so he's about as Kiwi as it gets. He's been climbing most of his life, and these days he's deep into alpinism: mixed climbing, ice, ski touring and first ascents. When we recorded, he was overseas on a climbing trip, and he still made the time to chat (thank you, Justin).

So we get into how he went from Youth Search and Rescue to alpine rock and ice, the two new routes on Lendenfeld Peak and Mount Haast, rime bombing (yes, that's a thing), the gear, why spring is the season, and a bunch of listener questions: fear of heights, mountaineering culture here, and making the jump from hiking. I got butterflies just listening.

## Growing up in the bush and Youth Search and Rescue {#justin-story}

Justin grew up in a very adventurous family, and he was hiking before he could walk, carried up mountains in a backpack.

At intermediate he joined Junats, the junior naturalists, then Venturers (like Scouts, for slightly older kids), and Youth Search and Rescue in Hamilton. Every second weekend they'd go into the bush with a map with a line drawn on it, and they had to follow it to within 100 metres. The closer you stayed to the line, the more points you got, and there was no GPS, so it was all map and compass navigation.

It pushes you out of your comfort zone: you get lost, find your way back, and might sleep out in a bush shelter you've built yourself.

I've had guests from Youth Search and Rescue before, like [Olly Dow](https://abigailhannah.nz/kaweka-ranges/), and it's such a cool way to take a passion for the outdoors further.

## From bouldering to alpinism {#bouldering-to-alpinism}

Justin has always been a climber. He describes the progression most people follow these days: bouldering, then sport climbing indoors, then top roping and lead climbing outside, then trad climbing. Somehow trad climbing leads into the mountains. Then it's [mountaineering](https://abigailhannah.nz/148-a-mountaineering-career-and-climbing-mt-aspiring-with-dom-huxley/), alpinism, [ski touring](https://abigailhannah.nz/ski-touring-tasman-glacier/), ski mountaineering, ice climbing, and on to big walls and big ice objectives. It's a slippery slope.

You can boulder over water too: deep water soloing. There isn't a lot of it in New Zealand, Justin says, but there's some around Lake Taupō, around some of the rivers, and a little near Raglan.

I had a chat with [Josh](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/) that broke down all the climbing lingo, and afterwards I went straight to the climbing gym after a couple of years off the walls. I'd hugely underestimated how much of your body you need. My hips, my knees, my ankles were all asking what I was doing. Justin agrees: it's 100% a full body sport.

## How to start mountaineering in New Zealand {#start-mountaineering}

The best way in, Justin says, is a New Zealand Alpine Club trip, or one of the courses on its website. He's been part of the clinics at the Remarkables Mixed Rock and Ice Festival, and he rates the Snowcraft 1 and 2 clinics. They teach you what to look for in the mountains: avalanches, objective hazards, snowpack conditions and ice.

He's not trying to overwhelm anyone, he says, and jumping straight in is the wrong way to go about it. There's a lot out there that can be quite scary if it isn't dealt with correctly.

His own way in was skiing from a young age, in Germany and Austria for part of the year and then in New Zealand, then ski touring. Then he studied at Tai Poutini Polytechnic in Greymouth, which gave him a really good intro to the whole mountain environment and climbing in the alpine. He calls the first year a great course for anyone who wants to learn a lot about themselves and build up their soft skills and hard skills.

## First ascents in Aoraki Mount Cook National Park {#first-ascents}

Justin and a friend put up two new routes in the national park. One is on Lendenfeld Peak, relatively close to Pioneer Hut, though still a couple of hours' ski or walk from it. The other is on Mount Haast, a variation of a summer route done a few years earlier.

### Rime bombing on the Mutant

It started with a bail. They'd gone to climb a common chimney route called the Mutant, got a few pitches up and turned back, because the conditions weren't right. There wasn't enough ice to climb it with crampons, and too much ice to climb it in rock shoes.

The big thing with alpinism in New Zealand is starting early, usually in the dark. We get a lot of rime ice here, caused by wind and moisture in the air. It sticks to the rock and builds into long, fat needles, two to three metres wide. When the sun heats it up, pieces start melting off the rock and falling. That's rime bombing. On some objectives, especially gullies, you need to reach the top before it starts.

It sounded terrifying to me.

### Picking a line from the ground

As they bailed, they looked at the wall next to the Mutant and thought they could make something work there.

Routes can go up ground up or top down. Top down is a lot safer, but there's less adventure: you abseil from the top and work out how to link features like corners, flakes and aretes (a corner turned inside out, like a ridge of rock). Theirs was ground up. For trad climbing, you also need cracks, flakes or gaps to put protection in.

They took a photo and studied it in the hut through a few days of storm. You're never 100% sure, because a crack might be fused (completely closed), and then you're thinking about bolts.

### The new route on Lendenfeld Peak

Justin's climbing partner, an Australian climber he met at the Remarkables Mixed Rock and Ice Festival, came along after another partner pulled out. They flew up by helicopter, skied out to the line, swapped ski boots for climbing shoes and started up. His friend led a really fun, very technical first pitch, with an interesting traverse under a roof on marginal gear (Justin would still fall on it).

They're the first people to climb that part of the wall. They got three or four pitches up before the terrain eased off a lot, then bailed, because the rime bombs had started. A chunk of ice hit Justin in the shoulder, and he thought he'd broken it. He was only badly bruised. That's mother nature letting you know it's time to get down.

New routes get written up so others can follow them. In New Zealand the common directory is ClimbNZ, and Justin is uploading more to theCrag, an Australian site, especially for harder alpine climbing.

### An ice line on Mount Haast

The second route came out of cabin fever. During the storm they went for a small ski tour on the glacier, keeping away from any slopes that could avalanche, because they couldn't stay in the hut any longer.

They spotted a waterfall of ice, checked the guidebook and found no sign anyone had climbed it, or even talked about it. The first pitch was water ice 3 (WI3), not too steep. The next two were steeper, WI4+ to 5, 80 to 90 degrees. On their last day on the glacier, they woke up really early, climbed it, and found a way to link up to the summit of Mount Haast.

Only the first three pitches were really ice. The rest was mixed climbing, on snow and rime, with a lot of simul climbing. That's when the terrain isn't steep or technical enough to pitch it one at a time, so you tie in partway along the rope, the leader climbs until it comes tight, and then you both climb together with protection between you. When the leader runs out of gear, they build an anchor, bring you up and you swap. It means you cover a lot of ground quickly.

How does a first ascent feel? Justin puts it this way:

> "It's quite exciting. You're like, that was really fun. … or it might be, that was terrifying and that could have gone so wrong in so many different ways."

Next, he's been invited on an expedition to Greenland, sailing out of a fjord to climb cliffs nobody has climbed before.

## Mountaineering gear and the spring season {#gear-and-season}

If you're starting out, Justin's priorities:

* **Sturdy boots** first, for sure.
* **Gloves**, ahead of an ice axe and crampons, because you can start mountaineering in summer, when there's no ice and snow hazard, only rockfall, which is easier to manage.
* **Warm layers.**
* **Sunglasses.** Really, really important. He's had sunglasses blown off his face and broken them before.
* **A helmet.** Don't go anywhere without one.

His summer trad rack is a double rack of cams, from size 3 down to 0.1, plus a set of nuts. Most people know cams by number or by colour, since most are colour coded. The smallest nuts are brass, called RPs, because brass is softer and bites into the rock better.

Spring is definitely the best season for alpine climbing in New Zealand. The big winter avalanche hazard has passed, and the snow has settled and become isothermic: the layers are all the same temperature, so it's one big solid pack that won't slide as easily. You get ice forming, and the ice freezes the rock together, so there's less rockfall. When we recorded, the season was starting.

## Exposure and the fear of heights {#fear-of-heights}

Justin doesn't have a strong fear of exposure any more. His advice is to build it up slowly and not go too deep too fast: start on single pitch climbs, then some overhanging routes, and grow your tolerance from there.

What kicked it out of the window for him was climbing in Fiordland, from up on [Gertrude Saddle](https://abigailhannah.nz/037-earnslaw-burn-gertrude-saddle-lauren-murray/). You walk across a thin ridge with a 600 metre drop on one side and a 900 metre drop on the other. The first time, you crawl across it. Then you climb a big wall, and on the way back you run across, because your brain is used to the environment.

His other trick: if you can walk along a kerb on the ground, one foot in front of the other, you can do the same thing hundreds of metres up. You know you won't step off the kerb. The height is what freaks people out.

I got butterflies thinking about it. Even back in the climbing gym, 20 metres felt a little bit high to me.

## Mountaineering culture and weather in New Zealand {#culture-and-weather}

Justin's careful with this one, because he could annoy quite a lot of people. People who push it hard in the mountains usually get a bad reputation here, which he thinks is unfortunate. A friend of his was seen as risking his life when he felt very in control. People have different risk tolerances, and what's scary to one person might be well within someone else's experience.

A lot of New Zealanders are very humble about what they've done, and tall poppy syndrome is a big thing. You can annoy people by talking too much, and by talking too little.

The community is really small. Around [Wānaka](https://abigailhannah.nz/154-working-as-a-sky-diving-instructor-in-wnaka-with-anne-bonfert/), [Queenstown](https://abigailhannah.nz/189-moving-to-aotearoa-from-chicago-and-building-a-life-in-queenstown-with-viktoria-de-dominicis/) and Mount Cook Village, everyone knows everyone. That means you can bounce ideas off people and get trip reports and conditions updates. It also means people are quite cliquey, so finding partners is hard if you're a foreigner or new, and if you like to push a bit, as Justin does, being called dangerous or incompetent shoots around the whole community.

Then there's the weather. New Zealand is such a coastal country, with strong weather patterns coming across from Australia onto the West Coast, cold weather from the south and warm, wet weather from the north-west. Europe, with a lot of landlocked countries, can predict stable weather for weeks. Justin compares our climate to Patagonia's.

> "You need to take a little bit of risk because if you only go out when the conditions are absolutely perfect, you're not going to get anywhere because New Zealand hardly ever has absolutely perfect conditions"

## From hiking to mountaineering {#hiking-to-mountaineering}

A listener asked about the biggest thing to know when making the jump from hiking. For Justin, it's this:

* **There's often no trail.** Hiking usually follows a track or route. Mountaineering means navigating yourself.
* **Objective hazards.** Hiking has a few. Mountaineering has a lot more: snow, ice and rock that can come from above you or push you into places below you.
* **Exposure.** You're most likely walking with exposure around you, and you're not always roped up, because a rope slows you down, and in the mountains speed is safety. The weather can kill you.

In his words, that's the reality:

> "Because if you misstep and you're on the side of a mountain, you slip, you are gone."

The best way to build the skills is to take a course. You'll meet people who want to do things with you, and you can grow together. Facebook and Instagram are good for finding people too, but don't expect people who aren't guides to guide you up things.

I've got friends who do heaps of hiking and heaps of rock climbing and are now bringing the two together. Off-track trips, picking a route on a topo map, feel like good practice for the alpine as well.

### Justin's bucket list

He wants to go deeper into the Darran Mountains and deeper into Aoraki Mount Cook National Park, and to do the Symphony on Skis while climbing a few peaks along the way. It's a crossing from the west coast to the east coast on skis, unassisted (you might fly food into huts), finishing with a walk or a [packraft](https://abigailhannah.nz/196-packrafting-the-landsborough-river-from-the-west-coast-with-tom-waters/) back down. He had the chance to do half of it a few years ago, but didn't end up going.
