I really hope this one inspires someone to take that next small step towards doing things their own way. Because that's Lisa's whole story.

Lisa grew up in Germany and came to Aotearoa almost seventeen years before we recorded, for what was meant to be one year. She worked her way up in marketing, then at 33 she quit the office, bought a [camper van](https://abigailhannah.nz/179-vanlife-and-revisiting-favourite-new-zealand-hidden-gems-with-daniel-mauden/) and told everyone it was just for a summer. That summer turned into four and a half years on the road. And when we chatted, she'd sold the van and was a full-time house sitter.

So we get into how she made it work, money and remote work included, and why it ended up being the best decision she's ever made. Plus the East Cape, which is one of my favourite regions too, so I was very very happy to nerd out about it. Her stops around the coast, the camping permits, where to watch the sunrise, and a wee corner of Golden Bay most people drive straight past. Then the hard bits (a van lost to rust and a lockdown in a paddock), and her advice for living life your own way.

## From Germany to Auckland and a marketing career {#lisa-story}

Lisa had what she calls a traditional, safe and nice upbringing in a small town in northwest Germany. She lived in the same house from the age of three, biked to school and played in the streets and the forest. There was some travel, mostly within Europe, and as a teenager she went on a student exchange and lived overseas.

She came to New Zealand while she was still at university, on what was supposed to be a year of study abroad. She kept extending it, finished uni here and started working, and at some point New Zealand was simply home. She thinks it's the best place in the world.

Lisa went straight from uni into a junior marketing job and worked her way up to marketing manager. Before she started travelling, she went freelance as a virtual marketing manager, though still very much in offices in Auckland.

Back in 2017, when she decided to buy a camper van, she was working full time for one client as a contractor. They were super supportive and offered her remote hours while she travelled, way before COVID had everyone working remotely. From there she picked up other remote clients, and now she's fully remote.

## One summer in a camper van {#one-summer}

At 33, all Lisa's friends were getting married and having kids. She loved seeing them happy, but it didn't feel right for her, and she didn't want a completely career-focused life for the next thirty-plus years either. So she started asking herself what else there is, if you don't want that traditional life.

She has no idea where the idea of living in a camper van came from. It was there one day, and she thought, sweet, that's what I'm going to do.

The story she told everyone, herself included, was that she'd do it for one summer. In the back of her mind she knew it could be more. But calling it a summer made it seem far less risky: if she didn't like it, she'd come back to Auckland at the end of it and return to that life. It made it easier for the people around her to get on board too, and once they saw how much she loved it, nobody was surprised when she kept going.

Summer turned into autumn, and then came the big question: what about winter? Living in a van over winter is a very different story to the warm, long days of summer. That's when she discovered [house sitting](https://abigailhannah.nz/151-house-sitting-across-new-zealand-with-travel-writer-jessica-lockhart/). Her first winter, she house sat for a few months, which was perfect. She could escape the worst of the weather and the cold, and that's when it really became something she could do for a while.

## What vanlife costs and what it changed {#vanlife-costs}

Exploring the country, Lisa realised how little she needs:

> "realizing how little I need to be happy and to have this awesome life … was just a really liberating and freeing kind of experience."

She realised she doesn't have to work 40 hours a week for the rest of her life to afford the life that makes her happy, and that's a big part of why she kept going so long. Living in a van, she lived so cheaply that, without paying rent or a mortgage, she got ahead financially over those years even though she wasn't working full time.

She's upfront that she's very lucky with her work, which pays well and can be done from anywhere, and someone on minimum wage would have a much harder time. And be prepared for repairs: these vans always seem to have something wrong with them.

She's also noticed that back in Auckland she gets pulled into consumerism and buys things she doesn't need. Out exploring somewhere beautiful, there's nothing to spend money on.

## Golden Bay's west coast {#golden-bay-west}

Lisa has been to every region and all the major areas, and has done a few loops of the country, travelling quite [slowly](https://abigailhannah.nz/018-slow-travel-mental-wellbeing-pamela/) in the last few years. There are still so many little gravel roads down to beaches and lakes she hasn't seen, and she could keep travelling for another four years without running out.

One secret spot she didn't even know existed until someone she met up there told her about it: the west coast of [Golden Bay](https://abigailhannah.nz/192-six-week-road-trip-in-the-top-of-the-south-island-with-abigail-isaac/). Everyone goes to the east side up there, to [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/), Tākaka, the coast and Farewell Spit. But from Collingwood you can cut through, driving alongside a massive estuary for ages, and come out on the west coast. It's so amazing and so off the beaten track. She had her stand up [paddle board](https://abigailhannah.nz/112-crossing-the-cook-strait-on-a-paddleboard-and-surf-lifesaving-with-britt-spencer/) with her and paddled the estuary and all the bays.

I loved that spot near Collingwood so much. It's so pretty.

Her tip for finding places like it: follow the sign to the beach you've never heard of.

## East Cape road trip highlights {#east-cape-highlights}

People always ask Lisa for her favourite part of New Zealand. She doesn't have one, but the East Cape always comes to mind. It was the one big part of the North Island she'd never been to, so she went that first summer, in January and February 2018, and she's been back twice since.

Afterwards she wrote a blog post about it called New Zealand's unpolished gem, because that's how it felt:

> "it was like a bit more … rough and rugged than other parts of the country. Definitely less touristy."

She imagines a lot of other parts of New Zealand might have been like that twenty, thirty or forty years ago. The landscape and scenery are beautiful, and she really appreciated that the people always felt real.

### Before you go

When we recorded, the East Cape had been through really big floods, and the community had been pretty badly affected by Gabrielle. Parts of it are very remote, with only one road going around. So check the official websites to see what's open, whether it's safe and whether visiting is a good idea.

If you do go, Lisa reckons it's a community that would really appreciate you paying for a campground, or having that extra meal at the pub.

### Tolaga Bay and Tokomaru Bay

Starting from Gisborne, you follow the coastline past some really cool bays. Tolaga Bay is quite well known for its massive wharf, and there are walks up the hill. There's a great campground there too, right behind the dunes on the beach.

A bit further up, Tokomaru Bay is a little settlement, and Lisa had great [surf](https://abigailhannah.nz/learn-to-surf-new-zealand/) there.

### East Cape Lighthouse at sunrise

Further up again, the East Cape Lighthouse is a must-do for Lisa. It's said to be the first place in the world to see the day begin, so go for sunrise. The walk sounds short and involves a lot of steps, so plan extra time, especially if you're going up in the dark.

The road out to the lighthouse was a highlight for me too. It runs right along the coastline, with waves crashing over the rocks, and on the other side were massive fields going up into the hills, full of horses, like a scene out of a fairy tale.

Lisa had the same impression of that road, with the ocean on one side and the horses on the other.

### Mount Hikurangi and the carvings

One of Lisa's highlights from her second trip was going up Mount Hikurangi to see the Māori carvings. You can walk up, or do a tour, which is what she did. The guide's storytelling was amazing, and between the carvings, the story behind them and the views over the East Cape, it was a total highlight.

From there, keep following the coast, with heaps of bays to stop at along the way, and eventually you reach Ōpōtiki.

### My own East Cape trip

I did the East Cape in the summer of 2021 with a bunch of mates, and none of us had heard much about it, so we drove and saw what happened.

A rock slide outside Gisborne was a massive highlight. It's a huge natural rock, like a waterfall with a massive pool at the bottom, and people slide down on boogie boards, air mattresses or inner tubes. I only did one run because it was pretty terrifying, but the boys went over and over again.

Lisa reckons she now has to go back to the East Cape and experience it for herself.

## Camping on the East Cape {#east-cape-camping}

It's been a few years, so check the current rules. At the time, there was a really great system: you bought a camping permit from the council, and it let you park at several spots around the coast. It ended up being about $3 a night. It came with a rubbish bag, and there were rubbish drop-off points. Lisa was more than happy to pay $3 a night if it meant the spots were looked after, and she used it a lot.

She also stayed at the campground at Tolaga Bay, and at another one up at the northern tip of the East Cape. At the time, there were no public dump stations or fresh water points around the East Cape, so on that first trip, almost four weeks long, she stayed at campgrounds in between to fill the water tanks. She likes that anyway: campground owners are always happy to have a chat and give you a few tips.

## Losing a van to rust and two lockdowns {#van-challenges}

The biggest setback came about eighteen months into her vanlife, when Lisa lost her first van to rust. It's a long story, involving insurance and a garage that had passed a warrant of fitness it shouldn't have. For months the van wasn't road legal, and she house sat while she waited for answers. Not having her home was really hard.

Funnily enough, it made her more determined. The whole way through, she was totally convinced she'd buy another van and start travelling again as soon as she could afford it.

COVID was tough at times too. The first lockdown she quite enjoyed: she ended up at a campground near the beach in Christchurch and met some really nice people. But she had to be in Auckland for the second long lockdown, and she spent about three months parked in a paddock in Pukekohe. The people who took her in were lovely, and she knows she was lucky, but three months stuck in a grass field was a really hard time. She thought about making a run for it a few times.

On the whole, the good stuff far outweighs the challenges. She calls it, without a doubt, the best decision she's ever made and the best time of her life. When we recorded, she was thinking about getting a van, or maybe a caravan, again, because she really misses it.

## Life done differently: lessons and advice {#life-done-differently}

Writing is second nature for Lisa. Her most recent book, Life Done Differently, tells this story: wondering what to do with your life if you don't want to get married, have kids and focus on your career, then the van, the ups and downs, and how it changed her priorities.

The biggest lesson is that realisation of how little she needs to live a really good life, which means she can work less and have more time. The other big one is living with intent. She wants to be present and make conscious decisions, off autopilot. Sometimes doing what everyone else is doing is the right decision, and that's fine, as long as she's chosen it consciously.

She's often been asked whether she worries she'll regret her choices, like not having children. She's come to see regret as a choice, and a waste of energy, because you can't change the past and you don't know what would have happened otherwise:

> "I want to make the best decisions at the time based on what I know and what feels right … then just make the most out of whatever happens"

Her advice for anyone thinking about full-time house sitting, vanlife or doing things their own way:

* **Start small.** Do what she did by accident: say you're going to try it for a month or a summer. It makes it less scary and more achievable, for you and for the people around you.
* **Back yourself.** Only you know what will make you happy, and the happiest life for the people around you might not be that for you. Trust your own instincts.
* **Put yourself out there.** Follow your own path and you'll meet like-minded people with similar values and priorities, and all of a sudden you don't feel like the odd one out anymore.

I loved that. Happiness looks different for everybody, so chase what makes you happy.
