I'll be upfront: I had ulterior motives for this one. Joel has sea kayaked around the North Island, solo and self-supported, and with our own [expedition](https://abigailhannah.nz/162-we-are-kayaking-from-cape-reinga-to-tauranga-to-raise-20-000-/) coming up, I wanted to pick his brain about everything.

Joel has been an outdoor instructor and guide for 10 years. He spends winters in Christchurch training people to climb mountains, works in Queenstown and Wānaka teaching other instructors to get qualified, and dives and guides a bit when he's in Auckland.

His trip took 122 days and covered 3,036 kilometres by his GPS. And one of his stories had me promising, on air, that I will not get to day three without water (I'll explain).

So this post covers how he got into the outdoors, the route and daily distances, how he decided when to paddle, the dehydration that nearly ended it, his best moments, eating raw fish like Gollum (his words), his safety kit, and how to get started in sea kayaking yourself.

## From horse riding to Hillary Outdoors {#joel-story}

Joel wouldn't call his family adventurous. They'd never been on a hike or a kayak together. His way into the outdoors was horse riding as a kid, and that's where he did his first abseil.

He was all set to study music theory and songwriting with the guitar at university in Auckland, when someone at home pointed out that he seemed to like the outdoors, so why not study that? About three days before uni started, a letter arrived to say he'd been accepted into what was then OPC, now the Hillary Outdoors diploma. He shifted his whole life to the little town of Tūrangi, and at 19 he fell in love with the outdoors and a whole new world.

He's really glad he changed.

I love a side quest like that, and the centre in Tūrangi, with the river and the mountains right there, is an awesome spot.

## Why sea kayak around the North Island {#why-north-island}

Joel got into sea kayaking at uni, with two weeks paddling around [Great Barrier Island](https://abigailhannah.nz/142-tramping-and-exploring-aotea-great-barrier-island-with-kristina-montgomerie/). When he finished, he got a job kayak guiding in Fiordland, and he did solo trips around the [Fiordland coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/110-a-sea-kayaking-expedition-along-the-fiordland-coast-with-mark-jones/) to explore his own challenges.

There he met a colleague who'd done some big paddling expeditions. He asked her what she'd never do again, and she said the West Coast of the North Island. So of course he decided he was going to do that.

He sat on the idea until a family friend became very ill, which spurred him to make the trip a fundraiser for that family. The encouragement from home was to do it now, before life took over.

Why the hardest section? Ego, for one, he says. As a young guy he'd never been allowed to see how far he could go, and he'd loved his solo trips down rivers and across coastlines, with space to think and no objective other than being with himself.

### A purpose that changed along the way

That purpose changed as the trip went on. When things got tough, he'd reflect on the people he was doing it for and what he'd want in their place. As he puts it:

> "I don't have to change the world. I don't have to fix every issue. It's not my responsibility to be the hero, but I would love to put some energy into helping something that's important."

When you feel like you're doing something important, he reckons, your mental health comes up too, because you feel there's value in what you're doing.

## Training and gear prep for a sea kayak expedition {#prep-and-gear}

Joel admits he did very little training, because he didn't really know what training was. What he did do:

* **Surf with a loaded boat.** He knew the surf would be heavy, so he loaded his kayak up and took it into the surf to see whether he could steer it at all, using his whitewater background.
* **Distance tests.** Two sessions to find out how far he could paddle before he got sore.
* **Gear from suppliers.** A lot of emailing suppliers to ask for gear, then testing it all.
* **Rescues and the small stuff.** Getting efficient at rescues, and checking things like whether his gas fitted his cooker.

### What he missed

He didn't plan for how much water would get into his kayak. A bit of water on a quick surf is one thing. Ten hours a day on the water is another: the boat would often fill up, adding weight and soaking his gear. By the end of the trip a lot of things had broken, including his cooker, and with nowhere to buy another, he ate raw fish.

### The Skua and a leaky hatch

I'll be paddling a Skua (a pink one!), and Joel paddled one too. His tip: on a Mission Skua, the rubber slap-on front hatch cover leaks quite badly, so check it before you leave and get a better-fitting system if you can.

## The route: 122 days and 3,036 kilometres {#the-route}

Joel's trip took 122 days, with 67 paddle days. He started at Mission Bay in Auckland because it has a cool name, paddled down towards Wellington, up the West Coast and around Cape Reinga, then back down to Auckland. His GPS says 3,036 kilometres. He paddled from the end of spring into summer, when the days are long.

His distances grew as he went:

* **Auckland to Tauranga:** about 30 kilometres a day, while his body got used to the long hours. He ditched some fresh food and planned where to fill up with water instead of carrying it all.
* **After Wellington:** about 50 kilometres on a full day.
* **The West Coast:** averaging 80 kilometres a day. Weather windows are rare, so when one opens you go. No lunch stops, constant snacking, on the water at first light and off just before dark. He calls it a pretty brutal section of coast.

### A near miss with a tanker

Joel only paddled in the dark a couple of times. The first was near Tauranga, where he'd never been, even on land. Birds and fish were hitting him as they flew at his light. Then he saw what he thought was the beautiful city, until a boat flew up to ask what he was doing. That wasn't the city. It was a tanker ship, and it almost ran him over.

It was a big wake-up call. Ships like that move fast and probably can't see you, and couldn't turn in time if they did. After that he avoided harbour crossings and busy water in the dark. He reckons the boat only saw him because of the lights on his kayak.

## Choosing paddle days and reading the forecast {#paddle-days}

What made a day a paddle day changed over the trip, as he learned what his boat did and how he felt in it. His criteria:

* **Wind strength.** Paddling into a 20-knot headwind, he wouldn't go anywhere, so he'd wait and save his energy.
* **Wind direction.** Is it helping or fighting him?
* **Swell versus waves.**
* **The coastline.** Taranaki has big hidden boulders and cliffs, so landing a fully loaded kayak is much harder and more dangerous. From Muriwai to Cape Reinga the swell and waves can be massive, but there are fewer rocks, so he was more confident he wouldn't hit something underwater.

### Don't trust the forecast alone

I asked how accurate swell forecasts are, because paddling in Kaikōura a forecast of 1.1 metres looked a lot bigger to me. Joel doesn't think they're very accurate. He likes Windy for swell, and you still have to trust yourself. Plenty of times the forecast said two-metre swell and 40 knots and he found 10 knots and a metre: it felt easy, the coastline was safe to retreat to, so he went. Other times a forecast metre was giant.

His tip is to build your own gauge before you leave. Go out and look: is it head height? That's probably about a metre. Double that, and it's two metres. And plan for shelter, because there's nothing like a big day at sea, then coming around the corner to 20-centimetre waves.

On our last big training trip, in [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/) over New Year, I wasn't comfortable after day one, so we walked out to Mārahau and came back for the boats later. It's such a hard balance in training between pushing yourself and calling it.

On fitness, Joel finds consistency works better for him than big days: 10 kilometres a day, as often as he can. When it came to the trip, his body was much more ready for the bigger days.

## Paddling self-supported: water, kindness and technique {#self-supported}

Joel paddled solo and self-supported, sleeping mostly on beaches and in secluded coves. The challenges depend on your style: are you happy to pause in a town for supplies, or do you want to keep going?

* **Water.** His biggest challenge. Water is so heavy, so it's about knowing where the next campsite is, or having the confidence to ask a boatie or a house. Sometimes there's no house for a few days' paddle.
* **People.** The generosity of Kiwis was amazing, and people were so invested in his story. Most people will treat you kindly.
* **A hand line.** He highly recommends one for catching a kahawai.
* **Chafe.** You're wet and salty all day, so think about how you'll stay clean.
* **Technique.** Over a long trip, it doesn't take many days of bad technique to damage your shoulders or arms.
* **A sore bum.** It drags down your spirits and your paddling. Spend time sitting in your kayak before a big trip and work out where you need support. It took Joel a while to sort his, and it changed his trip.

## Dehydration between Māhia and Napier {#dehydration}

Joel had a lot of hairy moments, which he thinks is bound to happen on a trip like that. The hairiest was dehydration.

He'd slept on a rocky shelf on the Māhia Peninsula with no water. The next evening he landed on a steep, rocky surf beach around nine o'clock, and as he got out, his kayak slid down the beach and a wave threw it back over him at about head height. He jumped down so it wouldn't crush him against anything, and lost his sun hat, sunglasses, shoes and water. He grabbed the boat on its second round and spent the night wet and defeated, having had about a litre all day.

He paddled 50 kilometres to a creek on the map: completely dry. Then another 50 to a river full of dead cows. He should have used his radio, he says. By day three without water he was probably a bit delirious, kayaking naked, when a crayfisherman stopped beside him and gave him some water and two crayfish. A friend picked him up and looked after him until he recovered, and he got a checkup.

He thinks that crayfisherman is the reason he was okay. His lesson:

> "So that was a moment for me as a young guy to reflect on, actually just get help."

Being young, he adapted to the fear and made reasonably safe calls. At 40, with a better understanding of risk, there are days he wouldn't have gone out.

His fix for drinking on the water: a CamelBak, with the hose in a purpose-built flap on his PFD. When it's windy or the waves are big, you can't put your paddle down to grab a bottle.

I'm terrible at drinking water at the best of times, so this one hit home. I will not get to day three without water.

## Best moments: a baby dolphin, Raglan and Cape Reinga {#best-moments}

Joel's top three:

* **A baby dolphin.** A pod around his kayak is pretty normal on a trip like that, but this one had a tiny baby, so new and so young, surfing the front of his kayak with its mum for a good half hour.
* **Raglan.** He came into Manu Bay and slept in a public changing room, because camping wasn't allowed (full dirtbag). The next day a man pulled up in a van and asked if he was the kayaker: no van, no surfboard, so he must be. He took Joel home, gave him his son's room, fed him and housed him for seven days, and introduced him to all the locals. No questions asked.
* **Rounding Cape Reinga.** He still had about 700 kilometres to go, and he knew he'd survived. His stress had been really high, to the point of dreaming about getting beaten up by waves and drowning. Then suddenly he was safe.

On the dolphins:

> "there was no sound, no wind, just the tiny little blow from this tiny dolphin and that kind of connection that trust."

His big regret is not leaving enough time. Two friends gave up a week of annual leave each to cover his work so he could keep paddling, and he had to keep moving down the coast from Cape Reinga to Auckland when he wanted another month there for the rock gardening, the beaches and the people.

That's exactly why we're giving ourselves six to eight weeks. I saw a post that said fastest known time is out and slowest known time is in, and I'm here for it: I want time for rock gardening, caves, and the dolphins if they show up.

## Food, fishing and spearfishing on the water {#food-and-fishing}

The first two weeks, Joel carried a lot of fresh food. It was good, and he regretted it, because it was heavy and hard to prep alone. In a team, one person can cook while the other sets up camp.

His usual setup:

* **About three days of fresh food.**
* **Five days of dehydrated meals** as backup. He tried to eat fish instead where he could, because too many dehydrated meals make you feel bad.
* **Fish, mostly raw.** He caught fish and ate it as sashimi. People whose homes he stayed in would inevitably feed him too.

From Cape Reinga to Auckland he ate fish almost every day. He'd lost his filleting knife and his normal knife, so he'd bite into them raw like Gollum, crouched on the beach while tourists and locals stared.

He also [spearfished](https://abigailhannah.nz/107-learning-to-freedive-spearfish-and-building-community-with-renee-taylor/) from Cape Reinga to Auckland. Around [Wellington](https://abigailhannah.nz/181-scuba-diving-in-wellington-and-motorbikes-with-tyla-bow/), the pāua were knee deep in places, which blew his mind after growing up diving deep for them around Auckland. His picks for anyone not used to fishy flavours:

* **Cook's turban.** Shells you can collect, easy to cook, quite beefy, and really good in pasta.
* **Pāua** in a creamy pasta.
* **Kahawai** in a curry, in chunks, which hides a lot of the fishy flavour.

Diving and eating what he caught was good for his mental health: calm, a connection with nature and an appreciation for where he lives. He encourages people to try the species that don't get much pressure, like cook's turban or kina, beyond the usual snapper.

I grew up in India two days from the coast, so I'm not a fish person, and this trip is going to challenge me. Friends recently made kahawai ceviche and I thought, okay, I might survive.

## Sea kayak safety and how to get started {#safety-and-starting}

### Joel's safety kit

* **A** [**PLB**](https://abigailhannah.nz/plb-safety/)**, on you.** You can carry an EPIRB at sea, which is designed for a boat, and Joel uses a PLB. It has to be on your body: if it's in the kayak or a dry bag, you can drift away from it.
* **An inReach**, for messages, forecasts when there's no other way to check, tracking, and checking in with home at least once a week. Before a dangerous section, he'd let someone know that if he wasn't in by a certain time, they should use the tracking and send help.
* **A VHF radio**, to talk to boats and check they'd seen him, and to get a decent marine forecast from Coast Guard.
* **Rescue skills** with and without a paddle float, practised in a heavy, loaded kayak, including paddling a boat full of water.
* **A light.** A head torch with a long battery life, for night fishing or coming in at dusk, and around busy spots like the Bay of Islands.
* **Local knowledge.** He'd often land at surf lifesaving clubs for a yarn, a safe spot for his kayak and the lowdown on the next section of coast, the currents and dive spots.

### Getting into sea kayaking

If [sea kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/114-sea-kayaking-for-beginners-and-the-best-of-the-hauraki-gulf-with-bennett-lim/) sounds cool, Joel's tips:

* **Join a kayak group.** Most areas have one. They're often mainly older people, and if that's intimidating, take a friend, because they're usually a cool community.
* **Join the NZOIA mailing list** to be a client on a Sea Kayak 1 or Sea Kayak 2 assessment and get some free instruction.
* **Plan a first overnight** using other people's write-ups. Pick a harbour where, if you get blown off course, you'll be blown to another beach.
* **Go when it's good.** There are so many weather windows where even a novice can have a really good time on the Waitematā Harbour.

I love the Waitematā, and Tiritiri Matangi is a favourite we keep going back to. Clubs are also great for borrowing gear while you learn, because sea kayaking can be a big investment up front, and some have started running Wahine on Water, a women-only course. Or tap someone with a sea kayak on the shoulder and ask to come along.

### What's next for Joel

The winter before we recorded, he took 162 people into the Southern Alps over 30 days, to 15 peaks. Next, he's thinking about biking around Japan and doing some [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/). He's happy to hear from anyone with questions: part of his mission is getting out with people and giving back.
