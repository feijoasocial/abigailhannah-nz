Billy was my guest for [episode one](https://abigailhannah.nz/001-helicopter-over-aoraki-with-billy-nunweek/), so having him back for episode 101 felt really really right. And this time we recorded in person, which we don't do often (cue some very silly banter before we got going).

Billy is a landscape photographer based in Auckland, and he's passionate about nature, camping and four-wheel driving. He's also brilliant at bringing people together, because he's always the one asking what everyone's doing this weekend. Usually in my DMs.

So we catch up on the camera gear that got him started, the Land Cruiser that changed his life, and a month alone in the Far North that got him creating again. Then he tells me the full story of the Rakaia Valley trip, which I hadn't heard yet: boulders, 200k gusts and a very hard call on day four. And we finish on campfire friendships and a whole summer of nothing to do in the South Island.

## How landscape photography started for Billy {#billy-story}

Like most kids in the early 90s, Billy loved playing outside and stayed out until the lights came on, though he was never outdoorsy the way he is now.

The passion for landscapes started with a flight over Mount Everest, which is when he remembers falling in love with the mountains. It didn't cement itself until he moved to Christchurch in 2015. Driving through the Port Hills, he felt like he needed a way to bottle what he was seeing, and photography turned out to be the creative way to do it.

He remembers going to Castle Point for sunrise with a GoPro, because his phone wasn't doing it. Neither was the GoPro. So he invested in a Canon 750D, his very first digital camera, and never looked back. He started driving around more of Canterbury for sunsets and the odd sunrise.

What really captivated him back then was the night sky. Capturing the stars the way he'd seen others do it was one of the big reasons he bought a DSLR. These days [astrophotography](https://abigailhannah.nz/027-astrophotography-larryn-rae/) doesn't captivate him the same way traditional landscapes do, and it's still there for him to fall back on from time to time.

His friend Larryn, an incredible astrophotographer, was a stranger who rolled up on a road trip. They've since travelled the world together, including to Iceland for the northern lights (I said no Iceland this episode, and he got it in anyway).

## Camera gear, and why it isn't everything {#camera-gear}

Billy quickly outgrew the 750D and moved to a Canon 6D, which he ran for quite some time. When the pandemic hit, he invested in the Canon R and has been running mirrorless ever since, still with Canon and still loving it. When we recorded, he was about to try out a Fujifilm X-T50 for the weekend.

Billy knows a lot of beginners who want to jump straight into full frame and mirrorless and spend over $5,000, because everyone thinks they need the gear. His take: you'll grow out of your first system if you're passionate, and you don't know its limits until you hit them.

He only left his 6D behind because it struggled to focus with some third-party lenses and he was missing shots. It's still his time-lapse workhorse, with technology more than a decade old.

The thing that changed most for him was meeting a French photographer who introduced him to prime lenses. His all-time favourite is the 24mm f/1.4, and he uses it in ways it isn't meant to be used. He's untraditional, and he loves it.

Isaac and I started with a GoPro too. Our second camera was a Canon 1300D, and while pulling together my guide to New Zealand, I've been looking back at photos from the summer of 2021 on that entry-level camera with a kit lens. Some of them are still my favourite photos I've ever taken. It's not about the gear.

## Four-wheel driving and Kaya the Land Cruiser {#kaya-land-cruiser}

Four-wheel driving is the other big passion, and Billy's name came up heaps when I was chatting with [Jack](https://abigailhannah.nz/overlanding-nz/).

It started when Billy and a flatmate, also a landscape photographer, took a Trekker Adventures Land Rover Defender over to the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/). Some of the photos he took on that trip led to work with Trekker, and he developed a passion for taking vehicles to places his feet couldn't or wouldn't get to. Then the camping followed: drive to a spot, stay the night, capture first light.

He'd always kept an eye out for a Land Cruiser, and a conversation about wanting a 70 series sent him on the hunt. He opened Trade Me one day, and the first truck that popped up was Kaya. He bought her on 29 May 2021, so that's her birthday. She's a 1994 Land Cruiser Prado, a KZJ78.

Since then, Billy says, his life has changed drastically. He spends a lot more time away and travels at least every second weekend. He's picked up some incredible photography clients, and he convinced one of his best friends, Jack, to start a business outfitting vehicles (then convinced him to outfit Kaya). He's in a place he never imagined: essentially getting paid to travel around the most beautiful country in the world in his Land Cruiser, taking photographs.

## A solo month in the Far North {#far-north-solo}

Billy does both solo trips and trips with friends. Trips with friends come easily: they're easy to organise and almost a guaranteed good time. And he's learned the most about himself on his own:

> "on solo trips … you do a lot more soul searching. … You find out a lot about yourself."

He spent the whole of December 2023 on a solo trip up in the [Far North](https://abigailhannah.nz/176-a-locals-guide-to-northland-and-building-hiking-communities-with-hannah-whittaker/), and his life hasn't been the same since, very much for the better. He's changed a lot in the last year, and that trip was a pivotal part of it.

Part of it was the unknown, like taking the truck onto beaches without knowing what the sand would be like. The moments that matter most to Billy are when you stop, and have time to sit and think.

What he found was that he was yearning to create. He'd gone months without wanting to pick up his camera, even on incredible trips. In the Far North, he was desperate to get it out: watching the light, waiting for it to get better, scoping a shot for hours.

He came back thinking everything was fixed, then went through another rut that lasted five months. He remembers trying to force it at one spot on a windy day with bad light, and having a cry in the driver's seat of his truck. Less than a week later he was in the exact same spot, losing his mind over the conditions, and took some of his favourite photos from that area. As soon as the pressure was off, the flow came back.

To me, that's such a good reflection of life too. You hit periods where you're stuck, then you come out of it on a high, and sometimes you hit it again.

## First hikes, running and the Tongariro Crossing {#hiking-and-running}

Isaac and I joined Billy for a couple of days in the Far North, and we walked Duke's Nose together. There's a video of me asking him if he still hated me.

It was Billy's first real adult hike. He has lost over 60 kilos, and he was about 30 kilos into that at Duke's Nose, so it was challenging, and some words were said. Looking back, he doesn't hate me anymore.

Not long after, he came out to the Coromandel while I was doing my Everest summit challenge, and he was racing up the mountain. I'd brought my hiking poles just to keep up with him.

Since Duke's Nose, Billy has picked up running. He's done up to 10K and signed up for a 14K, and he did the [Tongariro Crossing](https://abigailhannah.nz/172-hiking-the-tongariro-alpine-crossing-and-tongariro-northern-circuit-with-ben-boland/) and Tama Lakes in the same weekend, running down both times. Being overweight definitely restricts you, he says, and having the shackles taken off has been a drastic change. He's now planning his next trips around hiking more than four-wheel driving, which is why he was trialling the Fujifilm: to find a lighter hiking camera.

## Four-wheel driving the Rakaia Valley {#rakaia-valley}

The Rakaia Valley was the last piece of a puzzle left unfinished when Billy and the crew drove the Godley and Havelock valleys together. (Jack's episode covers the lead-up, so Billy skipped it.) After filming that first trip, the Rakaia was the natural next part of the project.

The Rakaia sits right on the boundary of the Adams Wilderness Area, so it's remote in the truest sense. Billy describes a wilderness area as a reflection of what the world would be like if people didn't exist, and the valley is pristine. The goal was to make their way up the entire valley.

### Into the boulders

They went in September, a year after the first film, setting off from Christchurch in a convoy of trucks with friends, a videographer and a very beautiful dog. They started at the Ashburton Lakes and made their way into the valley. Coming out of Lake Heron Station onto the riverbed, it felt a lot like the Godley and Havelock, and very achievable.

Then they came around the corner into the Rakaia, and went from head-sized rocks to torso-sized boulders. Billy never thought he'd be trying to escape a boulder field by driving through matagouri, and that's what they did. He ripped out his handbrake cable, and it wasn't great for any of the trucks. The scale of the place, he says, can't really be shown on a map. The sheer size of the rocks was the problem: they couldn't be moved, and they rocked you about. Billy credits two of the crew with getting them as far as they did.

The weather gods were on their side for the first couple of days, and they made it up to Reischek Hut. On a recce the next day, they went as far up the southern bank as they could, and it became very clear it wasn't passable. Just on twilight, they went back a bit, and found a point where they could cross the river.

### The storm and the call on day four

They'd been out of reception for four days, and anyone who knows the mountains in New Zealand knows you're lucky if the third day of the forecast is right. They knew a weather cell was coming and that it would be wet. They never anticipated how wet. They were being hit by 200k gusts, and Billy has photos of a friend giving him the thumbs up while walking into the wind, then turning his back to shelter from it.

They got the trucks across the river, with Kaya the only one needing a hand, and it became so apparent that what they were trying to do was much bigger than anyone had anticipated. The radio at the hut wasn't working, and not knowing what the weather would do made them unwilling to take any unnecessary risks. So, much to Billy's dismay, they turned around on the fourth day. To do it properly, they reckon you need 10.

Getting back out is the key thing. They carried more reserves and fuel this time, and where the Havelock had St Winifred's Hut, the wilderness area has no shelters: it would have been them and their trucks.

They made it back to Reischek Hut and made the call. In typical mountain fashion, the next morning was beautiful. After they got out, the weather system that hit where they'd been trying to get to brought 500 millimetres of rain up the Rakaia, more than they'd already struggled in. Billy believes it could have killed them, so they count their blessings. As one of the crew likes to say, everything happens for a reason.

### Damage, and going back

On the way out, Billy dragged his truck back through one river and did quite significant damage to the exhaust, fuel tank and transmission. Lesson learnt.

They'll definitely go back, with a better understanding of what's ahead. Next time it'll be two weeks for the Rakaia alone, with four days of contingency built in. Jack has seen a lot and made it to a lot of places, and the Rakaia is one that has evaded him.

## Campfires, friends and community {#campfire-community}

What Billy loves about trips with friends is that there's usually no reception, and it's a really good chance to get to know people. He met me around a campfire, and he's got to know Isaac and me better and better around one. We've probably gone camping together more than we've hung out in a house.

Out there, he says, you can't pretend:

> "I just think there are no walls. There's no facade. You can't hide who you truly are when you're in these places."

Most of his nearest and dearest, he's spent time with around a campfire. It's never been about what gear you've got: Kaya is kitted out so he can look after everyone, cook for people and make sure there are beers to go round. Quality time is his love language.

It's mine too. After four days of dirt, the only thing that changes is how dishevelled you look, and when we've crossed paths on month-long road trips, Billy and those friends feel like an element of home.

Community is something Billy does really, really well. On one of our early camping trips with him, he put a message on his stories asking who was around for a weekend away, and eight trucks turned up, including people he'd never met who are now close friends. We're so bad at taking that initiative in Kiwi culture, and I admire it in him as a friend.

## A South Island summer, from Cobb Reservoir to Aoraki {#south-island-summer}

When we recorded, Billy was finishing his work contract on 8 November and had a one-way ferry ticket south on 15 November, with nowhere to be and nothing to do until the middle of March. The plan is to tiki tour the South Island, doing the hikes he always imagined and going to places that have only ever been a picture on a map.

One that keeps sticking in his mind is Cobb Reservoir in [Kahurangi National Park](https://abigailhannah.nz/165-an-18-day-mission-through-nelson-lakes-kahurangi-and-abel-tasman-with-the-national-park-trifecta/), with several hikes, a bit of four-wheel driving and a lot of photography. It's very different to where he's spent most of his time, a bit more unknown and uncovered, and there's a rock bivvy you can camp under.

We were up there this summer and walked to Lake Sylvester, staying overnight to shoot sunset and sunrise. The scenery up on the Tablelands was completely different.

He also wants to spend time with people who've only been a name behind a screen so far. In his words:

> "I'm excited to have nothing to do and everything to see."

And he wants to reconnect with favourite places. He hadn't been back to [Aoraki Mount Cook](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) for over a year, which seemed ridiculous to him, because he used to go at least every six months when he lived in the South Island. Some of his best memories are there, as he shared in episode one, and he was heading back that weekend with a mate.
