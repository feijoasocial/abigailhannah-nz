I'm the biggest advocate of finding adventure close to home. So when Lauren picked her own backyard to talk about, I was in. And she'd been up there that very afternoon, right before we jumped on the call (perfect timing).

Lauren is originally from Canada and has lived in Methven, in Mid Canterbury, for most of her time in New Zealand. She hikes, she runs, she takes photos, and she's fallen in love with the mountains here. Mount Somers is pretty much in her backyard.

So we go through the Mount Somers Walkway: the tracks, the huts, Woolshed Creek and the times the mountain has pushed her. Then trail running, from her first race to her tips for making the switch from road. And the tramping logbooks she makes, where she finds track info, and her advice for anyone starting out. By the end she'd realised how passionate she is about the place, and so had I.

## From the Canadian prairies to Methven {#lauren-story}

Lauren grew up in a small town in Manitoba, right in the middle of Canada: flat prairie land. Her family spent a lot of time at the lake, tenting and later in a travel trailer, so she walked the trails, biked, water skied and wakeboarded. Outdoorsy, in a different way to now.

She moved to New Zealand in 2016 and has been in Methven for most of that time. A lot of people know it better as the town near the Mt Hutt ski field. It's in Mid Canterbury, inland about an hour from Christchurch, with a handful of mountains around, lots of hiking and rivers. If you like the outdoors, it's a good place to be.

To Lauren, New Zealand is like a miniature version of Canada. She thinks nothing beats the Canadian Rockies, though [Mount Cook](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) is amazing too.

Her day job is as a team leader at the hot pools in Methven. She works 10 hour days, three or four days a week, which leaves her three or sometimes four days off for everything else she wants to do.

## Mount Somers Walkway tracks and huts {#mount-somers-walkway}

There's a town called Mount Somers and a mountain called Mount Somers, and the tracks around it are known locally as the Mount Somers Walkway. It's pretty much right in Lauren's backyard, and she's there often, picking a track depending on how she feels that day.

Why she loves it so much: the variety. There are:

* **Beginner-friendly and family-friendly tracks**, if you're starting your hiking journey.
* **Bush tracks**, technical rocky tracks and a 4WD track.
* **The gnarly grunt up Mount Somers** itself, a very technical track.

The afternoon we recorded, she didn't want anything too hefty, so she walked a little way in, sat on a rock with some snacks, then turned around and came home.

### Pinnacles Hut

There are two huts on the walkway. One is Pinnacles Hut (easy to confuse with the Pinnacles in the North Island), and there are pinnacles in the mountain too. That track was Lauren's first ever overnight in a hut, pretty soon after she moved here, and doing it so close to home made it a very cool experience.

### Woolshed Creek

Woolshed Creek is the track Lauren does most. If you hike a lot, it's pretty quick, and you don't even have to go all the way to the hut: you go up to the top of the ridge, then back down to the hut, and the views at the top are really good. There are a few ways to do it as a loop, including an easier loop, and one that goes back on itself, which adds an extra challenge and different views.

When we were in the South Island in September, we drove past Mount Somers on our way into Hakatere Conservation Park, and tossed up doing Woolshed Creek. It was a bit wet, the weather looked like it was coming in and we weren't sure we'd squeeze it in, so it's on the list for next time.

### When Mount Somers gets tough

Lauren's first time up Mount Somers was definitely a challenge, getting off the top on technical, slippery rocks that were new to her then. She's been up when it's really windy too, which you don't realise until you get out of the trees. She does not like hiking in the wind.

## Trail running and her first race {#first-trail-race}

Lauren has always been a runner and has done a lot of road runs and road events. Her first trail race was the Mission Mount Somers half marathon, which will always hold a special place in her heart because she loves that area so much.

She wanted a change from road running, and found the reason it clicked:

> "trail running for me mixes my love for hiking and running"

She doesn't run much up the hills, and even power walking them used to sound crazy to her. Now it's about seeing what her body and mind can do.

Her goal for that race was to start and finish. She trained on the track, knew she could finish, and decided she didn't care about her time: if she had to stop or walk, that was fine. She's racing against herself, and every time she crosses a finish line she feels like an Olympic athlete (just don't look at her times).

Watching other people motivates her too. At the Coast to Coast in February, seeing what the human body can do had her asking why she couldn't.

When I did my first event, the 11 km at the Auckland Marathon a couple of weekends before we recorded, I'd done minimal training and it was a walk-run. My incentive was walking across the Harbour Bridge, which you hardly ever get to do. Lauren's take:

> "we can always do more than what we think and definitely with running a lot of it is mental"

## Switching from road to trail running {#road-to-trail}

For Lauren, the big difference is elevation. She walks up hills and is still learning to run them. Her tips:

* **Start somewhere**, even if it's a run through the trees and nowhere hilly.
* **Take the pressure off.** You don't have to run the same pace or run fast.
* **Pick up your feet.** As a self-confessed lazy road runner, she's learnt to lift them over tree roots and rocks.
* **Walk, run, walk.** Do what your body feels it needs, then build on it.

I drag my feet so much when I hike that my husband is always asking why my shoes are so scuffed.

The mental side was the fear of the elevation, for a prairie girl who'd only run flat surfaces. In the two weeks before the race she wondered whether she could make it. In training she split it up into thirds, and on the day, she got to each point and kept going. Once she was in, she forgot about those hurdles, because once you're in, you've got to get out somehow. Nobody can pick you up from the side of the road.

Lauren has a lot of respect for road running too: long-distance road running has its own challenges.

## Tramping logbooks and finding track info {#logbooks-track-info}

Lauren also runs a small e-commerce business from home, making tramping and travel logbooks. It started from her own need. When she moved to New Zealand she wasn't much of a hiker, starting out was very difficult, and she wanted a way to track her hikes and see how far she'd come.

She sat on the idea for years. When COVID came, she told herself to either do it now or let the idea go. She loves stationery, so the logbook is fill-in-the-blank, with each entry recording the same stats. There are no track details, so it works anywhere in New Zealand. She posts online too, and she likes having something tangible to flip back through.

I need one. Someone asks me about a hike and all I remember is "three hours", with no idea whether that's return or one way.

For track info, Lauren reckons there's a lot more out there now than when she started. Her go-tos:

* **Other people's blogs and trip reports**, including her own for Mount Somers.
* **DOC**, which lists all its hikes, though sometimes you don't quite know what to expect from the descriptions.
* **Social media**, searching it and messaging people to ask around.

## What's next and advice for starting out {#whats-next}

Lauren has a huge bucket list written down. Top of it is the [Paparoa Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/168-from-first-tramp-to-paparoa-and-all-the-great-walks-with-nicola-harvie/), her first [Great Walk](https://abigailhannah.nz/10-great-walks-in-10-days/), which a good friend has locked in for them in February. She'd love to head down to [Fiordland](https://abigailhannah.nz/049-dusky-track-fiordland/) for a hike too. With her focus on running, she's also got it in her head that it would be fun to run the [Routeburn Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/009-solo-travel-routeburn-track-emily/): looking at the elevation and the ridgeline, it looks doable.

When we recorded, she was deliberating over the Valley Ultra in the Craigieburn area, deciding which distance to do.

Her advice for anyone new to hiking, trail running or New Zealand is to be open to trying new things. When she came here she wasn't a hiker at all: she used to show jump horses competitively. Then:

* **Pick an easier track** if you're starting out.
* **Always tell someone where you're going.**
* **Connect with people** who like hiking or running. She's a little more introverted and has done a lot on her own, and it can be nice to have people to go with. Don't be scared to put yourself out there, and get on the trails.

Those communities are out there if you look for them. Facebook and Instagram are great places to find groups, and most of the major universities have [tramping clubs](https://abigailhannah.nz/180-hiking-the-cascade-saddle-route-tramping-clubs-and-influencers-with-alice-milne/). As Lauren says, put your big girl pants on.
