I'd been following Grace on socials for a little while before this chat, and she did not disappoint. She describes herself as a slightly hippie ADHD adventure princess slash country cottage girly, and I need that on a T-shirt.

Grace grew up in Wellington, spent a year living and working from a campervan, and now lives in the Canterbury countryside with her Land Cruiser, the buggy. She surfs, spearfishes, hunts, tramps and four-wheel drives, mostly solo, and she's a spoonie, so she's very careful about what her body can and can't do on any given day.

So we talk about her favourite adventures, how a childhood of ballet and shooting led to representing New Zealand, what hunting looks like for her (I had a lot of beginner questions), and how she juggles so many passions. Plus her lessons from getting it wrong solo, and a few four-wheel drive tracks to try.

## Ballet, bullets and a campervan {#grace-story}

Grace calls her childhood ballet and bullets. She did ballet, started shooting at five, and fitted in a bit of horse riding and hunting too. Her real passion for the outdoors came later in life, as she realised how short and precious life is:

> "I've realized how short life is and how precious life is and that is my main motivator as to why I so passionately pursue adventures and the outdoors now"

Grace grew up in Wellington, which she calls the concrete jungle. She was working full-time in tech and trying to smoosh more adventure into her life. She nearly bought a sailing boat, then realised she didn't know how to sail yet. So she bought a [campervan](https://abigailhannah.nz/121-avoiding-vanlife-mistakes-and-life-on-the-road-in-nz-with-tiff-and-nathan/) instead and travelled New Zealand full-time for 12 months, working remotely with Starlink. Touring the South Island showed her what she was missing, and she now lives in the [Canterbury](https://abigailhannah.nz/141-best-christchurch-and-canterbury-adventures-with-kids-with-jasmine-trafford/) countryside, close to the mountains and the ocean, with horses out the back and room for her truck.

That truck is a 2015 Toyota Land Cruiser Prado, aka the buggy, and her first. Before it, she pushed sedans and station wagons places they shouldn't go and got them stuck. Still, she's passionate about this:

> "You don't have to have a rooftop tent. You don't have to have all of these things to be able to go out on adventure. You can literally get on a bus, go and take a picnic and sit at the bottom of a hill or something."

We used to go camping out of our station wagon, and [our first van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/) was a tiny little box. We were scraping ice off the inside of the windows in South Island winters. You work your way up, one little stepping stone at a time.

## Favourite adventures, from the Kepler Track to Skippers Canyon {#favourite-adventures}

Grace doesn't have one favourite adventure: every one teaches you something. Over summer she did a five-week road trip from Christchurch all the way up to [Northland](https://abigailhannah.nz/176-a-locals-guide-to-northland-and-building-hiking-communities-with-hannah-whittaker/) and back. She took the back roads as much as she could, met new people, [surfed](https://abigailhannah.nz/learn-to-surf-new-zealand/) and [spearfished](https://abigailhannah.nz/147-an-ocean-obsession-great-white-encounter-and-spearfishing-with-laura-kuggeleijn/). With ADHD, there's no real routine (she leans into what she calls adventure plot twists), but most mornings on that trip she was up at 5am, surfing or doing yoga, then enjoying a really slow coffee, checking the weather and working out what the day would bring. A few more she rates:

* **The** [**Kepler Track**](https://abigailhannah.nz/003-hiking-the-kepler-track-in-fiordland-with-dua/)**.** She had tears in her eyes by the first viewpoint.
* [**Angelus Hut**](https://abigailhannah.nz/039-angelus-hut-dylan-arthur/)**.** Extremely beautiful.
* **Skippers Canyon**, for a driving adventure.

Angelus Hut is one of my key memories too. Spending a night out there was so beautiful.

Most of Grace's adventures are solo, though she links up with friends along the way. She describes herself as a bit like a clam with a really shiny pearl: around her people she's an extrovert and sunbeams come out of her face, but she also needs to shut down and be introverted. Hashtag ambivert life.

## Competitive shooting and coming back to hunting {#competitive-shooting}

Grace learned to shoot at a rifle range when she was five. As a teenager she'd always wanted to try fullbore rifle shooting, which is long-distance target shooting from 300 to 1,000 yards. One day she turned into the local range, and she basically didn't leave for three years.

She ended up representing New Zealand in three teams, shooting in Australia and America. It taught her that you can only control what you can control: the dials on her aperture sights, never the wind. After three weeks straight of shooting in really hot conditions in America, which she found really challenging, she retired from the sport. Part of her wishes she'd taken the challenge on the chin and carried on.

For the decade since, she's hunted on and off. Falling in love with spearfishing is what got her back behind the rifle. She feels a lot of pressure from the hunting community to bring home an animal every weekend and to know everything, so her relationship with [hunting](https://abigailhannah.nz/161-empowering-kiwi-women-in-hunting-fishing-with-wild-chix-isabell-zitzelsberger/) is complicated. It's been part of her story for so long, but it isn't all of who she is. She loves hiking and being in nature just as much, and she's also, in her words, a girl and a princess.

What drives her, on land and in the sea, is food. She wants to be capable and self-sufficient: an organic veggie garden, venison when she wants it, beautiful fish from the sea. Her number one life goal is a massive dining table covered in really beautiful food, and hunting and spearfishing give her ocean to table and mountain to table.

## How Grace approaches hunting {#how-grace-hunts}

Pig hunting isn't for her, because she finds wild pork quite gamey.

When we recorded, the roar had finished, so the female deer would be pregnant. On a private block where the owner wants numbers to grow, you wouldn't target them. On a recent trip to a private block in Waverley, she saw two fallow does and left them for exactly that reason.

Grace only shoots something if she wants to eat it, unless it's an exceptional trophy and she's certain of the distance, the recovery and a shot that's as humane as possible. Most of the time she leaves the animal, and that's okay.

She's planning to give tahr a go during their rut, which was on when we recorded. She did a lot of skeet shooting and a little duck hunting at high school, and this year she got her duck licence to see if it's for her. Just don't expect animals from her every week, or every month.

Her way of thinking about the reaction to hunting: if Martha Stewart prepares a duck on a show, nobody blinks. Show the whole duck and the whole process, and people are a bit shocked. This is her, and people can take it or leave it.

I came into this with a lot of beginner questions. As I've had more adventures and met more hunters, I've been working out what I think about it, and I'm at the point where it might be nice to go along with someone and see what it's like.

## Adventuring with a body that sometimes can't {#spoonie-adventures}

Grace's truck rarely breaks down. She does. She's a spoonie with autoimmune conditions, so she has to be very careful about what she can do and when. You can't judge a book by its cover, she says: some of her micro adventures involve walking 500 metres to pitch her tent for sunrise on a mountain, and that counts as an adventure too.

She's proud to have done three [Great Walks](https://abigailhannah.nz/10-great-walks-in-10-days/): the [Milford](https://abigailhannah.nz/034-milford-track-nz-popular-walk/), the [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/) and the Kepler. They were all long on her body. At the end of the Abel Tasman she was sick, with both ankles strapped, and she had the choice of retracing her steps or looping over the hill for the full experience. People could see her hobbling. Someone told her that if she didn't do the full thing, she wouldn't be okay with it, and that's her to a T. Standing on the Milford Track, she was so proud that despite months when she physically can't, she still puts herself in these situations, because life is too short not to.

Being outdoors is also a non-negotiable for her [mental wellbeing](https://abigailhannah.nz/052-walking-wellbeing/). She's written an article for a four-wheel drive magazine on how getting out into nature supports it. Whether it's less screen time, being with your people, the ocean, the moon or the vitamin D, she thinks the calm of nature helps regulate our nervous systems and gives our brains a reset. For an overthinker like her, it's a moment to just exist.

Grace's take is to audit how you're spending your time, check that's how you want your life to go, and if not, start putting stepping stones in place to change it.

## Being multi-passionate without spreading yourself thin {#multi-passionate}

Grace gets asked this a lot. A couple of years ago she audited everything she likes doing and realised she wasn't going deeper in any of it. So she built a Kanban board, a project management board, with columns for what she's focused on now and an ice box for things that are out of season. She gives a few things her time, energy and brain cells, so she can go further in them.

About six months before we recorded, she mapped out all her activities and decided consciously which get more investment and which get minimal time or no more dollars. You can't do everything well if you spread yourself too thin.

Her other advice is to ignore other people's judgement of what skill level you should be at:

> "i love being a turtle paddling out on my board, being a terrible surfer. And I will love that for the rest of my life"

And it's okay to admit something isn't for you. Grace has tried mountain biking and stand-up paddleboarding, and they're not for her.

I'm taking this straight back to my husband, because between climbing, tramping, sea kayaking and his plans for mountaineering, it's a lot. And the idea that you don't have to be good at it hit home, because feeling like everyone else is so good is part of what's held me back from surfing (that and the cold).

## Solo adventure lessons, learned the hard way {#solo-lessons}

Grace offers herself as a cautionary tale. She's put herself in situations beyond her capability, preparation and gear, and her big mistake is treating plan B as a fine option when it should be a last resort.

Two nights before we recorded, she left way too late, took a wrong turn and had to backtrack, so she ended up walking in the dark. She figured it was fine: a great head torch, she could pitch her tent anywhere, and it wasn't windy. Then it got really windy, it was very dark, and there was nowhere to pitch the tent. She had to draw on years of past mistakes to make a plan to get herself warm and out of the elements safely.

Her tips for going solo:

* **Do your research.** Watch YouTube videos, listen to podcasts and talk to people who've been out there.
* **Visit your local hunting and fishing or outdoor store** for advice on the minimum gear to stay safe, ideas for adventures and what to watch for, like a track to avoid after rain because of a creek crossing.
* **Take your time over decisions.** When she drove to Macaulay Hut solo in her truck, she took a 20 minute mandarin break to decide on her line. Four-wheel driving means constant micro decisions.
* **Pack the night before.** That's her new rule, because packing chews up her morning and then she's dealing with darkness she didn't need to.

Feeling scared is a universal experience, Grace says, even for very experienced adventurers. Feel the fear, do it anyway, and prepare so you don't make unnecessary mistakes.

My first adventure was a solo hike, five days on the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/). Everyone I've asked on the podcast says to start small, and I went full send. I knew my gear, there were plenty of people on the trail and it was well marked, so I was fine. But I probably could have done more mental prep, because there were a lot of tears on the last day.

## Four-wheel drive tracks to try {#4wd-tracks}

A listener asked for [four-wheel drive](https://abigailhannah.nz/128-4wd-adventures-across-the-length-of-the-south-island-with-rich-soult/) tracks. Grace's suggestions:

* **The Nevis**, in the South Island. She hasn't done it yet, but it's on her list.
* **Macaulay**, which is definitely not for beginners, but epic.
* **Skippers Canyon**, Queenstown way, a lot more beginner friendly, with a lot of bang for your buck.
* **Gravel roads from Raglan to South Auckland**, very chill.
* **Rainbow Road**, which is amazing when it's open, whether you're in a four-wheel drive, a car, on a bicycle or a motorbike.
