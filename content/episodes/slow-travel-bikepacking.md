Nicole is a full-time firefighter in Auckland, and a little while before we recorded she'd bought her first ever mountain bike. Then she did one two-day bikepacking trip, and went all in and spent 16 days riding the South Island on her own, on a bike that cost her $400.

And I love that. I'm very much an all-in person too (my first solo hike was five days and 85 kilometres, so I'm in no position to judge). But what got me in this chat was day three. Nicole had a full meltdown on the side of State Highway 63, sat in her tent that night and threw the whole plan out. From then on it was trails, slower days and camping wherever she felt like it. Soooo much more fun.

So in this post: how Nicole got into biking, the Coromandel trip that took some recovering from, the day three reset, Rainbow Road, wild camping, her full route from the Queen Charlotte Track to Cromwell, her favourite trails, and her best advice if you want to give bikepacking a go.

## Nicole's story, from Seattle to firefighting {#nicole-story}

Nicole was born in Seattle and spent her childhood there, then moved to New Zealand when she was eleven. She spent a lot of time outdoors there, in the backyard and learning to ride bikes around the neighbourhood.

In New Zealand, the family moved to the Whangaparāoa Peninsula, just north of Auckland. It's surrounded by beaches, so they were always outside swimming and snorkelling. At school she did every single sport she could get into, and she's been active basically her whole life.

These days Nicole works in Auckland Central as a career firefighter, and when we recorded she'd been doing it full time for six and a half years. Before that she volunteered for three years. She fell into it by accident: she met a volunteer who talked about all the cool stuff she'd learned, and there was a volunteer station down the road. She was already way too busy, with a full-time job and filming weddings on the side, but she gave it whatever time she could, fell in love with it and spent every single weekend there.

It can be physically demanding, especially for women who aren't used to a lot of weightlifting, and she had to train to get in.

## Mountain biking and a first bikepacking trip in Coromandel {#first-bikepacking-trip}

Biking is a recent love. Nicole bought her first [mountain bike](https://abigailhannah.nz/047-mountainbiking-nz-laura-goldsworthy/) in the June of the year before we recorded, and she's obsessed. Through mountain biking she met a few people who'd done bikepacking trips, so she and her best mate decided to ride around the [Coromandel Peninsula](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/) over two days.

It was so hard: type two fun, almost type three. One section from Stony Bay was basically walking through cow paddocks in the rain, so muddy it was unridable, and they pushed their bikes. Then they rode the remaining 50 kilometres of the route. The weather wasn't great either. It's one of those trips that was amazing looking back, while at the time you're wondering what you're doing.

Nicole reckons they possibly could have chosen something easier to start with. She's that type of person, though, who'll give it a go.

I'm the same. When I did [my first solo hike](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/), I went all in with five days and 85 kilometres right off the bat, and I found it extremely challenging. So my advice is to start small and do an overnighter. Two days is better than five.

## Planning a solo South Island bikepacking trip {#planning-the-trip}

After the Coromandel, it took Nicole a little while to recover, emotionally, before she felt ready for another bike trip. She didn't do any more in between. She went straight to the big one.

The idea came from her leave. She'd moved a chunk of it to February and March, with plans at the start and a [canyoning](https://abigailhannah.nz/105-a-multi-day-canyoning-trip-in-abel-tasman-with-aimee-jules/) festival at the end, based at Makarora. That left just over two weeks in the middle, and she wanted to do something exciting with it. More than anything, she wanted space, and to be away on her own.

Her first thought was to ride there from Auckland, but the numbers were too tight. And she loves the South Island so much that she decided to spend the whole time there. Her goal, kept to herself, was to ride from the top of the South Island to the bottom.

## The Queen Charlotte Track and a day three meltdown {#day-three}

Nicole started on the Queen Charlotte Track, and she rates it as an amazing trail for anyone. Her rough plan had it taking about a day. With a fully loaded bike, she didn't get close to finishing it on the first day.

By day three she was a day behind, with a lot of pressure on herself to stick to the route and her time limits. That day she rode along State Highway 63, into a headwind. It looks flat, apparently it's uphill, and it felt like it. She struggled all day, and every car that drove past, she imagined different ways she could get her bike onto it and catch a ride.

Forty kilometres in, she had a little breakdown and a cry on the side of the road. At 60 kilometres she called it a day, set up camp and hid in her tent from the bugs. That was the moment it clicked:

> "I'm out here for the journey … I don't want the pressure I want that slow travel I want that immersive experience of being outdoors and seeing and hearing and smelling things"

She sat in her tent brooding, then changed the whole trip. She'd target every bike trail she could, and go slower.

I feel like day three is a safe bet for that kind of moment. It's also a good wake-up call. Everyone gets out there for different reasons, whether it's hiking or biking or anything in between, so it's important to know why you're there and do what you want to do.

## Rainbow Road to Hanmer Springs {#rainbow-road}

After that, everything fell into place. Once she accepted that some of it would be hard and slow, she enjoyed the challenges a lot more.

Day four took her through Rainbow Road, and it was probably her favourite day of the whole trip. She'd decided to chill, and to camp wherever she felt like it. She was riding really well and felt way more free than she had in the first three days. She ended up riding 125 kilometres.

Not far from Hanmer Springs, she had a really bad bail, came off her bike and hurt herself. Then she came down the hill and saw the lights of Hanmer Springs, and it was one of the best feelings of the trip:

> "I'm actually out here and I'm doing it and this is what it is to be alive"

It felt like she'd been reset, and she'd figured out why she was there.

## Wild camping on a bikepacking trip {#wild-camping}

Nicole would have a rough idea of where to camp, then mostly camp wherever she could find a sneaky spot. She knows some places, like private property, don't want you camping. For her, that's the beauty of bikepacking with a tent: you can say you've had enough, pull over and camp, or keep going.

She sometimes aimed for campsites, but a lot of the time she wanted to be out there alone. A couple of times she splurged on a motel room for a shower and an actual bed, which was really nice.

I find the DOC rules on wild camping so unclear. They basically say you can camp anywhere unless you can't, which doesn't help.

A couple of weeks before we recorded, we were kayaking and had timed things wrong. It was getting dark, we weren't going to make our campsite, and we spotted a beach with a little bach at the end. We knocked on the door and no one answered, so we camped there because we had no choice. When the people staying in the bach came out, we told them we were there and they were fine with it. If you can ask, you probably should.

## Nicole's South Island bikepacking route {#south-island-route}

All up, Nicole rode for 14 days with two rest days, so 16 days with the bike, and she made it to the canyoning festival.

### Marlborough to Christchurch

* **Queen Charlotte Track**, then down through Havelock and Renwick.
* **Rainbow Road** to Hanmer Springs.
* **Hurunui Heartland Ride**, part of it, ending up on the beach at Leithfield.
* **Christchurch**, riding down from Leithfield.

### The Mackenzie and the Alps 2 Ocean

In Christchurch she caught up with a mate and got a ride to Burkes Pass. From there she rode into Lake Tekapo and started the Alps 2 Ocean.

The Alps 2 Ocean was incredible, and her top pick of the whole trip. She had the most amazing weather for three days and could see [Mount Cook](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) from all different angles. One of her favourite places was Lake Ōhau Lodge, the cutest little ski lodge beside Lake Ōhau, with hot tubs on the deck and the best lookout to drink a beer.

### Dunedin to Cromwell

From the Alps 2 Ocean she rode down to [Dunedin](https://abigailhannah.nz/139-slow-travel-in-an-ev-and-catlins-dunedin-and-oamaru-with-mature-flaneur-tim-ward/), caught up with a mate there and took her second rest day with a little tour of the city. Then:

* **Clutha Gold Trail**, from Dunedin through to the Roxburgh Gorge. It has been extended, and it has a couple of really cool tunnels that are hectic to ride through.
* **Roxburgh Gorge**, one of her big highlights. The [jet boat](https://abigailhannah.nz/125-jetboating-27-wild-rivers-on-a-solo-trip-with-ollie-sharpe/) was really informative, taking you through the gorge to the different gold mining sites and talking about the history.
* **Dunstan Trail**, beautiful, and her second time on it. This time she rode it in reverse, from Clyde to Cromwell, and that's where she finished.

If she'd had more time, she had ideas for the Nevis Valley and the Mavora Lakes too.

### Distances and the people she met

Her daily distances were all over the place: some days 70 kilometres, some days 140. If she found somewhere nice, she'd stop. By the end, riding over 100 kilometres a day felt easy, which she never thought she'd say.

And the people were amazing. Everywhere she stopped, people talked to her and offered her accommodation, food or a motivational chat.

Nicole had already spent a lot of time in the South Island, hiking and road tripping, but the bike was way more immersive. On her last day she was devastated it was ending and wished she could ride for another couple of weeks, because it felt unfinished. She can always go back.

When I hiked the Old Ghost Road, I met a woman over from Australia who had just bought her bike and was cycling around the South Island, with her tent and all her gear on it.

## Bikepacking solo, tips and what's next {#tips-and-next}

This was the first time Nicole had done something this big solo. The challenges are that it's all on you: every decision, the cooking, packing up, managing your own equipment and carrying everything. The flip side is ultimate freedom to do whatever you want, whenever.

It was the right time for her to do it. She wanted to get away from what she was going through back home, and on her own, she felt detached from all of it and could be herself a little bit, which is exactly what she needed.

I've had past guests talk about the way nature lets you be who you are, with no judgement, and solo adventures give you that space to be.

### Nicole's tips for getting into bikepacking

If you want to get into [bikepacking](https://abigailhannah.nz/beginner-bikepacking/), Nicole's advice is:

* **Don't wait for the right gear.** Get some stuff together and go.
* **Don't worry about the right bike either.** Hers was worth about $400, and it made it all that way.
* **Skip the lightweight kit.** You don't need it.

In her words:

> "you don't need anything special just go out and do it. The pace that it takes you will be what it is and you'll have an amazing experience."

Gear is such a roadblock for a lot of people, so I love that one.

### What's next

Nicole always has something planned. For her next bikepacking trip she's been thinking about going overseas, maybe Tasmania, or British Columbia and down through Seattle.
