Ever since our Rangitoto and Motutapu circumnavigation, my DMs have been full of sea kayaking questions. How do you do it? Where do you get the gear? Can we come too? I was a little bit surprised by how many people were keen. But I think we forget how much there is right off the coast of Auckland, and how out of reach it can feel.

So I asked Bennett. He's in the same programme as [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/) at uni, he's taken us out on a couple of kayaking trips in the Hauraki Gulf, and he's always (always!) the one squeezing through the gap in the rocks. Bennett is from Singapore, and since arriving he's had, in his words, a little bit too much ease of access to kayaks.

We cover how he learned to roll in a swimming pool, his favourite day paddles and island campsites in the Gulf, a 16-hour sufferfest around the Auckland isthmus, and how to get started from zero. Plus the hazards, because the sea is soooo much bigger than you are.

## From Singapore to a sea kayak {#bennett-story}

Bennett grew up in Singapore, which is about the same size as Lake Taupō but has more people than all of New Zealand.

His love for the outdoors started with being allowed to play a lot as a kid. Singapore gets a lot of rain, and every time it really poured, he'd beg to go downstairs and play in the mud on the field across the road. Add beach days, trips to the zoo and a fascination with insects and animals, and that's where it got going.

The water has always been his happy place, though kayaking on school camps felt like one more thing school was making him do.

That changed around 17, when he was studying for a Diploma in Outdoor Adventure Learning at a polytechnic in Singapore. He was competing in swimming and lifesaving, and starting to get a bit sick of it. Then he saw someone roll a kayak right in front of him. Here was a way to be underwater whenever he felt like it.

It took him about a month to learn, in an old whitewater kayak in a swimming pool. After that he kept hassling his lecturers to open the pool so he could muck around in a boat.

People started suggesting he get into coaching, and he went on to pass a coaching course that took him four years, with six months in Vietnam and two years of military service in between. Then came a gap year of freelancing around Singapore as a kayak coach and guide, with the odd five days in Bali.

> "I came to New Zealand … with kayaking in mind because I knew that this is the country where I could push myself into conditions that Singapore just doesn't have."

## Paddling the Hauraki Gulf from Auckland {#hauraki-gulf}

In Auckland, Bennett did a graduate diploma in sport and exercise at AUT, Auckland University of Technology. In his first week he spotted a sign for Auckland Sea Kayaks in a souvenir shop, emailed to ask for work, and started on rentals, then guided trips. He also joined the Auckland University Canoe Club (AUCC), which is how he got into [whitewater](https://abigailhannah.nz/white-water-kayaking-nz/). For school, for work and on his days off, he's been kayaking.

When he first arrived he couldn't drive, but AUT let him borrow kayaks and gear from the North Shore campus. So he'd pull a loaded kayak three kilometres down to Takapuna Beach, then paddle to Rangitoto, or head off on three-day trips somewhere in the [Hauraki Gulf](https://abigailhannah.nz/025-sailing-stephanie-haden/).

Bennett gets a touch of imposter syndrome talking about the Gulf, because he's not from New Zealand.

I can guarantee he's seen a lot more of it than most Aucklanders.

His favourite spot depends on what he wants out of a trip. Almost any island works for rock gardening, and he loves wildlife and any hole in the rocks or narrow gap that takes a bit more skill. That's where all the cool stuff is, he says.

I can attest to this. On our trips, if there's a cave or a tunnel with light on the other side, Bennett's there, trying to squeeze through.

## Best Hauraki Gulf day paddles {#day-paddles}

Bennett keeps a ranked list of trips from Auckland that are relatively accessible if you have a boat and good value for money on fuel.

### Tiritiri Matangi

Top of his list for a day paddle is Tiritiri Matangi, the island off the Whangaparāoa Peninsula. He's been back many times, sometimes alone and sometimes with a mate.

He raves about it to Isaac and me constantly, and we're working on getting there.

It's a bird paradise. On the eastern side there's a big, cavernous sea cave you can fit a double kayak through. You need to be there at the right tide, without too much swell coming in from the east. Every time he's been round the back, there have been fur seals. On the far side there's good rock hopping and tide pooling, and a tiny island off the island (an island-ception) with nice channels and caves he hasn't caught at the right tide yet.

The full trip around the island that he rates as his best is more like 20 kilometres, or 25 if you paddle like Bennett and go through every gap.

That can sound like a lot, but it's doable with a bit of experience. I've done 20-kilometre days twice, with only a handful of paddle days behind me.

Bennett tells his clients that sea kayaking is about finding a nice pace and trotting along at it.

> "You know, it's kind of like running a marathon, kind of like going for a long tramp. You just want to get into your rhythm and enjoy it."

### Moturekareka and the Rewa shipwreck

Up in Mahurangi, Bennett has a soft spot for Moturekareka, with another sea cave and, most times, fur seals. There's also a big, rusted shipwreck, the Rewa, which you can find on Google Maps, with fish swimming in and around it. It's a wonderful snorkelling spot. He's snorkelled it in the middle of winter, which he admits isn't his best decision.

When Isaac told me there was a shipwreck in the Hauraki Gulf, I thought he was kidding. Look it up. We're not lying.

## Kayak camping on Motuora and Motuihe {#kayak-camping}

DOC campsites are spread across the Hauraki Gulf, and Bennett's favourite is Motuora Island, also up in Mahurangi. You can link it with Moturekareka and the shipwreck for a two-day trip with a bit of a snorkel.

Motuora has kiwi, so you might not get the best night's sleep: they can be noisy, with a screech Bennett describes as somewhat dinosaury. The campsite is right on the beach. You pull your kayak up, carry it about 12 metres, put your tent up and you're done. Then head out on a night walk with red-light torches and see how many kiwi you can spot. For an overnight trip out of Auckland, Motuora is at the top of his list.

Motuihe comes second for Bennett, right after Motuora. Our Rangitoto, Motutapu and Motuihe trip with him was the easiest to do from the North Shore without much driving.

I had no idea where Motuihe was in the Gulf, or that there was a DOC campsite there. It was so beautiful, and so close to home.

## Circumnavigating the Auckland isthmus by kayak {#isthmus-circumnavigation}

For a sufferfest, Bennett's pick is the circumnavigation of the Auckland isthmus he did in the middle of winter with Isaac and a friend: more than 60 kilometres in a day, starting at 4am and finishing at 8pm.

The route:

* **Mission Bay** to start, then past the CBD and under the Harbour Bridge.
* **Point Chev** and on to the mouth of the Whau River, near Te Atatū.
* **The Whau River**, paddling all the way up into New Lynn.
* **Portage Road**, walking the kayaks from New Lynn down to Blockhouse Bay.
* **The Manukau Harbour**, paddling towards Māngere Bridge and Ōtāhuhu.
* **The Ōtāhuhu portage**, the other Portage Road in Auckland, back into the Tāmaki River.
* **The Tāmaki River**, out to sea and back to Mission Bay.

It meant bashing through random mangroves. Bennett would 100% do it again, but his advice is to skip the middle of winter and check the tides.

I drove around meeting them at different spots while they did all the hard mahi, with doughnuts for breakfast and fried chicken for lunch. It popped off on TikTok, and a lot of people didn't realise Portage Road is called Portage Road because you can portage on it.

Bennett points out that's the whole idea. Pā sites around Auckland were built to defend these waterways, because they were the main route for travelling by waka. He loves having done a modern-day version of that.

Of all his sufferfests, it's the most worthwhile, because you want to add meaning to your suffering.

## How to start sea kayaking in Auckland {#getting-started}

For Bennett, the biggest barrier is getting a boat. He wouldn't recommend doing most of the trips above in a random boat from Trade Me or Facebook Marketplace, because you could get caught out in conditions that a sit-on-top or an old whitewater boat can't handle.

His ways to start from zero:

* **Make friends who love sea kayaking** and get them to take you out. Join a Facebook group, ask nicely, then buy someone a beer or dinner. It's more social, and having someone with you is safer.
* **Sign up for a course.** There are a few commercial providers, and Bennett has run an introduction to sea kayaking course at Mission Bay for Auckland Sea Kayaks.
* **Book a guided trip.** If you have no idea what you're doing and want to test the water, this is the place to start, and arguably the most affordable, since a training course still won't get you out to an island.

Making paddling friends is how I got out in the Gulf: Bennett took us. Highly recommend.

Auckland Sea Kayaks also runs Rangitoto sunset tours, which Bennett guides on. A guide looks after your safety, so it's a good way to find out whether you love it or hate the sea.

## Sea kayaking safety in the Hauraki Gulf {#safety}

Even in the sheltered Hauraki Gulf, Bennett says the sea is bigger and more powerful than you will ever be. Mother Nature is the biggest hazard:

* **Wind** that's too strong, or that you don't understand.
* **Tidal streams**, as water moves in and out of the Gulf.
* **Currents** at certain points.
* **Swell**, which can combine with the wind so you end up somewhere you didn't expect.

It all takes time to understand, so don't go out blind. The other risk is ending up somewhere you can't rescue yourself: you've drifted out of cell service and your only phone has sunk. Worst case, but completely possible.

Then there's boat traffic, with a fair number of ferries moving around Auckland.

On our trip we crossed the Waiheke ferry route, and even when you don't notice the ferry going past, suddenly a big wake is rocking you about. Yachts came past quickly too, and we had to stop and let them through.

Bennett has a good way to picture it:

> "It's like you're a pedestrian and you're walking about. You realistically can't walk all that fast. But this time round, instead of walking on the sidewalk, everywhere is the road."

Get too close to the side and the road can rise up and smash you into the walls. With sailboats, ferries and container ships coming through, you're the small fry.

## What's next: Waiheke and Great Barrier {#whats-next}

Next on Bennett's list is a full circumnavigation of Waiheke, because it's one he hasn't done yet. He wants to do it in three days.

My fun fact for him: Waiheke has 120 kilometres of coastline.

He thinks he might be a bit overzealous.

He also wants to reach Pakatoa and Tarahiki, off Waiheke's east coast. Then there's [Great Barrier](https://abigailhannah.nz/142-tramping-and-exploring-aotea-great-barrier-island-with-kristina-montgomerie/). He wants a few days there, mixing sufferfest with enjoyable paddle: paddling himself out, then circumnavigating it because he's already there. With his busy work season about to start, it may have to wait until closer to winter.

And yes, he's often out there on a Tuesday with dolphins and seals while I'm wishing I'd gone too. I do get invited. It's a me problem.
