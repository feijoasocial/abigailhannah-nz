Nobody had talked about canyoning on the podcast before this chat. So I went in knowing basically nothing, and came out with it firmly on my bucket list.

Aimee Jules is a photographer from Nelson, and a lot of her work happens outdoors, shooting for outdoor brands. And she calls herself a beginner at canyoning, which made her the perfect person to break it down for the rest of us (no jargon, I promise).

The story here is a multi-day trip that ties it all together: kayaking through Abel Tasman with her boyfriend, a former canyoning guide, to tackle three canyons in a row. There's a wasp nest, a scary water slide, a slippery rock above a giant waterfall, and a really really tough call.

So we cover what canyoning is, the gear, how the grades work, each canyon on the trip, why turning back was the right decision, and how she gets her camera through it all in one piece. Plus her first film.

## Photography and finding her people {#aimee-story}

Aimee studied design at university, where there was a big emphasis on photographing your designs. She preferred the photographing, it snowballed, and now she's a full-time photographer. More and more of her work is outdoors, which she feels very lucky about.

The hard part is balance. She always wants to get outdoors for herself too, without a camera or a job attached. She can feel the pressure to take amazing photos and post them when she goes hiking, so it's nice sometimes to keep those moments for yourself.

Growing up, her family was active more than outdoorsy, into really competitive sports, with Sunday drives in a super old van that usually ended at a river or a beach.

She got into proper adventures later, when she tagged along with a friend on a four-day [four-wheel drive trip](https://abigailhannah.nz/106-overlanding-in-the-old-man-range-and-4wd-trips-with-jono-hitchcox/) over a long weekend near Hanmer Springs. She barely knew anyone and had no idea about four-wheel driving, but she and a group of girls clicked immediately. They started hiking together with no idea what they were doing, got themselves into some questionable situations and learned a lot. She wouldn't change a thing.

Her advice for finding your people: make a plan, and don't worry about experience. They invited anyone and everyone, and when someone said they'd never hiked before, the answer was easy:

> "oh, well, … you know how to walk. Perfect. Let's go."

## Canyoning 101 {#canyoning-101}

Aimee's description:

> "It's kind of like a water park, but like nature."

You jump off waterfalls, slide down them and abseil off them, scramble over rocks and swim. There are dry canyons and aquatic canyons, and all the ones in Abel Tasman have water in them. Aimee thinks Abel Tasman is awesome because you get everything in its canyons, including zip lines.

Her advice for a first go is a tour, and there are canyoning tour companies in Abel Tasman, including Abel Tasman Canyons. Canyons can be very unsafe if you don't know what you're doing, so go with a tour group or a friend who does.

She's no expert herself. Her boyfriend runs around on the slippery rocks, darting all over the show, while she waddles behind like a duck.

### Canyoning gear and protection

* **A canyoning harness.** Some people use a climbing harness, but canyoning ones have a bum pad, because sliding down the rocks will rip up your wetsuit.
* **Old running shoes.** Better than wetsuit booties, and an old pair is best because the worn rubber is softer and grips the rocks better.
* **A wetsuit.** It gets pretty chilly, and because the canyons sit in deep gorges, they don't get all-day sun.
* **Bolts at the features.** There are bolts at all the features, and sometimes a line is set up for crossing a sketchy bit, so you're not on rope the whole time. Torrent River has a bolted zip line.

As a [rock climber](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/), I was curious about the crossover. My only canyoning, in air quotes, was at a jungle camp at school as a kid, where we scrambled up a river and abseiled down a little waterfall with a figure eight, feeding the rope out ourselves. Was that safe?

In a canyon she hasn't done before, her boyfriend will sometimes send her down first, and she'll end up waiting under a waterfall, freezing, with the noise roaring, thinking this is actually kind of dodgy.

## Kayaking Abel Tasman to three canyons {#the-plan}

Aimee's boyfriend used to be a canyoning guide in [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/) for five summers. When they met, he took her canyoning, and she found a whole other world. They'd done Torrent River a few times, and Waterfall Creek once. Aimee was super keen to tick off the third, Falls River, so they planned a mission: kayak through Abel Tasman and do all three.

They took a water taxi up to Awaroa, which cut out a whole day of kayaking, and camped there the first night. The taxi arrived as the tide was coming off low, and the kayaks would only float in the estuary without their gear in them. Dropped on the main beach, Aimee ended up carrying most of the canyon gear, lots of heavy ropes and harnesses, trudging through the sand to the campsite.

I crossed that estuary when we walked Abel Tasman last summer, and you really do have to think about the tides.

## Canyoning Waterfall Creek {#waterfall-creek}

The next day they kayaked into the estuary below Waterfall Creek. Finding the entry was a mission. There's no real track to the Abel Tasman canyons, only little tags to follow, and it's a steep climb with a bit of bush bashing. Aimee loves that part: you feel like no one's ever been there before. Right before they dropped in, they walked through a wasp nest by mistake and ran.

At the top, you gear up, enjoy your last moments of being warm and dry, and pack everything into the canyon bag with the rope. It's best not to take too much, because you have to get it all down every feature, and the rope is really heavy (thankfully her boyfriend carries it).

Then you're straight into the slides and jumps. You get into a tight position, knees up and arms linked, and slide down with the waterfall into the pools. At the big drops, her boyfriend goes first with a snorkelling mask to check there's nothing in the bottom, like trees, because you never know if there's been a flood.

Once you're in, you're committed. You can get out, but it's an effort. It's really loud, with water rushing everywhere, and very isolated, so it can be a really uncomfortable place if you're not having a good time. When you are having a good time, it's the craziest, coolest thing.

### The last drop

Waterfall Creek finishes with a really big drop, and you can abseil it, jump it or take the water slide. The first time, Aimee took the slide. It's a scary one: you go a short way, get airborne for what feels like ages, hit the slide again, then fly off the actual waterfall. It was pretty terrifying, and it hurt a bit when she hit the water. This time she jumped, because it's nice to finish the canyon on a really positive jump.

Then they rock-hopped to the end and kayaked out at sunset on perfect water. What a start.

A few summers ago, we kayaked Abel Tasman, and it's still my favourite way to experience the park. Walking it last summer was hard work carrying our gear, and in a boat you can take as much as you want.

## Falls River and the call to turn back {#falls-river}

Next they paddled from Awaroa to Torrent Bay, a full day, and the day after went up to Falls River. Aimee hadn't done it before, and it's a higher grade: more risk, and once you're in, you're really in.

### How canyon grades work

Canyons are graded on a few things. There's a v grade for vertical, an a grade for aquatic, and a separate rating for commitment, meaning how hard it is to get out once you're in. A zero, Aimee says, would be something by the side of the road.

### The first feature

It's a steep climb up to Falls River, because you've got to get all that vert. Aimee had seen photos of its giant waterfall, the first feature, and was so stoked to see it. She'd also pitched the trip to a company and was making a video, which added to the pressure and the commitment.

They scrambled in on a beautiful day and reached the first feature: narrow, really loud, a rocky pool leading to a giant drop you can't see until you're over it. Since her boyfriend had last been in, it had changed. There'd been a lot of water through, and the rock they used to get across the river was coated in slippery algae. There were no bolts before that point, and it was high consequence: fall there and you go off the waterfall.

He tried standing in a narrow gap to lift her across, but it was too far. So he went across to the first bolt, and she tied into a line from it to scale a rocky wall of slippery, algae-covered rock. If she fell, she'd swing into the river and potentially dangle over the waterfall, still attached to the rope.

It was terrifying and so loud. Inching across, she started crying. She wanted to do it so badly, but the risk versus reward wasn't there, and the longer she stood on the rock, the slipperier the algae got.

### Bailing out

Her boyfriend suggested coming back down to reassess. Before he crossed back to her, he warned her: if she fell now, she'd take him out too, and he wasn't attached to anything. So please don't fall. He made his way over very carefully, and they sat down together. It was pretty emotional. He'd never seen her that terrified, and told her he didn't know if he could keep going seeing her like that, because in there they had to make really good decisions, and rattled as they were, they wouldn't.

So they bailed, which was gutting. Aimee had been so determined to do all three canyons, and she'd told everyone they were going in to do them. But she thinks it's really good to make those calls, and to know that nobody else can know what it felt like in there:

> "as much as I can explain it, no one's going to know the sounds and how cold it was and how slippery it was. And you just have to kind of be okay with that."

Falls River is harder than any canyon she'd done. If she'd fallen, they'd have been stuck in it, terrified, with her confidence gone. It was a sad walk back down a steep track, but they had some good food and some chocolate, and all was well.

You're the only one who knows your own boundaries, and people would much rather you miss one part of your trip than pull your [PLB](https://abigailhannah.nz/plb-safety/) and call search and rescue. It sounds like Aimee tried every option first.

## Torrent River and a day on Observation Beach {#torrent-river}

The Falls River day knocked Aimee's confidence, so the next day's canyon mattered. Torrent River was one she'd been in a couple of times, it was a beautiful day, and they had a lot of fun. She used to be a gymnast, so they spent time trying flips off things and doing it a bit differently to their first time down. It meant she left the trip on a high instead of bummed out.

They spent the whole next day on Observation Beach in a hammock.

If you've never been to Abel Tasman, I'm going to pitch it. You don't have to go canyoning. Go sit on a beach. On my first trip there, I was asking, are we in Fiji? Are we in Tahiti? It's one of my hands-down favourite parts of the country.

## Cameras in canyons and her first film {#camera-and-film}

On her first few canyoning trips, Aimee took in a GoPro, mainly for video. Since then she's taken her Canon R5, which was quite scary the first time. Her setup:

* **A Watershed bag**, a dry bag, with a camera insert for extra padding.
* **A beanie** wrapped around the camera inside it. It probably doesn't do much, she says, but it makes her feel good.

To shoot, she gets herself to a dry spot, lets everyone go, and follows on afterwards. She'd love an underwater housing, but they're bulky, and more camera gear slows the trip down a lot.

Aimee has done a couple more canyoning trips since. She'd love to go back to Falls River with a few more people, so they could make a human anchor to get her across the first feature. She's heard there are lots of cool canyons around Queenstown, and she's keen to go down there, learn more and take her camera in.

Her first film is Paddle X, a sports documentary on [Britt Spencer](https://abigailhannah.nz/112-crossing-the-cook-strait-on-a-paddleboard-and-surf-lifesaving-with-britt-spencer/). When we recorded, it had been in the mountain film festival, was going on the Gutsy Girls film tour in August, and would go up on YouTube after that. For her next film, she wants an expedition, and maybe it'll be canyoning.
