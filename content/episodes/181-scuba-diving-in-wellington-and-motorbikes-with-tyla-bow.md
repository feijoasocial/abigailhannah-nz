Tyla and I met on a motorbike course, of all places. So it was really really fun to reconnect, this time on the podcast, and talk about the two things she loves most: being underwater, and riding.

Tyla is originally from Auckland and based in Wellington now. She's a scuba instructor by trade, an artist, and a self-confessed jack of all trades who loves learning new things and teaching what she knows along the way. And she's the person you want next to you if the ocean makes you nervous. (It makes me nervous. I grew up a two-day trip from the sea.)

So we cover how she got from 17 years of gymnastics to teaching scuba, how to get over a fear of sharks, every step from a try dive to rescue diver, and why Wellington's south coast is one of her favourite places to dive. Plus cold water gear, the bends, buoyancy (I was all over the place on my first dive), and how to get into motorbikes when nobody around you rides.

## From gymnastics to the outdoors {#gymnastics-to-outdoors}

Tyla did gymnastics for 17 years. Her whole childhood was school, then the gym, so being outside became her happy place: somewhere she could be free. She grew up diving, surfing and going for bush walks with family, and when she stopped gym, she threw herself into the outdoors.

The shift was easy. She was always the barefoot kid at school, taking off her shoes to run around outside, and gymnastics meant never wearing shoes and being in tune with your body. So she took that outside. She reckons the outdoors is a beautiful space for your mental health, too.

## Becoming a scuba instructor {#scuba-instructor}

Tyla wanted to scuba dive from the moment she first went under as a kid. Training 30-hour weeks in gymnastics, she never had the two free weekends a course needs. So when she finished gymnastics, she jumped on an open water course as soon as she could. She loved it so much that she did a three-month course in Auckland up to rescue diver. She'd been a gym coach and is super passionate about teaching, so she became an instructor the following year.

Here's how she describes that first pull:

> "as soon as I was under there … I just want to explore everything. And then just being able to breathe under the water, like, man, that's a superpower"

For Tyla, the underwater world is an alien world. You can move in every direction, up and down, with a whole different level of freedom, and sitting there neutrally buoyant is peaceful and meditative. She puts the spark down to a childlike curiosity: everyone else at the beach lying in the sun, and her wondering what's under that rock.

She's a PADI instructor and has also crossed over into SSI, and she works at a Wellington dive shop where, when we recorded, the team was five women and one man.

## Getting over a fear of sharks and deep water {#fear-of-sharks}

Believe it or not, Tyla used to be terrified of stingrays (they've got stingers) and sharks (thanks, Jaws). She thinks fear is something you learn. These days she's asking where the sharks are.

Deep water can still get her. Looking down through a mask off a boat and seeing nothing but blue, she sometimes thinks something's going to eat her. Then she remembers it would be a shark, and she loves sharks.

Her way through fear is exposure therapy, moving slowly, with people who care and understand. And learning about the animals around you:

> "I was scared of sharks until I came across one and my heart is racing and then I just observe it and like, they want nothing to do with me."

As she puts it, humans are scarier than anything out there. Knowledge is key.

I relate to that from the [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/) world. Going up a cliff looks scary, and there are all these checks and precautions in place to make it safe. And a lot of my fears have very little basis: I watched Finding Nemo when I was five, and that shark has stuck.

## Scuba safety and how to get certified {#scuba-certification}

Tyla understands the fear of [scuba](https://abigailhannah.nz/045-scuba-diving-annika-andresen/), too: feeling claustrophobic, and relying on an air source. The whole point of a course is that it teaches you everything that could go wrong, so you can eliminate it.

Her safety basics:

* **Pre-dive buddy checks**, to make sure all your gear is working.
* **Serviced gear**, hired from reputable places, with tested air.
* **Checking your SPG** (submerged pressure gauge, your air) frequently.
* **Planning your dive and sticking to the plan**, because the number one rule is to make it home.

Things go wrong when people get complacent, skip their checks, or go out without a course and don't realise the risks, which can happen in [freediving](https://abigailhannah.nz/107-learning-to-freedive-spearfish-and-building-community-with-renee-taylor/) as well. Tyla's view is that scuba diving is extremely safe.

### From try dive to open water

Tyla's steps into scuba:

* **A try dive.** A lot of dive places run them, often in a pool, to get a feel for it. Some people feel claustrophobic in a pool and fine in the ocean, or the other way around.
* **A full open water course.** Your scuba certification, like a driver's licence for diving. Then you can dive with a buddy to 18 metres.
* **Guided dives and experienced mates.** Even after your course, they build your confidence.
* **Specialties and higher certifications, up to rescue.** If something goes wrong, you know what to do, because you've repeated those skills so many times.

My husband, [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/), has his PADI open water. On a trip to Thailand, I snorkelled on my first day, watching divers in a shipwreck below, which was baby steps for me. On our next trip I did a try dive: three dives in a day. The two others in my group skipped the middle one, and that was the dive with a huge stingray. The instructor signalled and pointed, and all I could see was a big rock. It was the stingray, and I was a little closer than I wanted to be. No one else saw it that day. That's my one and only scuba experience.

## Where to scuba dive in New Zealand {#where-to-dive}

Tyla hasn't dived overseas yet, and she thinks every region and dive spot in New Zealand has its own unique thing. She did all her training in Auckland, diving around northern Auckland and on boat dives.

* **Poor Knights Islands.** One of her favourite spots, with rich biodiversity and all sorts of ocean creatures passing through.
* **Lake Pupuke.** Where she's done a lot of dive training. She calls it a bit of a poopy lake: an underwater playground, old mussel beds that are now a bit sad, a lot of silt and a lot of invasive fish.
* **Lake Taupō.** Clearer, with freshwater kōura.
* **The Waikato River before Huka Falls.** A drift dive when the floodgates open, with great visibility and trout.

I love how many different ways there are to experience a place like Taupō: tramping, mountain bike trails, [fly fishing](https://abigailhannah.nz/fly-fishing-nz/), and now diving.

### Diving in Wellington

When Tyla first visited [Wellington](https://abigailhannah.nz/021-wellington-gems-local-travel-tilleigh/) before moving, people asked if she was going diving, and she thought, what diving? Then she found a massive marine reserve on the south coast. Most of the shore dives she does off the south coast are better than any boat dives she's done around Auckland, with some of the richest biodiversity around.

Highlights:

* **Taputeranga Marine Reserve**, on the south coast.
* **Island Bay**, with a big island out the front that gives protection if there's a bit of southerly swell. There's a small snorkel trail marked with buoys.
* **Swim-throughs** a couple of metres deep, lined with anemones in all sorts of colours.
* **Nudibranchs** everywhere.
* **Night dives.** Get the torches out and a whole bunch of other species come out.

Visibility in Wellington can be limited, and what you're looking for is very small, so you dive close to the rock shelves with a torch. You have to know what to look for.

## Scuba gear for cold water diving {#scuba-gear}

New Zealand is cold for diving, so you'll want one of two suits:

* **A dry suit.** Basically a human-shaped balloon. You stay dry, unless it floods, which isn't great.
* **A seven-millimetre semi-dry.** It has special seals at the neck and wrists. The first bit of seawater is cold, then you're diving in a little warm bath.

Tyla reckons cold water is the best place to train. If you can dive in a seven-mil two-piece suit, super floaty and carrying all those weights, with good buoyancy, going overseas and diving in togs is way easier. Going the other way, you'd wonder what all this bulk is.

### What to buy first

* **If you dive to travel:** your own mask, snorkel and dive computer, so you travel light.
* **If you'll mostly dive cold water at home:** a full snorkel set, meaning fins, booties, suit, gloves, hood, mask, snorkel and a weight belt. Then you only hire the regs, BCD and tank, which need the most servicing.
* **Buying gear:** go to a shop, so you know it comes from a reputable source and hasn't been sitting in someone's garage for 12 years. Cost is a barrier, so second-hand is fine, as long as a shop checks it and it's serviced and in good working order.

## Advanced courses, the bends and buoyancy {#advanced-diving}

If you've got your open water, Tyla's plea is to do your advanced. You can dive deeper, you navigate better so you're less likely to get lost, and your buoyancy improves, which helps with taking photos. You also get a taster of different dives, like a night dive, with supervision. Then go on to rescue, because if things go wrong, you'll know how to save your own life or someone else's.

More of her tips:

* **Do a boat dive with a shop first**, before diving off your mate's boat.
* **Do the deep specialty** if you want to go deeper overseas, to 40 metres.
* **Enriched air (nitrox)** lets you stay at depth a bit longer, to look at that cool thing or take photos.

Tyla would love to try technical diving, cave diving and extended range one day.

My eyes were opened to how technical diving can get when I watched Deeper, a documentary about cave diving. It follows one of the cave divers from the Thailand cave rescue, who has had an obsession with the Pearse Resurgence, near Nelson in [Kahurangi](https://abigailhannah.nz/165-an-18-day-mission-through-nelson-lakes-kahurangi-and-abel-tasman-with-the-national-park-trifecta/) National Park. He keeps going back to dive it deeper and deeper.

### How the bends works

The bends is short for decompression sickness. When you dive, the nitrogen you breathe goes into solution in your blood. If you come up too quickly, that nitrogen comes out as bubbles inside your body, when it should be breathed out.

To stay safe:

* **Follow your dive tables or dive computer.** A computer runs an algorithm to estimate how much nitrogen is in your body and keep you within your no-decompression limit. If you go deeper than planned, it tells you to do decompression stops.
* **Do a safety stop** at five metres for three minutes. It can be skipped in an emergency, and Tyla always does them.
* **Ascend slowly.** Your maximum ascent rate is 18 metres per minute.

Even within the limits there's always a chance, for example if you're tired, hungover or dehydrated. The safety measures are based on years and years of research.

### Buoyancy tips

On my try dive, I was bobbing all over the place. Tyla's tips:

* **Breathe steadily.** Breathing in lifts you a little and breathing out drops you. Never hold your breath, and take nice steady half breaths.
* **Adjust your BCD in tiny amounts.** On the way down, the air in it compresses, so if you're sinking, add a little bit of air, take a breath and check. Add too much and you'll shoot up. If you're rising, vent a little.
* **Practise.** Any change to your gear will feel off at first. Keep diving, do the buoyancy specialty or your advanced, and watch videos online. Nobody's buoyancy is great when they start.

I loved the feeling of taking a big breath to float up and over a rock. It was so buzzy. The Poor Knights are high on my list now, and Tyla's advice is to get my advanced first. (I need my open water first. I've only done a try dive.)

### Wild Chix and women in diving

If you're nervous, Tyla's involved with [Wild Chix](https://abigailhannah.nz/161-empowering-kiwi-women-in-hunting-fishing-with-wild-chix-isabell-zitzelsberger/), where she offers a women-only space and will personally teach you scuba, freediving or anything else. Everywhere she's dived, the gender split has been pretty even.

I took so much from my conversation with Isabell about Wild Chix. When we're [sea kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/182-finishing-a-two-month-slow-paddle-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/), I think about knowing how to do everything myself.

## Learning to ride a motorbike {#motorbikes}

Tyla and I met on a Ride Forever Bronze course, a defensive riding course for motorcyclists.

I had a motorbike when I had my corporate job, because I didn't want to sit in Auckland traffic or take too many buses to work. It was the only way I'd accept the job. Growing up in India, motorbikes were a normal way to get around, so I was surprised by how shocked people were here. And riding home from work on a rainy long weekend once, I nearly got hit three times in half an hour, none of it my fault. After that, I refused to ride on long weekends.

People get pretty excited when they find out Tyla rides: this tiny girl has a motorbike? Then they hear about everything else she does, and it makes sense. For Tyla, riding is an extra level of freedom. On a bike, you're tuned in, noticing everything around you, and it makes you feel more alive.

Most of Tyla's riding has been commuting, and she rode from Auckland to Wellington when she moved. The back roads were really fun, even if it's hard to trust there's no gravel around the next corner. Her rule: ride like everything's out to get you, in all the gear, all the time.

### How to get into riding

Tyla found it hard to get into riding with nobody around her who rode. How are you supposed to pass a riding test when you can't ride? She learned with family support, with someone following her in a car at first. Her tips:

* **Find people to practise with.** Make friends with someone who has a bike. There's a beautiful riding community out there.
* **Do the written test, then the basic handling test.** That gets you your learners.
* **Get as much gear as you can**, even second-hand.
* **Get a helmet that fits.** Go in store, because head shapes, brands and sizes vary. Only one brand and size fits Tyla's head, and it's very expensive. It's your life.
* **Do a defensive riding course**, like Ride Forever.
* **Practise everything**, including stopping and riding in the wet, in car parks, back roads and quiet streets first. Go slow, be kind to yourself, and don't push yourself into Auckland traffic (definitely not at rush hour).

My helmet and boots were new, and my jacket and pants second-hand. I'd ridden a scooter at uni, so the basic handling test wasn't new to me. Then suddenly you're on the road on your learners, putting it all together, and you're motorbike size, not car size. For my first rides, I got Isaac to follow me in the car, on the 50 roads, then the 80s, the motorway and the 100s, until I had more confidence. I don't have a bike at the moment, and I can't wait to get another one.

Tyla's motorbike bucket list is open: if someone asked her to ride somewhere, she'd go. She'd love to ride around the South Island and to ride overseas, ideally with a mate carrying the dive gear in a car, so she can ride, dive and ride again.

## Listener questions and getting started {#getting-started}

A listener asked about a ride that surprised her, and what you get from a bike that you miss hiking or driving. Tyla's favourite is the road around Wellington's south coast: after a long, hard day at work scuba diving, a ride beside the coast is very freeing. On a bike, she says, you notice the turns, the steep bits and the fun corners, and you're a lot more present.

I'd love to see more of New Zealand from a bike. You're exposed to everything, it's more intimate than the protected bubble of a car, and you cannot be a lazy rider.

Another listener said New Zealand's underwater world doesn't get talked about as much as the mountains. Tyla's answer: get down here, go underwater, and tell her that again. Search for diving destinations and you'll find so many incredible boat dives and shore dives. She didn't even know Wellington was a diving spot until she moved there.

### Tyla's advice

There are so many excited people waiting to help you become an epic diver. Tyla is setting up Wild Chix events across New Zealand soon, and she's happy to take people out. Her advice:

> "If you're thinking about it, just go for it. If you're anxious about it, just go for it."

She also wanted to add something about respect. Being outside makes you more in tune with your surroundings, and underwater you see the trash and the damage that's been done, which makes you more aware of how to be sustainable.

I feel that from sea kayaking, too: we found a cat's eye from a road floating in the sea. Once you see nature first-hand, you realise how special it is and how much we need to look after it.
