Nobody had ever shared the Around the Mountain track on this podcast before. So when Kels and Liv said Taranaki was one of their favourite adventures, I was really really keen to hear it.

Kelsea and Olivia, or Kels and Liv, are Girls Off Grid. They met on the first day of year one and they're still best mates. Between them they hike, hunt, surf, mountain bike and do a bit of mountaineering, and they'll tell you they're self-taught and not experts in any of it (I'd argue with that).

They walked Around the Mountain in three days. DOC gave it four to five, and most of it was closed. There's a near fall in the first hour, a 12-hour day, ferns over their faces and a car park about to shut for a 1080 drop.

So this post covers the trip day by day, how they keep each other going when it gets hard, hunting and mountaineering as older beginners, advice for women getting outdoors, the gear they'd splurge on (pink, ideally) and their 30 before 30 list.

## Kels and Liv, best mates since year one {#kels-and-liv}

Kels started primary school about six weeks before Liv. On Liv's first day, the teacher asked for volunteers to show her around. Kels stood up, Liv picked her, and they've been inseparable ever since. Years later, someone from their primary school told them nobody ever knew who was Liv and who was Kels, so everyone called them Liv and Kels.

Neither grew up hunting or tramping. There was plenty of camping, one of them did surf life saving through nippers, and their parents encouraged them to play outside. Liv's turning point was Duke of Ed at high school, which taught her some good hiking skills. Then, by their own account, they partied pretty hard for a good decade.

Both are Auckland based with busy jobs. Weekends partying shrank to once a week, until they realised they preferred being outdoors, and friends introduced them to the things they do now. They count themselves lucky, too: an overnight hike is far easier with a mate who'll always say yes.

I feel the same about my husband. We got married at 20 and 21 (not something I'd recommend to everyone), and our interests have gone the same way. While we were engaged, he suggested buying a [van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/) to live in. I thought, what kind of crazy person would do that? Now it's my life.

## Fitting adventures around Auckland work weeks {#auckland-weekends}

I struggled heaps with this when I was in [corporate](https://abigailhannah.nz/bonus-i-quit-my-corporate-job/). I did the Friday-to-Monday dash a couple of times, but I couldn't do it every other weekend. So I asked how they manage.

Kels says you've got to suck it up and do it. Overthinking it is the trap: the packing, the tiredness, the drive. You don't realise how much getting out fills your cup. Liv is the spontaneous one, deciding on a Friday morning to head to the mountains, and a lot of the time Kels tags along. Kels also says it's important to keep some weekends to reset.

Nature is how they recharge. Stuck at home on a Saturday, they get a little bit feral and lose four hours to scrolling. The bush is the one place nobody can reach them. Getting out of Auckland past the Bombays is a nightmare, but they've never once regretted it.

I can top that. Even when traffic's flowing, my van only does about 40 up the Bombays. So if you're stuck behind a green van on the motorway, it's me.

## Around the Mountain on Taranaki {#around-the-mountain}

Liv was training for a trip to Nepal and wanted something really challenging, so of course she texted Kels to drag her along. Earlier that year, Liv had met a friend at Syme Hut, and the three of them decided to get in the trenches together.

They found Around the Mountain. It had been closed for a year and a half, it's marked on the DOC website as an advanced track for experienced trampers, and DOC said four to five days. They had three.

It was far harder than they imagined, and they still rate it one of the hardest multi-day hikes they've done, because of the timeframe and because most of the track was closed. They're very clear it's not recommended. What they did have was confidence in their nav skills and their group: they've done a lot of hiking, and Kels has done a lot of hunting off track.

Before we got into it, I shared my trick for telling the two mountain circuits apart. Around the Mountain starts with an A, which is the shape of Taranaki. Round the Mountain starts with an R, for [Ruapehu](https://abigailhannah.nz/032-ruapehu-solo-hazel-phillips/). When we recorded, I'd looked it up and Around the Mountain was still closed.

Kels and Liv aren't sure it would even be doable now, given how overgrown it was. You'd need a machete for sure.

## Three days on Around the Mountain {#three-days}

### Day one to Holly Hut

Day one built a bit of false confidence: a cruisy four-hour walk into Holly Hut, yapping the whole way. It was late September, still cold, with snow on the mountain. In hindsight they could have taken snowshoes or basic mountaineering gear, though they didn't really need it.

### Day two: a near fall and a 12-hour day

Expecting an easy day, they had a leisurely hut morning of coffee and oats and left at 10am. At the junction, they went to photograph the sign. Over the time to the next hut was a piece of tape, because that part was closed. Kels peeled it back: eight and a half hours, and that was for an open track. This one was overgrown, with landslides, and it was still getting dark early.

At the closed section they tossed up whether to go on. Liv had to be ready for Nepal, so they kept going. About an hour in, they stopped near some steep stairs, and Kels, under a heavy three-day pack, lost her footing.

> "Like everyone does when you're hiking, you kind of just, when you lose focus and you've got your big packs, you kind of take a stumble. I took a stumble."

She almost went off an eight-metre sheer drop onto rocks. Their friend jumped on top and grabbed her, breaking his hiking poles, and something else caught her too. Liv, up on a ladder section, was cracking up and about to start filming until their friend told her not to look. They call it the first near-death experience, and Mother Nature's reminder to lock in.

That was the first hour of a 12-hour day, and some of the hardest walking they've done. The track dips in and out of little valleys, and they call it really, really technical terrain. They crossed big landslides, it rained and got very dark, and a shortcut ended at a river they couldn't cross, so they went all the way back. They reached the hut at 10pm, absolutely rooted, to find a few Hare Krishnas inside with the fire on high.

For terrain, Liv says, Nepal turned out to be a walk in the park: beautiful gravel.

### Day three: bush, ice and the wrong car park

Their maps were quite different to what they found on the ground. Their friend laid out two options: walk a few hours out to the road end and hitchhike back around, or finish the circuit with no guarantee they could get past an overhang of rocks. They were absolutely finishing it.

The day went from alpine snowy sections to bush to river valleys, with a lot of elevation. After hours in the bush line, they peeped out at a sheer face that looked vertical, and they had to climb to the top of the alpine section to get around it. By then they were too far in to turn back.

Kels can tell when Liv is in a dark place. Liv loves a good yarn, so when she goes mute, Kels checks in with a "hey Liv, you all good bro". Their friendship runs on fist pumps and banter, so Liv usually snaps back. This time she was trying to keep it together, and may have had a little cry. She was spent, with a really sore shin, in the most overgrown part of the whole track. The ferns were over their faces, and they were swimming through it.

Then came the tussock, everyone non-verbal, up and down, up and down, until a section so icy their friend wasn't sure they could pass. Liv was not going back down what they'd come up. They punched on through, and admit it may not have been the most responsible call.

Finally, a boardwalk, then stairs, stairs, stairs down to a car park. The wrong car park. So they climbed all the way back up to their own, where the gates were about to be locked for a 1080 drop. They got out in time, then drove back to Auckland for work on Monday.

They call it type three fun, a layer deeper than type two. But they stand by the bush in Taranaki as the most beautiful bush, like a fairy forest, and they reckon that done in the days allocated, you won't find a more diverse, beautiful track.

Both name the same highlight: breaking out of the bush line on the last day, with the sun hitting the tussocks. Kels adds a beautiful waterfall, and three good mates doing hard stuff together.

## Hitting a wall on a hard tramp {#hitting-a-wall}

Liv's lowest point was that rock face on day three. Nepal was her benchmark, and she was in a lot of pain.

> "There was a thought in the back of my head that we might have to turn around and just go back and still walk the road out. And I hate that."

She's a loop girl, and hates an out and back.

Kels's rule is that when one of them is down, the other one stays up and keeps the morale going. They're pretty good at vibe checking each other.

> "If you're both down, you're out. … someone's got to be okay someone's got to get pushing"

Their best tip is the simplest: nothing beats a break and a snack. Liv says they don't go light on food, and overwhelm is when mistakes happen, like a wrong turn. So stop, have a snack and some water, and take a moment. Take breaks regularly, before you're dehydrated and hungry. As they put it, fuel as much as you move.

With people you don't know well, or a destination-focused group, it's easy to push on so you're not a hassle, so speak up when you need to stop.

So many trampers will relate to going non-verbal when you hit a wall. My own hack is to give myself a number of cries before any hard hike. A day hike might get one. Roy's Peak, definitely two. Five days, three. If I finish with cries left over, it's a win. You're allowed to be mad at the ferns.

## Hunting, mountaineering and being a beginner {#beginner-hobbies}

A lot of hunters grew up hunting through family or on farms, Kels says. She and her boyfriend got into it after going out with a friend a couple of times, and got their gun licences. She's had hers for three years and is still learning: where and how to hunt, how to kill an animal humanely, how to process it, and gun safety. They already loved hiking, so why not get some food and do their part for nature, since pests do a lot of damage to native flora and fauna. She learns from YouTube, Instagram and getting out there, and recommends NZDA as a great place to start.

[Hunting](https://abigailhannah.nz/161-empowering-kiwi-women-in-hunting-fishing-with-wild-chix-isabell-zitzelsberger/) is more Kels's thing, and [mountaineering](https://abigailhannah.nz/035-mt-earnslaw-mountaineering/) is Liv's. After their first mountaineering trip together, Liv stuck with it and Kels backed off.

Liv started out with more experienced people she knew. This year she realised how dangerous mountaineering is and that she wanted to go further, so she did courses. Without them, she says, she's a large liability to the people she climbs with. Some things need proper training, so you pick where to spend your money, time and resources.

On learning, they say commit, and get over feeling like a loser. In [surfing](https://abigailhannah.nz/learn-to-surf-new-zealand/) or [mountain biking](https://abigailhannah.nz/047-mountainbiking-nz-laura-goldsworthy/), you always feel out of your depth at first, but everyone was there once and the excitement is in the learning. Be the older newbie, out with 10-year-olds at 30. Last week they tried [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/), and it was so humbling.

I know that feeling. I used to climb fairly often, and going back after a three-year break, in my late 20s, nothing was more humbling. The next day every muscle in my body hurt.

## Advice for women getting into the outdoors {#women-outdoors}

Kels and Liv know they're a bit spoiled, having always had each other. Even so, Liv has met some of her best mates outdoors, including that Around the Mountain friend, in a hut.

She once told a mate who wanted friends beyond the pub that she had to put herself in that context. Outdoorsy people cross over into ice baths and run clubs too. And even in Auckland there are trails everywhere: on a weekend at home, they'll hit a west coast trail.

Social media helps: there are hiking groups for women, and all you need is one person. They're happy to plan a hike off a TikTok video, because you can see what you'll be dealing with, and they find the DOC website a bit janky sometimes.

Start with a cheap pair of boots and a day hike, because outdoor gear gets expensive once you upgrade. Liv rented her mountaineering gear for two and a half years, which kept it low commitment.

## Tramping gear they swear by {#tramping-gear}

Pink kit is their signature. Is any of it essential? Maybe not, but Liv's pink ice axe is her favourite thing ever.

I'm with them. When I was looking for a kayak, the only one on Marketplace in six months was half yellow, half orange, so I took it. Two weeks later my husband found a pink one, and the orange boat went. It's so much better to suffer in a pink boat, and pink is one of the most visible colours, so it's a safety thing.

Otherwise they buy very different things. One carries an Osprey pack. One wears Scarpa boots and the other Lowa, and one wears Arc'teryx and the other Stoney Creek. They both swear by:

- **Merino:** a good merino top, like Icebreaker.
- **Pants:** they've switched from leggings to pants, though finding pants that fit is a nightmare when you're built like garden gnomes.
- **The splurges:** good boots, a pack that fits you, a good rain jacket and good merino.
- **Nice to have:** a Jetboil.

If you're dry and your pack and feet are comfortable, you're pretty good. And if it's pink, it's a bonus.

## A 30 before 30 list and an adventure race {#thirty-before-thirty}

Both are about to turn 30, so there's a 30 before 30 list, with a March deadline. When we recorded, they weren't a quarter of the way through. There are some rogue ones, like night surfing and a multi-day hike in Crocs. They don't do many Great Walks, preferring the little random side ones.

Also on the list is the Spirited Women's Adventure Race, an all-women's event for teams of four. There's a short, medium or long course mixing kayaking, mountain biking, running or hiking, and navigation. They're doing the medium course, which takes 6 to 12 hours, next year. It's their first adventure race, and they've started training.

When I asked who's kayaking, they suggested borrowing the pink one and asked me to join the team. Adventure racing terrifies me, and my pink boat is very slow. They said that's okay, they're plotters.

Liv wants redemption on Pakistan. She was meant to go in April, but plans changed and she went to Mongolia, which was amazing. She's going back, and hoping to convince Kels to come.
