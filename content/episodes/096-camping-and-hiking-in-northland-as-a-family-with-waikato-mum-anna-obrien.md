I met Anna on the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/). It was my first solo hike, I was at the end of a long downhill and so ready for the hut, and then there she was, coming the other way with SO much energy. We stopped for a chat, and after we'd gone our separate ways I was kicking myself for not asking for her details. (A friend of hers spotted my post about it later and sent me her handle.)

Anna is a maths teacher and an ex-accountant. She grew up in the Wairarapa, now lives in the Waikato, and loves running trails. She splits her hiking three ways: an annual solo trip, big girls' trips, and adventures with her kids.

So this one covers all three. Her solo trips in the Tararuas and on the Old Ghost Road, her favourite trip ever (a Northland road trip with her three kids, hut nights included), her tips for tramping with little ones, and how she found her hiking girlfriends.

## How Anna got into the outdoors {#anna-story}

Anna grew up riding horses on the farm. Then, in her first year after school, she spent a year in Kangaroo Valley in Australia as an assistant teacher in outdoor education: rock climbing, abseiling, canoeing, kayaking, bushcraft, navigation (at night, too), trail running and mountain biking, camping way off-grid. It was pivotal, and ever since, she's needed the outdoors.

When she had babies, she started trail running, because she still needed to get to the mountains and had to do it faster. Once the kids fitted in the backpack, they came too. Some of their first words were "orange triangle", and once they could walk, they'd point to the next one and lead the way. Indoors they might squabble. Outside, they get along, laugh and make up games, and the bush calms Anna down. It feels like home.

## Abel Tasman and the Old Ghost Road solo {#old-ghost-road-solo}

The trip where we met was day seven of back-to-back solo adventures for Anna. After some time with her kids in the Nelson region, they flew home on Christmas Eve, and she drove on in pouring rain to Tōtaranui in [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/).

She spent four nights tenting by herself: at Mutton Cove, with a run around the northern headland, then Anapai Bay and Onetahuti Beach. Then she drove to Lyell and walked into Lyell Saddle Hut in the dark. She wanted to see in New Year's Eve at Ghost Lake Hut.

On the way through, there was a total whiteout at Heaven's Door. On the way back, new friends from the huts offered her a ride, but she turned it down, because she hadn't seen the views. Walking back was absolutely worthwhile.

She met a few other solo women on the trail, including an Australian riding it backwards on a loaded bike, fresh off the [Paparoa](https://abigailhannah.nz/168-from-first-tramp-to-paparoa-and-all-the-great-walks-with-nicola-harvie/), with panniers and bags front and back. Anna kept offering to stay with her and help carry the bike up the Skyline Steps.

I spent a night in a hut with the same woman. She'd come over from Australia with her bike and nothing else, as her whole way of getting around New Zealand, and I thought that was so cool.

My own Old Ghost Road happened because my husband was off kayaking. I usually rely on him to tell me what to pack and look after the food, so going solo meant knowing exactly what was in my pack and what I was eating every day. When I finished, I cried for the whole afternoon and the next morning. Relief, I think, and being quite proud of myself. The last two days were hard, but the sunset at Ghost Lake Hut and the walk over Skyline Ridge made it worth it.

Anna gets that. In her words:

> "I'm so proud of all of us who get out there and do it, right? And live our lives because I feel like too many people wait to die."

## Solo tramping in the Tararuas {#tararuas-solo}

Anna tries to do one reasonably big solo trip a year. Sometimes it's because no one else can come, and sometimes she goes on purpose, after a stressful stretch. Alone, she notices everything beautiful, and it's a reset for her.

### Maungahuka Hut and the Tararua ladder

One of her first solo trips was in the Tararuas, up to Maungahuka Hut, which she'd seen in Wilderness magazine with its tarn out the front. A man she passed on the way told her there was no way she'd make it that night. She got there late, alone up on the tops, with cloud cascading over the ridges and the sun sitting low in the west. She felt capable, and like she was exactly where she was supposed to be.

She'd hoped to have the hut to herself, but three others turned up, and as you do in huts, they all got on really well. Next morning they dropped their packs and went up the famous Tararua ladder together as a side trip. It was nice having buddies for her first time on it.

### Pinnacles Ridge to Cow Creek

She still wants to walk the Tararuas north to south. Recently she set out to do it and realised she wasn't in training: ultra fitness doesn't stay unless you maintain it. So she did a big loop past Dundas Hut and Arete Forks Hut and out via Cow Creek, which had some of the most rooty, slippery track in the range.

At Arete Forks Hut she finally had a hut to herself. She lit the fire and calls it the ideal, like having your own little hotel. She reckons being alone in a hut is more comfortable than sharing it with one other person.

I've never been the only person in a backcountry hut, and I think it would scare me a little bit.

Anna grew up near the Tararuas, and she has a lot of respect for them. She's always cautious and sensible there, and always checks in with people as she goes.

### Learning through necessity

The first time Anna took her daughter tramping on her own, they went to Waitawheta Hut. On an earlier trail run there, she'd met some big black bulls in a paddock and turned around and run back. With her daughter, she walked the fence line, chatting loudly, ready to throw her over the fence at any second. Going on her own taught her how not scary it was, and led to her hiking solo.

> "I feel like a lot of us women end up learning through necessity"

## A Northland road trip with three kids {#northland-road-trip}

Anna's favourite trip ever, ahead of everywhere she's been in Europe, was a Christmas road trip around [Northland](https://abigailhannah.nz/176-a-locals-guide-to-northland-and-building-hiking-communities-with-hannah-whittaker/) with her three kids. They'd just become really good fun to road trip with, so she planned something epic.

### Whangaroa, Lane Cove Hut and Duke's Nose

They drove up to Whangaroa and spent a night there, learning about the area's Māori history and the European colonisation. Anna was a primary teacher at the time, and she thinks learning our history is so important. They learned about the Boyd massacre, which led to conversations about cause and effect and how we treat people.

From Whangaroa, they took a water taxi to Lane Cove Hut for two nights. The hut is right on the water, and the kids dug for crabs, swam in the river and walked the track. They climbed Duke's Nose too, which is close to vertical, with poles to climb, and Anna was so proud watching them look after each other on the way up.

I've done Duke's Nose as an out-and-back walk, and it's one of my favourites in Northland.

### Otamure Bay

Next they drove south to Otamure Bay, near Whananaki, for two nights in a tent, with plenty of hiking and time on the beach. Anna brought exercise books so the kids could record things and draw. Her youngest couldn't write yet, but heading to the beach one day he told her he needed his book, because he had to write the news.

One night the wind nearly flattened the tent onto their faces while the boys slept. The kids were little troopers.

### Peach Cove Hut

Their last stop was a night at [Peach Cove Hut](https://abigailhannah.nz/126-adventures-with-kids-and-a-peach-cove-rescue-with-renata-wiles/) at Whangārei Heads. By then her youngest had had enough. So Anna carried the big pack with all the kids' gear, the older two carried their own, and the youngest rode on her shoulders, a kilometre and a half up and a kilometre and a half down. On the way back he lay across her arms while she carried him up the stairs.

They shared the eight-bunk hut with another woman and her four nieces and nephews, who were brilliant value. She led them in a guided meditation, they swam together and they heard kiwi calling at night.

There was no reception, and her middle child, the one who loves devices, said that was his favourite part. They have photos from the trip on the wall and still talk about it all the time.

As for challenges: Anna was training for a 100-mile race, so every time she had to carry a child, she counted it as the strength training she didn't have time for. Her youngest started the trip scared of crabs and ended it holding them.

## Tips for tramping with kids {#tramping-with-kids}

### Start small

When her kids were babies, Anna took them on little hour-long loops, in the backpack, then toddling on their unsteady legs, around the base of Pirongia and other local walks. Barefoot in the bush, following the orange arrows.

* **Find local walks.** Ask other parents, look for your region's walks booklet, and search the DOC website for easy one to three hour walks.
* **Keep notes.** Anna kept a spreadsheet of walks with the distance and whether there was meant to be stock in the paddocks, because with little ones, you don't want to have to run from cattle.
* **Head for water.** Waterfalls, rivers, anywhere they can throw stones or dam a stream. Her kids will play for hours, so camp near water too.
* **Snacks.** Jet planes are a winner.
* **Keep them warm.** Thermals, polar fleece and a warm sleeping bag.
* **Sleeping spots matter.** Little ones want to be next to their parent.

### Let them lead

The slowest person goes in front, so Anna is always at the back. The kids set the pace, and if they find something interesting, everyone marvels at it. They navigate too. She'll let them wander off course a little, then ask when they last saw an orange arrow, and they work out how to get back to it. That sense of ownership makes a big difference.

## Girls' trips and finding your hiking people {#girls-trips}

Anna's girls' group started with friends from London, where they were more about the pub and a Sunday roast. As it turns out, they all love hiking. There are now 14 of them, and they've walked the Tora walk, been to Tutukākā and [Aotea Great Barrier](https://abigailhannah.nz/142-tramping-and-exploring-aotea-great-barrier-island-with-kristina-montgomerie/) and done the Banks Peninsula Track, with a private track in Kaikōura booked next.

Hiking with girlfriends is a completely different thing to solo. Solo, she's in her head processing things. With the girls, it's chatting, connecting with each other, and food. With so many of them, they usually book a walk out (they try hard to include anyone else who's there).

My first tramp with a group of girls was the [Milford Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/034-milford-track-nz-popular-walk/). A friend was going with her friends, and a week before they left I texted to ask if anyone had dropped out. Someone had, about an hour earlier. She was the only one I really knew, and by the end we were great friends. On the Milford you see the same people every day, and we were the loud group of girls.

Anna found many of her friends through Waikato Trail Runners and Walkers. She was scared to join a group, and a girlfriend nudged her into going. Her advice is to be brave and go along to social groups, because people with a shared passion want to welcome you and share it.

## Solo hiking tips and what's next {#solo-tips-whats-next}

For solo hiking, Anna says start with day walks. Everyone you meet will say hello and help you, and wherever she's been, however remote, she feels every person she's met would have helped her if she'd needed it. She feels safer in the hills than walking to her car in the city. She carries a PLB and her phone, and knows she has fire, food, shelter, warm clothes and a raincoat.

> "I want to live my life. I don't want to be too scared to live my life."

Next on her list: the Blue Lake 24-hour race again, then a run of January hikes with a girlfriend: [Rakiura](https://abigailhannah.nz/149-solo-expedition-on-rakiura-and-adhd-with-wild-boy-brando-yelavich/) and the [Kepler](https://abigailhannah.nz/003-hiking-the-kepler-track-in-fiordland-with-dua/), with the [Hump Ridge](https://abigailhannah.nz/136-pushed-to-her-limits-on-the-hump-ridge-track-and-slow-mindful-travel-with-helen-griffiths/) squeezed in if she can. She'd love to camp at Lake Marian and visit Aoraki Mount Cook National Park, and she has her eye on the UTA 100 in the Blue Mountains. Make hay while the sun shines.

When we recorded, I was leaving for Round the Mountain the next day with my husband, who studies outdoor education, and some of his friends, though only halfway round because of the weather. And Anna had never done the [Hooker Valley](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) Track. It's my top walk in New Zealand for effort to views, short and super low effort. Her pick for the same bang for buck is Diamond Lake near Wānaka, which is still on my list.
