This is the last of our three Slow Paddle episodes, and [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/) is back on the mic with me. We recorded [an episode before we even started](https://abigailhannah.nz/162-we-are-kayaking-from-cape-reinga-to-tauranga-to-raise-20-000-/) and [another one mid-paddle](https://abigailhannah.nz/178-one-month-into-a-1000km-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/), so if you haven't heard those, go back and start there.

This one picks up where we left off, in Tutukākā. And it was a lot. A drowned power bank (my fault), a pod of whales in the middle of a long, long crossing, my favourite day of the whole trip, two nights in real beds thanks to total strangers, and fog so thick we could barely see the island we were paddling to.

So we go day by day from Tutukākā to Mission Bay, including the big decision about the 45km crossing to the Coromandel and why we ended up calling it at Auckland. Then we look back: what surprised us, what we'd change, how we shared the journey online, and the plan for part two. It was the biggest thing we've ever done (high five, Isaac).

## A week off in Tutukākā {#tutukaka-break}

We arrived in Tutukākā on day 29 and spent seven days off the water there, sitting out a lot of wind and wet at a really nice holiday park. We'd had a lot of paddle days leading up to it, so I was really grateful to rest my body. And I ate a lot.

## Tutukākā to Peach Cove and a flooded front hatch {#tutukaka-to-peach-cove}

We left on day 35, carrying all our gear from the holiday park down to the boat ramp. The water was really nice and flat coming out, a relief after the stormy week before, and we headed south towards Pataua.

### A paddling friend for the day

A kayaker from the Whangārei area had messaged me on Facebook early in the trip, keen to join us when we came through. He met us on the water near Pataua and paddled with us for most of the day. It was one of our biggest days, almost 36km, and having someone new to yarn with was really really cool. He knew a little beach for lunch with an old gold mining tunnel on it.

He also gave me his gel seat pad. Some days we were in the boats for 10 or 11 hours without getting out, and my sit bones had got really sore (Isaac had his shoulder, I had my bum). The pad made a big difference.

After lunch came our first rain squalls of the trip: sudden wind that wasn't forecast, with chop from every direction. One hit as we were rounding a rocky point, and I gave Isaac a four on our panic meter. He came in closer, we went a bit wider, and we were fine.

Our friend left us at Ocean Beach, and the sun was setting as we came around Whangārei Heads to [Peach Cove](https://abigailhannah.nz/126-adventures-with-kids-and-a-peach-cove-rescue-with-renata-wiles/), where there's a DOC hut. We've walked Bream Head and Mount Manaia, and seeing those huge cliffs from the water was so cool. After days of one headland after the next, we could finally see Mangawhai Heads across the bay. We were getting close to Auckland.

### The power bank

This was my biggest mistake of the journey. At lunch I took my raincoat out of my front hatch and forgot to close it. I noticed a while later and didn't say anything, because everything was in dry bags, and waves had already been washing over my deck.

At Peach Cove, Isaac found my front hatch pretty much full of water (which explained why I'd been so slow). Dry bags won't keep things dry after hours sloshing around underwater. The casualty was our main power bank, which charges our phones, the Garmin and our VHF radios, and the only one that could charge off our solar panel. When I picked it up, water poured out of the cooling vents. Isaac was mad at me, which was 100% allowed. At least we had the hut to ourselves, with real mattresses and no tent to put up.

## Crossing Bream Bay with whales and a headwind {#bream-bay-crossing}

The plan was 20km in a straight line across Bream Bay towards Mangawhai Heads. Hugging the coast past Uretiti Beach and Langs Beach would have been 30km or more, so the straight line looked like a great idea at the time.

The wind picked up, three-quarters into our faces all day. At our average of about 4km an hour, 20km should take about five hours. It took seven. Into a headwind, a one-second break to grab your water hose leaves you dead stopped. I was blasting Taylor Swift, and Isaac put me on tow for the last stretch.

### A pod of whales

About halfway across, I spotted some big splashes. Isaac thought it was birds working up some fish. Birds can't splash that big. It was a pod of whales, at least three, with spouts and fins, and at one point one surfaced fairly close to my boat. It was the pick-me-up we needed in the middle of a slog.

### Backtracking to Waipū Cove

At the end of Bream Bay we stopped on a beach to get out of the wind. Around the corner towards Mangawhai was more headwind. Waipū Cove was back the way we'd come, but sheltered, with the wind behind us. It was our first backtrack of the trip, and I was so sick of the headwind. More weather was coming, so we googled the holiday parks: Waipū Cove was a dollar cheaper. Decision made.

We sat out the back of the surf to watch the sets for a while. Some waka ama paddlers from the local surf club said it wasn't too bad, and it was fine, with the holiday park right on the beach. And I got my redemption: after a couple of hours in the sun, the power bank powered right back up.

## Four days stuck at Waipū Cove {#waipu-cove}

Two days on the water, then four days off at Waipū Cove. Mangawhai is the geographical halfway point, and we hadn't even got there. We'd lost a whole week at Spirits Bay at the start, and ever since it had felt like playing catch-up. February and March tend to be more settled. This year they weren't for us. We were talking quite a bit to [Red](https://abigailhannah.nz/nz-kayak-circumnavigation/) at this stage, and he encouraged us to keep going.

### What's the point of our trip?

This was the first time we asked whether we'd make it. I suggested skipping Auckland altogether and driving to Port Jackson, at the top of the Coromandel. What was the point of the trip? Were we fixated on getting to Tauranga no matter the cost, or on finishing by Easter? What were our rules?

We also agreed that even if the trip ended there, we'd be so happy with what we'd done. To me, everything north of Whangārei feels like [Northland](https://abigailhannah.nz/176-a-locals-guide-to-northland-and-building-hiking-communities-with-hannah-whittaker/), and kayaking from Cape Reinga to Whangārei to Waipū Cove felt insane.

### Borderline days

In Tutukākā we knew we were off for seven days. At Waipū, every evening was "are we going tomorrow?" The water was flat, but the swell kept a big surf on the beach, and I was nervous about the launch. One morning Isaac checked at 5am and said no. By 8 it had dropped, but packing down takes us two hours, and a 10am start wouldn't leave enough daylight.

### Fewer places to camp

The closer we got to Auckland, the fewer places there were to camp. Mangawhai has a sandy bar you want to cross at high tide, and the tides didn't suit us. Pakiri is on the beach, so if the surf came up we could get stuck. Goat Island has no campground, and the education centres said no to a tent on the lawn. So the next stop had to be Leigh, 46km away.

## Waipū Cove to Leigh, our best day on the water {#waipu-to-leigh}

Waiting for the right day paid off. Isaac pushed me out, someone on the beach gave him a shove into the surf, and we were away early. It was flat and sunny, with no headwind or chop, and after four days of rest and good food we were in great spirits. We saw 22 little blue penguins, lots of fishermen and a guy paramotoring along the beach, and we stayed well off the coast, away from the surf. Before Goat Island I saw a brown thing floating that I took for seaweed, until it moved and dived. A turtle!

It was one of my favourite days of the whole trip. I felt so good: nothing hurt, nothing ached, I had a really good pace, and I was never on tow. And it was all places we knew, Langs Beach, Mangawhai, Te Arai, Pakiri, Goat Island, Leigh. You could feel yourself getting closer.

### A guest room in Leigh

We reached Leigh as the sun set into our eyes. There's no campground there, and our loose plan was a square of grass by the boat ramp and an early pack-up. Then a couple pulling their boat out after an afternoon's fishing got chatting, and when I asked about somewhere to pitch a tent, they said they had a guest room.

We hadn't slept in a bed in weeks. They gave us hot showers, fresh snapper and vegetables from their garden, and the latest night of the trip, talking. In the morning there were blueberry pancakes, the best breakfast of the whole trip, before they dropped us back at our boats. Legends.

## Leigh to Martins Bay via Kawau Island {#leigh-to-martins-bay}

We straight-lined it to the campground on Tāwharanui Peninsula, then kept going around the end and into the channel between Tāwharanui and Kawau Island. We hadn't checked the tides. An outgoing tide against the incoming wind and swell made standing waves, and we had to paddle hard to avoid them. The tracker showed we'd aimed diagonally across the channel and been pushed straight across.

Emotionally, this day was the opposite of the one before. The 46km day had caught up with us, and we pulled up on a beach on Kawau and sat there for a while without talking. With bad weather coming, we chose Martins Bay, on the mainland, to keep our options open.

### Another bed

A woman who'd been following the trip had texted to ask where we were aiming for. She's a kayaking instructor who has paddled the coast from the Coromandel to the far north in sections, so I was excited to see her. What I didn't know was that she'd booked a two-bedroom cabin at Martins Bay for us, and Isaac had kept it a secret. Another bed, two nights in a row! Another huge dinner and late night talking kayaking, too, so once again the biryani I'd been saving for a hard day stayed in the bag.

### A week back in Auckland

With bad weather settling in, we left the kayaks at Martins Bay and went home for the week. That was a hard call for me. I was scared it would break our expedition mindset, the tent every night and the dehydrated food. But paying to sleep in a tent in wind and rain made no sense.

I run my own business, and I'd told my clients I'd be back after Easter, so that week I got ahead on client work to buy us more time to get to Tauranga.

## The Kawau to Port Jackson crossing decision {#port-jackson-decision}

Back at Martins Bay, we had the next big decision. Head south through Auckland, or go out to Kawau and cross the top of the Hauraki Gulf straight to Port Jackson, at the top of the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/) Peninsula? That's 45km of open sea, with no islands in between and nowhere to stop. It would have saved us five whole days of paddling.

### My case for going

I'm very goal-oriented, and I like to finish what I start. I'd felt so good on our 46km day to Leigh, without stopping anywhere, that in my head I could hundred percent do 45km.

### Isaac's case for waiting

Isaac believed we could do it, too. He was more risk-averse through the whole trip, and his worry was the margins: it would only take one thing to go wrong. If the wind picked up and our pace dropped from 4km an hour to 3km an hour, we'd run out of daylight. If one of us got injured, the same. He knew we could paddle for 11 hours. What worried him was having enough light to land somewhere fairly remote at the top of the Coromandel.

### The forecast that decided it

We camped on Kawau, so if we didn't cross, we'd only gone a few kilometres out of our way. That night the crossing forecast looked beautiful. But Isaac had looked at the long-range forecast, and the week after was bad. We'd make it across, get stuck at the top of the Coromandel for a week, and be left with too few days to reach Tauranga, even paddling every day. Getting picked up from there would be hard, too, with quite a few roads closed.

That's when we realised we couldn't finish the trip. At Waipū we'd been problem-solving our way to Tauranga. On Kawau that night, we knew our original goal wasn't possible. It was gutting.

Plan A was the crossing. Plan B was heading south through Auckland, but the next spell of bad weather would only have got us to Waiheke. So we went with Plan C: finish at Mission Bay and call this part one of the Slow Paddle.

## Fog, an Olympian and the finish at Mission Bay {#finish-mission-bay}

The next morning was super foggy, and we could barely see the island we were paddling to. If we'd been crossing to Port Jackson, it would have been a compass bearing all day, in the middle of the ocean with no sky and no sea. So scary. Good call.

We island-hopped, then crossed to the end of Whangaparāoa Peninsula, which we couldn't see either. After a month on the water, I thought we'd seen everything, and it kept changing. That night we stayed with a good friend from uni in Long Bay. After Waipū Cove, Kawau was our only night in the tent. So spoiled.

### A send-off on the beach

At Long Bay an older couple came over, excited about our trip. I usually told people we'd started at Kawau, because Cape Reinga got such big reactions, but I needed a morale boost, so I told them. I was feeling a little bit proud. Then he told us he was an Olympic gold medal-winning kayaker, from before we were born. Okay, that's way cooler. He was so encouraging, and on what we knew was our last day, it was a very cool send-off.

### Into the city

The last morning was even foggier. We followed the North Shore down from Long Bay, with paddleboarders emerging from the mist. Coming back into civilisation was surreal: houses, cars, music, a surf life saving competition, swimmers and kayakers everywhere.

Crossing the Waitematā, a big tanker was coming, and I find their speed really hard to judge, so I got a bit stressed. It was fine. The harbour is so protected that we cruised along and reached Mission Bay before anyone came to meet us. The end of this massive journey, and nobody there but some Aucklanders chilling on the beach. Then came balloons and ice creams. A guy renting out sit-on-top kayaks asked where we'd come from, and when I said Cape Reinga, he thought I was joking.

### Humbled by the weather

It was nice to be done, and sad too. We'd wanted that feeling in Tauranga. The original finish date was Good Friday, five days after we arrived, and most of that week was bad weather anyway.

From Tutukākā to the finish, we had only seven paddle days. We'd started with a nice spreadsheet of how far we'd go each day, and the weather humbled us on day two. As Isaac says, bad weather in a sailboat, at least you can move. In a little five-metre bit of plastic, you're going backwards or upside down.

## What surprised us on the Slow Paddle {#what-surprised-us}

### Wild camping

Isaac expected more wild camps. He loves pitching a tent somewhere and cooking on a fire, and he carried a campfire pot the whole way and used it once. We had a handful of wild camps, and a lot more holiday parks than he expected, with hot showers and cookers, which was nice too.

For me, wild camps came with anxiety. Isaac's point is that the coast is public land, with public access, even if someone tells you it's a private beach. People can still get nasty about it. Our scouting was all on Google Earth and Google Maps, and a satellite picture can't tell you if there's somewhere flat for a tent. A campground meant certainty.

### What we're proud of

We didn't get sick or injured, and apart from the power bank we didn't break anything. We managed our food well, found good rhythms for camp life, and communicated through all the uncertainty. We were self-supported, too: no car following us, and apart from the lift home from Martins Bay, no support on land. We carried enough food, water and fuel to sit out an extra week on a beach.

On the last day, I told Isaac I could do a solo kayak trip. I still need to learn my low brace, high brace and roll, and nothing that long, but having confidence in my own mental fortitude felt really cool. A couple of weeks off the water, I'm second-guessing it.

### Decisions every single day

What surprised me most was the constant decision-making, as a team of two. I'm a bit of a "she'll be right, what's the worst that can happen" type, and Isaac is more grounded, and that balance was really interesting. I didn't expect how emotionally exhausting the decisions would be on top of being physically tired: where are we staying tonight, where does the tent go, and all the packing and unpacking. This was by far the biggest trip we've done, and for a first time, we did really well.

### How other paddlers do it

Talking to other kayakers afterwards, I learnt that on a lot of [kayaking expeditions](https://abigailhannah.nz/170-122-day-self-supported-north-island-sea-kayak-expedition-with-joel-buckley/), the weather decides everything, and people paddle in sections, out of order, chasing the good weather. We were both on board with finishing early, and it doesn't diminish what we did.

## Sharing the journey and Slow Paddle part two {#part-two}

### The Slow Paddle community

Our Slow Paddle community was a small group with our live tracker and daily updates. They knew I'd drowned the power bank, that we'd turned around in Bream Bay and that we'd seen whales. Having people that invested, cheering us on, was incredibly valuable.

On Instagram and Facebook, I posted a week or so behind. Strangers offered beds and supplies, and I felt bad replying that we'd already gone past. Strangers can also be mean. When I shared about getting stung by a bee and crying, some people weren't very sympathetic, and before the trip one guy told us bigger kayak trips had been done. Posting late protected our emotions on the water. I haven't decided how I'll share part two, but the Slow Paddle community is staying.

We've been raising money for Lifeline and Sustainable Coastlines, and the fundraiser is staying open.

### Part two

Isaac's wish for part two: do it slower. Part one only became slow because the weather took our paddle days, so when the sun shone we had to go hard. In his ideal world, it's 20km a day, with time for snorkelling, random lunches on little beaches and a campfire in the evening.

Finishing at Mission Bay was a bit strategic, because it's a good place to pick up again. From there we'll head east and cross from Waiheke to the Coromandel, which is only 20km, about the same as Bream Bay, then go up and around the Coromandel. The plan is to wait for a good weather window and go. When we recorded, the hope was next summer.

I wanted the Slow Paddle to be one and done, then sell the boats. Isaac was never going to sell his kayak. Now I'm excited, because the Coromandel is the section I was looking forward to the whole time, with so many coves and bays and a completely different coastline.
