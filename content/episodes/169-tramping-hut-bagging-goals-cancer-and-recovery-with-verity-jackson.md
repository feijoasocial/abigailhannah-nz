Verity has been listening to the podcast for years, mostly at night in huts (which is exactly where it's supposed to be listened to). So it was really really special to finally have her on.

She's been tramping for 21 years, she shares her trips as NZ Hiking Adventures, and hut bagging is absolutely her thing. Her goal this year started at 25 huts. Then it became 52. And then, in the middle of the year, she was diagnosed with vulval cancer and had two surgeries.

She still got to 52 by October.

I didn't know vulval cancer existed until this chat, and I learnt so much from Verity's openness about it. So this post covers how she got into tramping, why she loves her tramping club, what hut bagging is, her diagnosis and what she wishes she'd known, getting back on the tracks after surgery, her favourite seldom-visited huts and why we all need to look after our huts.

## How Verity got into tramping {#verity-story}

Verity didn't grow up in an outdoorsy family. She was raised in Auckland in the 80s, when it was go out and play and be home for dinner, and she played netball and hockey.

Her very first tramp was at 26, to Pinnacles Hut in the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/). They got views, and on the walk out there was torrential rain, with detours and bridges to get over the creeks. And she didn't hate it. Working out how they'd get through those crossings was really cool.

I have a grudge against the Pinnacles, because I didn't get a view, so I've booked to go back and stay at the hut. My own first multi-day hike, Pirongia in winter, was muddy and miserable, and it didn't put me off either.

## Joining a tramping club in Mid Canterbury {#tramping-club}

At 30, Verity moved from Auckland to Mid Canterbury on her own. She didn't know anybody and had done no South Island tramping, so she joined the local [tramping club](https://abigailhannah.nz/180-hiking-the-cascade-saddle-route-tramping-clubs-and-influencers-with-alice-milne/), even though she'd always assumed clubs were full of old people. In her words:

> "I'd always thought, oh, I can never join a tramping club, they're full of old people. But you know what, I've had the most amazing adventures with the club and I've made some really awesome friends through there."

Through the club's trip schedule she got to know her local area. [Mount Somers](https://abigailhannah.nz/hiking-mount-somers/) and the Ashburton Lakes are where she's spent the most time. The club also took her off track, and she credits a lot of her map reading and navigation to its members. Without it, she thinks she'd have stuck to formed tracks and played it safe.

In her first few years, in her early 30s, she could barely keep up with members in their 60s. She remembers hoping she'd be that fit and active at 60. Some of the members she makes reels with are over 55, and they have an absolute ball. Her advice is to break down that perception: sign up, go along and meet people.

I felt the same on a koha-based women's kayaking weekend, where I was by far the youngest and the club women were super fit. We're losing that multi-generational connection, and a club is such a cool way to get it back.

## What hut bagging is {#what-is-hut-bagging}

In its most basic sense, Verity says, hut bagging is visiting a hut and keeping a record of the huts you've been to. Then it becomes personal. Some people only count a hut if they stay in it, others need to touch it and take a photo. For Verity it's a mixture, as long as she's been physically right there.

The Hutbagger website is her go-to. It lists DOC huts, private huts and derelict huts, with a map that shows huts that have been removed or replaced. Visit two versions of the same hut over the years and that counts as two. It keeps your total and a score, and when we recorded, Verity's total was 351 huts, with a score of 292, because a lot of her huts are derelict or private.

I set up a Hutbagger profile after my chat with [Ben](https://abigailhannah.nz/118-hut-bagging-and-visiting-over-500-doc-huts-with-benjamin-pigott/), who has bagged over 500 huts, and then added nothing to it. Before recording I got a notebook out to count. A couple of [Great Walks](https://abigailhannah.nz/10-great-walks-in-10-days/) and four nights on the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/) ticked off a few, then I kept remembering others, like walking past Pinnacles Hut and Routeburn Flats on the [Routeburn](https://abigailhannah.nz/009-solo-travel-routeburn-track-emily/). I was nowhere near Verity.

## From 25 huts to 52 in a year {#fifty-two-huts}

Verity first heard about hut bagging through the club and jumped on the bandwagon, back when it meant keeping a manual list.

Last year she didn't set a goal, and when she counted up at the end of the year she'd bagged 21. So this year she set a goal of 25, about three or four huts a month, allowing for winter's shorter days. By the end of April she was close. When she mentioned it at work, a colleague suggested 52, one for every week of the year.

She redid the maths: still only three or four huts a month. So she went back to Hutbagger and the Topo map, looked at the club's trip schedule and her calendar, and filled every weekend and long weekend. By the end of May she was on track.

Then her year turned upside down. She still reached hut number 52 in October, with tramping friends she's had for years through the club. Then she felt a bit underwhelmed, because it was only October and she had all these trips planned, so she kept going. Now it's less about the number and more about the journeys and the destinations.

## A vulval cancer diagnosis mid-year {#cancer-diagnosis}

Verity has lichen sclerosus, an autoimmune skin condition that mostly affects women and affects the vulva. It causes thinning of the skin, white patches, itching and pain, and it's often misdiagnosed as thrush. One of its complications is that it can lead to vulval cancer.

She has annual checkups with a gynaecologist, and in May the gynaecologist saw a lesion and took a biopsy. While she waited two weeks for the results, she went on a midweek overnight trip with a friend to Maitland Hut, near the top of Lake Ōhau in the Ruataniwha area of the Mackenzie. It's not a popular track, and there was so much windfall. Inner turmoil, outer turmoil.

The results came back as precancerous cells, and she had surgery to remove them with a margin of clear skin. The day before her follow-up appointment, the clinic asked her to bring a support person. That's when she was told they'd found a tumour: vulval cancer.

A multidisciplinary panel reviewed her case, and next came a CT scan, a lymph node biopsy and a second, bigger surgery. The first surgery was in early June, with four weeks of recovery. The second was in early August, with four to six.

Through all of it, she was determined not to cast her plans aside. Fifty-two huts was something to look forward to. When they were choosing a date for the second surgery, she told her oncologist she had a tramp planned for the end of July and she was going. He's a climber and a tramper, so he got it: they were looking at early August anyway, and she shouldn't have to give up tramping.

## What Verity wishes she'd known {#what-she-wishes}

Everything, she says. She was diagnosed with lichen sclerosus about 10 years ago, and it's more common in postmenopausal women. Nobody told her it could lead to vulval cancer until a new gynaecologist mentioned it a few years ago, and that shook her. Her oncologist told her she was far too young for this, because vulval cancer is more common in women in their 60s and 70s.

Neither condition is talked about much, there isn't a lot of research, and there's no pre-screening. Her advice:

* **Know your own body.** If you're being treated for thrush and the treatments aren't working or it keeps coming back, challenge your GP: could this be lichen sclerosus?
* **Check every month.** Do your first check to get a baseline, so you'll notice changes.
* **Go and ask.** The best outcome is that your GP says it's nothing to worry about, and if there is something, it's caught early.

As she puts it:

> "just like you do a monthly breast exam, you should actually check your vulva every month as well."

We so often have to be our own best advocates when it comes to health. And to the men listening, don't think you can tap out: men's health issues exist too, so check your bits. If anything in any part of your body feels off, get it checked.

## Back on the tracks after surgery {#back-tramping}

Verity spent two nights in hospital after her second surgery, and on discharge she asked when she could go tramping again (they fully expected it). One risk after the surgery is rupturing your stitches, so she did everything she was told.

Two weeks later, she went to Quail Island in Lyttelton Harbour with a couple of friends from the club, one of them also on a hut bagging mission. You catch a ferry over from Lyttelton, and the circuit around the island is only five kilometres. Halfway up the hill from the wharf she had pain in her groin where lymph nodes had been removed, and she carried on, because she knew that area had healed.

A work trip and a four-hour, 12-kilometre tramp near Lake Coleridge to bag a couple more huts followed. Then came significant bruising and more pain, and her oncologist pointed out that most women at that stage are managing half-hour walks. She'd probably burst a vein. It wasn't serious, but it meant a longer recovery. Setback after setback. Then strong nor'westers wiped out the plans she had locked in for September.

There were little wins too. A friend's 60th birthday trip to the West Coast changed to a few days near Ōmarama, and instead of maybe one hut, she bagged six.

### The Whitcombe trip and a book

After her first surgery, Verity wasn't in a happy place. Would she be able to keep tramping and bagging huts? Then came the trip up the Whitcombe Valley with [Renee](https://abigailhannah.nz/160-hut-etiquette-in-doc-huts-and-exploring-the-whitcombe-valley-with-renee-cadigan/), the one she'd refused to cancel. They rarely talked about what was going on with her, and those couple of nights changed her perspective completely. Her passion and drive came back, for the huts, the content and sharing her story.

She came home and started writing a book, and kept writing through two sleepless nights in hospital. It covers 12 months, from the biopsy in May through the surgeries and her hiking adventures, and next May will be the final chapter. She hopes it inspires people, and maybe adds a few huts to their lists.

I think we don't realise how much our outdoor pursuits do for our mental wellbeing until an injury or illness takes them away. Even through all that adversity, Verity found glimmers to hold on to.

## Memorable huts: Pearson, Staircase and Green Point {#memorable-huts}

Maitland Hut and Rapid Creek Hut are memorable for all the wrong reasons, Verity says. Here are three for the right ones.

### Pearson Hut, Kirkliston Range

On that Ōmarama birthday trip, her friend found Pearson Hut by poring over Topo maps and Hutbagger, as you do. It's behind Lake Waitaki in the Waitaki District, on the Kirkliston Range (neither of us had heard of it). They drove from Ōmarama to Kurow and up the Hakataramea Valley Road.

* **Access.** You go through farmland on a marked easement, which closes for lambing and calving, then follow a four-wheel drive track almost all the way.
* **The tops.** The range is dotted with rock tors she wished she'd had time to explore.
* **The hut.** From a knob near the end, look to the right and you'll see it below. They dropped straight down to it: a cute little hut in a beautiful, sheltered, warm tussock valley.
* **Take a tent.** There was a lot of rodent sign, and at least one mattress had been nibbled.

### Staircase Hut, off Mount Misery Road

Staircase Hut is out the back of Herbert, accessed off Mount Misery Road, which is ironic, because it's a quirky little hut. It's private, so you need to contact the group and book to stay. It has six bunks with very little headroom, a guitar, a sunny clearing and, when they visited, clematis flowering in the trees. You can do an in-and-out or a circuit, and either way it's relentless: lots of ups and lots of downs.

### Green Point Hut, Lake Pūkaki

Verity's favourite huts are the ones off the beaten track, and living in Mid Canterbury puts her right in the middle of the South Island with so many within reach. One she'd happily put on repeat is Green Point Hut, at the top of Lake Pūkaki on the opposite side from Mount Cook. Trampers rarely visit, but it's popular with hunters and packrafters, and hunters can fly in by helicopter. The hunters there were so nice and accommodating.

That's the bug: you get to a hut, pull out the map and wonder what's in the next valley.

## Looking after our huts {#love-our-huts}

Our hut system is world class. Every overseas guest says so, and I feel so deeply that we need to protect our backcountry huts.

Verity suggests FMC's Love Our Huts campaign, where you register for a hut and go and give it some love: cleaning, tidying and checking everything out. When she started tramping, DOC did all the track work. Now, she says, if we want these huts, everyone who uses them, trampers or hunters, needs to take some responsibility.

Her cancer changed how she thinks about this too. For years she'd put off tracks because she had plenty of time.

> "I've had this real shift that I don't want to put things off anymore. I'm able, I'm fit. I want to go there. I'm going to make a plan to go there."

She thinks about huts the same way. Huts get sick if they're neglected, or if people leave rubbish expecting someone else to pick it up. We need to respect them, for us and for the next generation.

## West Coast huts and 400 next year {#whats-next}

Verity started tramping in the North Island, with Pinnacles, Lake [Waikaremoana](https://abigailhannah.nz/175-starting-tramping-in-her-40s-and-lake-waikaremoana-as-a-family-with-lois-baldwin/), the [Tongariro Crossing](https://abigailhannah.nz/172-hiking-the-tongariro-alpine-crossing-and-tongariro-northern-circuit-with-ben-boland/), [Peach Cove](https://abigailhannah.nz/126-adventures-with-kids-and-a-peach-cove-rescue-with-renata-wiles/), Cape Brett and Pirongia. She'd go back to visit, but she loves the South Island too much to live anywhere else.

Bagging gets harder once you've ticked off the easy huts. There are still some in Mid Canterbury, and tagging along on hunting trips means she can drive in to some of them. On her wall is a poster of all the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/) huts, with a star sticker on each one she's bagged, and none on the right side of the map. There's a reason for that: they're not easy. So watch this space for some challenging West Coast missions.

She has the skills. Years ago she did a seven-day mountaineering course at [Mount Cook](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/), back when she wanted to climb it. Priorities changed, and summer alpine trips are still very much on.

When we recorded, she had 10 more huts planned for the year. The goal in the back of her mind is 400, and with the adventures already locked in, she reckons she'll go well over.

My wishlist this summer is the classics: Brewster, [Mueller](https://abigailhannah.nz/028-mueller-hut-backyard-travel-family/) and French Ridge. My conclusion: I need to get hut bagging.
