It had been a couple of weeks since I'd recorded, so I was feeling a bit rusty. And it was Renee's first ever podcast, so we were both feeling a bit weird. It turned into one of those chats that gave me goosebumps.

Renee lives on the West Coast of the South Island and works as a process worker at a dairy company. A few years before we recorded, a scooter accident fractured her face and flipped her whole life upside down. Walking became her healing journey, and then she started taking her two young boys with her. Now hiking is their thing.

So we talk about that recovery and the mental health side of it (we recorded during Mental Health Awareness Week, so it felt right). Then her first overnight hike with the boys, up Mount Brown, which took seven hours, lots of breaks and a few tears. Plus Mueller Hut in the snow, a plan B at Lake Kaurapataka, rugged West Coast huts, and her very practical tips for getting kids out tramping. I came away wanting to high five every kid I pass on a track.

## Walking as a healing journey {#healing-journey}

Renee grew up on a farm. Back then, kids were left to go and do whatever they wanted, and she's sometimes surprised they're still alive. If you stayed inside, you got a list of jobs, so the best thing to do was disappear outside for the entire day and come back when you were hungry.

Hiking came later. She'd done a few walks with friends and at school camp, then she and a friend found out there were a couple of huts out the back of where she lives. They got all the gear and, in their words the whole way, had no idea.

Then came the accident. Renee came off a moped and fractured her face, and her whole life got flipped upside down. She'd always been all go, go, go, especially with kids. After the accident she felt in constant competition with herself, trying to stay the person she was.

She didn't realise head injuries can affect you so badly mentally. She had delayed concussion: she was sore and pretty broken physically, but mentally fine at first. Then about a month later, her brain switched right off. Depression started coming in, the headaches got really bad, and she had to learn to listen and stop.

[Walking](https://abigailhannah.nz/052-walking-wellbeing/) became her way through. Out on a track, away from the hustle and bustle of normal life, she could concentrate on one foot in front of the other:

> "it was the one way that I actually managed to silence my brain … I just wanted to challenge myself even more"

She still has setbacks. But she knows her triggers now, and she's learned to really listen to herself. In some ways it's worked out better: she's learned to do things at a better pace, and she really enjoys the time she has with her children.

Renee takes her hat off to everyone who gets up every day and chooses to keep going, and does the best they can. We all carry different volumes of what we're going through, and she reckons everyone should be proud that they're still here. It's not acknowledged enough.

## Getting outdoors with kids on one wage {#outdoors-with-kids}

Renee was a stay-at-home mum when she decided to take the boys out walking and see how they went. It became a win-win. With a plan and a destination, she doesn't have to think too much, there are no phones or house chores, and all children thrive outdoors, so she finds it easier to parent outside than inside. Being in the moment together made her realise how amazing her children are, and how amazing she was for being out there.

Friends who are mums ask her how she does it. Her answer: once you get started it's addictive, and it's a lot easier than people realise.

With rising living costs on a single wage, she thought she needed to own a van to travel. Then she realised she could fit a mattress in the back of her Honda Fit, and off they went [car camping](https://abigailhannah.nz/044-nz-station-wagon-road-trip/). You can see amazing places for the price of petrol and a campsite. Her view is that we're told to work hard and enjoy life in retirement, when we might be too old to enjoy it, so stop putting up barriers and go and do it now.

## Mount Brown with two young boys {#mount-brown}

Mount Brown is right at Renee's back door. In November 2022 she picked it for her first overnight hike with the boys, partly because there's a hut up there with water, so she wouldn't have to carry as much.

She overpacked everything: so many changes of clothes for the boys, sleeping bags like carrying bricks, and food for five days on an overnight hike. She used hardly any of it.

Most people take about four hours to get up. It took them seven, with lots of breaks, lots of tears and a lot of wondering why she'd picked this as their first hike. A lot of Mount Brown is tree-root climbing, steep enough that you're on all fours. Renee kept smiling the whole way up so the kids would keep going, because she knew if she lost it, they'd all be crying on the side of the mountain.

Then they came up over the tussock and there was the hut. The boys' reaction is the moment she knew this was something she wanted to do a lot more of, and the whole struggle to keep them motivated disappeared. A couple at the hut were amazed the kids had made it up, and gave them some chocolate.

Everything up there was fun to the boys: setting up the tent, cooking dinner, making their beds. That evening they watched the sun go down over the coast, right out to the ocean, and Renee felt this:

> "I was just so so incredibly proud of the fact that my two boys have never hiked before and they've just climbed this amazing mountain and here we were sitting up there watching the sunset"

Even so, Renee doesn't recommend Mount Brown for families starting out. Try something shorter and nice and flat first. She rates [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/) as a great walk for that.

## Mount Brown Hut, the tent and the way down {#mount-brown-hut}

Mount Brown Hut is a little orange hut up in the tussock with four bunks. When they went, there were no bookings, first come first served, which is the other reason Renee's pack was so heavy. She carried a tent and pitched it just outside the hut.

She's more comfortable in a tent with kids anyway. You don't want them woken by late arrivals, or waking other people when they get up early, and in a tent they can be as feral as they want.

It was really windy all night, and it was her first night in that tent, so she was worried they'd wake up with it blowing halfway down the mountain. It held, and it's been her ride-or-die tent ever since.

Going down was a lot easier. They'd already climbed part of Tuhua, which is similar, and the boys had slid down on their bums, so they did the same on Mount Brown. It was mostly Renee's knees suffering under the heavy pack. At the bottom they played in the creek while they waited for their lift. Both days were beautiful.

## Tramping on the West Coast {#west-coast-huts}

Renee isn't one for lots of research. She looks at the huts on a map and goes. A lot of West Coast huts are pretty rugged to get to, so when a track time says four hours, she estimates six. There are a lot of slips, it's easy to lose the track, and you'll do a bit of scrambling. Plan your time wisely, because these backcountry tracks aren't very well maintained.

The bonus is that you'll generally get the huts to yourself. Nearly every hut she's been to, they've had to themselves, and that's one of her favourite things.

The weather turns pretty quickly. They always carry a Garmin, which gives them the weather for where they are, and they always check before they go. If it's going to rain for a couple of days, it's not worth going.

My first time down the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/), in the van on my first South Island trip, we went days without seeing the sun. We had to go back to Hokitika Gorge for redemption, and in summer it was the beautiful blue it's supposed to be, after the murky brown we got the first time. I made a reel about it, and the comments were scathing (people were sure it was filters).

## Mueller Hut in the snow {#mueller-hut-snow}

[Mueller Hut](https://abigailhannah.nz/028-mueller-hut-backyard-travel-family/) is one of Renee's favourites, a trip with one of the boys. They planned to camp up there for the night.

The climb to [Sealy Tarns](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) was great because of the steps, and they stopped there for lunch. For the last hundred metres they were climbing in snow, with no spikes, and Renee started to think they should turn back. Then a man walked past with no spikes either, and because the snow was soft, they weren't slipping too badly. They made it to the hut, and it was amazing: they could hear avalanches happening around them.

Then someone up there said the weather was turning quicker than normal, with strong winds due in the morning. Renee reckons she'd probably be fine on her own, but with her son and how steep it was, she made the call to go back down that day.

Because of the snow, they slid down on their bums almost the whole way to Sealy Tarns, past everyone, who told them they had the right idea. It ended up being the highlight of the day. They were soaked, so the spare clothes came in handy, and afterwards they soaked in the hot pools at Tekapo.

I did Sealy Tarns two weeks before we recorded, and we wondered about popping up to Mueller Hut but didn't feel like dealing with it. Next time, I'll slide down on my bum.

One thing Renee finds incredible when she hikes with kids is how people encourage them. Strangers celebrate the boys being out there, and it makes it that much more special:

> "giving them that praise like I just couldn't thank those people enough who have all interacted with my kids and celebrated them."

## Lake Kaurapataka, the plan B {#lake-kaurapataka}

Renee jokes that they plan a destination and most of the time never make it. Lake Kaurapataka was a plan B. The plan was the Otehake hot pools, where you can camp and soak for the evening. But the Taramakau River was high, and with the two boys she took the flood route, which she didn't realise would add three hours of up and down. Back on the main track it was a long walk up a 4WD track, and the boys did so well.

Time was getting on, so at the lake she made the call to camp. They had it to themselves, with wood already stacked for a fire. She surprised the boys with marshmallows, and they had a big campfire and talked about the stars.

Renee was really disappointed not to get to the hot pools. The boys were happy to be out there, and that made her check herself: the kids were happy, they were still out there doing it, and it was still fun.

She also admits that hiking with kids is stressful sometimes. You get tired and frustrated, and she sometimes yells when they're fighting and no one's listening. On that trip she made a conscious effort to stay positive and patient, and around the campfire one of the boys pointed out she hadn't yelled at them once. It does pay to stay positive and patient, even when you're dying on the inside.

## Renee's tips for hiking with kids {#hiking-with-kids-tips}

* **Break up the distances.** Do a big hike, then a few short ones, and another big one about every two months. Big hike after big hike and they'll lose interest fast and think every hike is hard.
* **Get them involved.** Her boys pick their own snacks and help plan and pack, so they're excited and on board before you even leave.
* **Let them set the breaks** and walk at the youngest's pace, so there's no rush.
* **Carry a tent.** With your accommodation on your back, it's fine if you don't make the hut. You can camp and stop worrying about time.
* **Make a deal on carrying.** Renee carries her youngest on her shoulders, sitting on top of her pack. Their deal is he gets carried on the flats and climbs the hills himself. On a trip to Kelly Saddle she tried a friend's lightweight clip-on child carrier, and wished she'd known about it sooner.

Her pack is a lot lighter these days, too, with her base weight down to nine kilos.

And if you see a kid out on the track, give them a high five. Renee highly recommends it, because it makes their day, and they're awesome for being out there. I'll definitely be doing that.
