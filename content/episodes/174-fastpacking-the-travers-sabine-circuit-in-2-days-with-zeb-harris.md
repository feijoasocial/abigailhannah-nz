It had been quite a while since I'd had a fastpacking episode on the podcast, and Zeb came recommended by one of my guests, who basically said we need to hear from him because they love his YouTube videos. So here he is.

Zeb makes YouTube videos about his adventures and the gear he loves, as Backcountry Kiwi. He's a keen fastpacker and tramper, does a bit of climbing and mountaineering, and has been doing a lot of trail running lately. And he walked me through fastpacking the Travers-Sabine Circuit in Nelson Lakes in two days, on a weekend, around a full-time job. I had my topo map open so I could follow along (and I really really want to do that circuit now).

So this post covers what fastpacking is, the Travers-Sabine day by day with all his side trips, his ultralight sleep system, how he paces himself and gets through the hard bits, the trips he'd suggest for your first go, and his take on the criticism fastpacking gets.

## How Zeb got into the outdoors {#zeb-story}

Zeb grew up in the Waikato, on a lifestyle block in a rural community, so he was always outdoors. The local scout group was small, only a few of them, but they got out on all sorts of activities, some of them pretty wild, and that grew his love for the outdoors.

He lost the passion a little in his early teens, then found it again, and at 16 he went on his first solo camping trip, because it was something he really wanted to do. Since then, it's been bigger and better trips.

## What fastpacking is {#what-is-fastpacking}

Zeb describes fastpacking as a mix of trail running and hiking, typically with quite a lightweight load. For him it grew out of his tramping trips. He enjoyed moving fast in the mountains, covering a lot of distance and doing big days. Then he fell down the rabbit hole of ultralight gear and ultralight hiking, got into trail running, and it all came together.

These days you can have a pretty light pack without a lot of skills or knowledge, purely from the modern gear available, which opens up tramping for people who never enjoyed carrying heavy packs. He sees fastpacking as more accessible because of it, and still a newer, more niche term.

I'm in that boat. I got a light pack, an Osprey (which model is a question for my husband, but I can tell you it's blue), and it has made tramping so much more enjoyable.

### Why move fast?

Zeb likes both kinds of trip. He's all for lounging in the sun on a long lunch break, or getting to camp early and hanging out. He also loves pushing really big days, up early and to bed late. A lot of the appeal is time: he works full time, and taking a multi-day trip and condensing it into a weekend means he can fit in trips he'd otherwise need time off for. It's much harder, and it gives him a lot of satisfaction.

I did a version of this myself this summer. My husband and I did a day walk on the [Routeburn Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/009-solo-travel-routeburn-track-emily/) to Routeburn Falls Hut, with a long lunch on a bluebird day, and we had more daylight and more left in the tank, so we pushed on to Lake Harris and back. If you'd told me at the start of the day I'd do a 20k day, I'd have said absolutely not.

## The Travers-Sabine Circuit in a weekend {#travers-sabine-weekend}

The Travers-Sabine Circuit is in [Nelson Lakes National Park](https://abigailhannah.nz/016-nelson-lakes-mel-white/), which Zeb highly recommends if you haven't been: a really beautiful part of the country. The circuit covers about 80 kilometres, and he added a few side trips to [bag a few huts](https://abigailhannah.nz/118-hut-bagging-and-visiting-over-500-doc-huts-with-benjamin-pigott/) along the way, which made it longer. His goal was to do it over two days, on his weekend, and smash out a load of kilometres. It was coming into summer, so he knew he'd have plenty of daylight.

Zeb is based in Christchurch, about four and a half hours' drive from St Arnaud, where the circuit pretty much starts. After work on the Friday he packed his gear, managed a couple of hours' sleep, got up at 2.30am and drove up, starting on very little sleep after a full week of work. He says it was definitely worth it.

## Travers-Sabine day one: Coldwater, Angelus and Upper Travers {#day-one}

Zeb walked the circuit clockwise. You can start towards Lakehead Hut or Coldwater Hut, and since he'd already been to Lakehead, he headed for Coldwater to bag it. He started walking at 7am. The first section runs right along the lake, really pretty and quite easygoing, so he made really good time.

On fastpacking trips he tries not to dilly dally. Short breaks only (a layer, a bite, filtering water), because long ones add up. He eats on the go, with easy snacks like lollies, bars, nuts and pretzels.

### The side trip to Angelus Hut

From Coldwater he took the Cascade Track up to [Angelus Hut](https://abigailhannah.nz/039-angelus-hut-dylan-arthur/). It isn't technically on the Travers-Sabine, so he did it as an in and out back to the main route. It had been on his list for quite a while, the weather was amazing, and it was the biggest highlight of day one. He'd have loved to stay up there, and he definitely wants to go back and spend the night.

We'd looked at kayaking to Coldwater Hut this summer, since it's right at the end of the lake, then heading up to Angelus on the same track. But Angelus was fully booked, and I'm always a last-minute booker. Snooze, you lose.

### Hopeless Hut, John Tait Hut and a 50k day

Coming down from Angelus, he was really happy with his time. He worked his way up the valley, with another side trip to Hopeless Hut before John Tait Hut. Around then his stomach started playing up, and he struggled a bit through that section.

By the time he reached John Tait Hut, it was nearing evening. He had a break, chatted to people in the hut, then pushed on to Upper Travers Hut for the night, getting there around 8pm, just before dark. The day ended up being about 13 hours and 50 kilometres exactly. When his watch said 49.9 something, he wandered around in circles for a bit to hit 50.

## Zeb's ultralight sleep system {#sleep-system}

Zeb camped outside near Upper Travers Hut, and slept well, so well that he slept in. His setup:

* **A tarp shelter he sewed himself**, from material ordered from overseas. It weighs only 170 grams.
* **A closed-cell foam mat**, the accordion kind, cut down to six panels so it covers his torso from his shoulders to about his waist. That's enough insulation, especially in summer. His legs go on a ground sheet or his pack.
* **A very lightweight sleeping bag.**
* **An inflatable pillow.** That's the one luxury item he allows himself.

I used to be the same, sleeping on my clothes in a stuff sack. Then every time my husband brought a pillow I'd steal it, so now we both have pillows.

## Travers-Sabine day two: Travers Saddle and the Sabine Valley {#day-two}

He got started later than he'd intended, and straight out of Upper Travers Hut is the climb to the Travers Saddle. It's really quite steep, a really beautiful climb, and a good way to wake the legs up. As you pop over the top, you get a big, expansive view of the Sabine Valley below.

Zeb had been there once before, walking [Te Araroa](https://abigailhannah.nz/043-te-araroa-thru-hike-zoe/), and seeing that valley open up below him will always be a big highlight. He sat up there for a little while, then got a move on.

The drop into the Sabine Valley, down to West Sabine Hut, is steep. He wouldn't want to come up it the other way. You cross a couple of little boulder fields, get back down to the bushline, then go through the bush to the valley floor.

### Along the Sabine Valley

At West Sabine Hut the sun came out, so he lounged for a bit and chatted to people. Then he made really good time along the valley floor, which is relatively flat and follows the river.

Before Sabine Hut there's a really cool, gorgy section of river with lovely blue water. Zeb always wants to swim, and loves jumping into any cold water he can find, but on this trip the goal was to keep moving, so he skipped it.

He also says the side trip to Blue Lake is very worth it, because it's some of the clearest fresh water in the world.

### A sore foot and the last 20 kilometres

Just before Sabine Hut, right on the edge of the lake, Zeb hurt his foot. It was quite sore, and he still had about 20 kilometres to go, so he had to keep moving. From Sabine Hut it's a straight shot over to Speargrass Hut, then back to where he started.

By Speargrass he was definitely feeling it, and to make it more interesting, it was raining quite hard. He remembers taking a moment at the hut, filling in his intentions in the book and getting his mind back into it. Leaving Speargrass, he found a bit of a second wind, picked up the pace, and ran the last section back to the car park because he was so eager to get out.

Then he drove back to Christchurch the same night, getting home after midnight, and went to work the next day tired and limping. Apart from the foot and his stomach, everything else went to plan. The one thing he skipped was Cupola Hut, another in and out past John Tait Hut, because he was too tired by then. That one's for another time.

## Fastpacking tips: pacing, poles and the mental side {#fastpacking-tips}

### Run the downhills, jog the flats, hike the uphills

Zeb's general principle for fastpacking is to run the downhills, jog the flats and hike the uphills. It keeps you moving and stops you tiring yourself out too early.

That makes it sound so much more achievable to me. A lot of people can jog the flats and run downhill.

He uses Black Diamond Carbon Distance Z-Poles, very lightweight collapsible poles. They're a big help on the downhills, saving his knees, and on the uphills they give him that extra bit of oomph, because he can use his arms as well as his legs.

### The mental side

Zeb expects every one of these trips to be a struggle. In his words:

> "Whenever I do these trips, I am expecting it to be like a physical and mental struggle. … I would say out of everything, the mental side of it is probably the hardest for me."

He gets through by telling himself he can do this, because he's done hard things before. He thinks ahead to how he'll feel after finishing, knowing he'll look back and be grateful he kept going.

I use the same trick when I'm [sea kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/182-finishing-a-two-month-slow-paddle-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/) in difficult conditions. When it feels really scary, I remind myself I've done other scary things before.

### Fastpacking your own way

Everyone tramps differently, Zeb says, and the same goes for fastpacking. You might run one section of a trip, or get to camp quicker so you can lounge around for longer.

> "I don't always think fast packing has to be like doing the absolute biggest distance possible."

## First fastpacking trips and lighter gear {#getting-started}

### The St James Walkway

In the South Island, especially around Christchurch or the upper South Island, Zeb thinks the [St James Walkway](https://abigailhannah.nz/113-tramping-the-st-james-walkway-and-an-unexpected-encounter-with-olivia-jacinda-and-sarah-blair/) is an excellent first fastpacking trip. It's well maintained, well marked and mostly through valleys, with a good network of huts to time your days around.

### The Kaimai North-South Track

In the North Island, Zeb fastpacked the North-South Track in the [Kaimai Ranges](https://abigailhannah.nz/048-kaimai-mamaku/). It runs from the highway over the Kaimai Range to the Karangahake Gorge near Waihī. It's about 80 kilometres, and most people take four to seven days. He did it in two. It's tougher, more rugged with more elevation, and yes to both fallen trees and mud, but it's an excellent trip.

### Getting started

His tips:

* **Pick a trip you've done before** and know well, and try doing it in less time.
* **Use the hut network.** Zeb prefers to camp, but huts mean you can carry a bit less and still have a comfortable shelter between bigger days.
* **Shave weight where you can.** Lightweight gear can be expensive, but you can think about how many clothes you carry, what you cook with, and the extra plates and cups.
* **Go for a smaller pack.** It's less cumbersome when you're moving fast, and it forces you to think more about what you bring.

That last one works for me. If it doesn't fit, it stays, although my stretchy side pouches don't help.

Zeb's fastpacking base weight is around three kilos, not including food or water. He's trying to get a little lighter. But he won't recommend his setup to everyone: there's a line between going light and safety, and at some point you have to be comfortable with the amount of risk you're willing to take. He's dialled his kit in over a long time and knows its limitations.

When he tramps at a normal pace, he takes a bigger pack, a bigger mat, a bigger tent and a few more luxuries. That's how he tramps with his partner, and it's a nice change of pace.

## Trail running and his first ultra {#ultra-running}

Zeb has been trail running for quite a while, and the distances kept getting bigger. He wanted to push himself, and signing up for a race gave him a goal, so he gave himself a 16-week training plan and ran his first ultra. It was an amazing experience.

When we recorded, his next race was the Motatapu in Wānaka in March, which is supposed to be a pretty tough one. He's been running four to six times a week, and his most recent big weekend was a variation of the Minchin Pass route in [Arthur's Pass](https://abigailhannah.nz/153-arthurs-pass-and-three-attempts-at-mount-philistine-with-amelia-hayes/), good training for elevation and distance.

## Solo trips, criticism and what's next {#whats-next}

### Why the big trips are solo

A lot of Zeb's trips are solo, and his bigger fastpacking trips all have been. It's his chance to push himself, and he wouldn't want to hold anyone back, or feel held back himself. On group trips with friends or his partner, he's more than happy at a relaxed pace, enjoying the scenery and lounging in the sun.

### Listener questions

A listener asked what inspires him. He loves being out in the mountains, where he can clear his head and be himself, discover new things about himself, and push his body or take time to relax.

Another asked what extra considerations he takes when fastpacking. Mostly food and water (he eats a lot more, a lot more often), plus bailout routes and places to camp if he's slower than expected.

### The criticism

Fastpacking the way Zeb does it goes hand in hand with ultralight hiking, and most of the negativity is about safety: having less gear to hunker down with if you get in trouble. He accepts that it adds a little risk, and he factors in what happens if something goes wrong.

The other debate is what's the point of moving so quickly if you don't stop to smell the roses. His answer:

> "I get to see more than most people would on their whole weekend … Although I don't get to spend as long at these places, I would say I get to see a lot and cover a lot of ground."

My take is that people experience the outdoors in different ways. Nobody criticises a road trip for going too fast, and driving is way faster than running. Road trips are how I first started exploring, and I'd see a DOC sign and think of all the places we were missing. I'd love to try [bikepacking](https://abigailhannah.nz/051-bikepacking-south-island/) one day, as a medium pace between tramping and road trips.

Zeb has never been the keenest cyclist, and he thinks he'd really enjoy bikepacking too.

### What's next

Zeb isn't really a planner, and his list of tramps only gets longer. The only thing planned when we recorded was the Motatapu. He'd love to tick off the Five Passes and the [Three Passes](https://abigailhannah.nz/062-three-passes-hike/) in Arthur's Pass, and get back down to [Stewart Island](https://abigailhannah.nz/194-summer-as-a-doc-ranger-on-stewart-island-and-women-in-trades-with-brooke-thompson/), which he really loved.

I had [Alice](https://abigailhannah.nz/108-fastpacking-from-st-arnaud-to-arthurs-pass-in-8-days-with-alice-brice/) on the podcast to talk about fastpacking a while back.

Zeb has recently been helping her run Fastpacking NZ, a Facebook page and Instagram account.
