I'd never heard of Lake Unknown before this chat. And it turns out Sarah hadn't either, 24 hours before she set off for it. That made me feel a little bit better.

Sarah lives in Central Otago and spends every spare moment in the mountains: hiking, climbing, mountaineering, anything that gets her outdoors. She found it all through her university tramping club in Auckland, and spent her uni summers living out of her car in the South Island until she could finally move south for good.

Over Christmas and New Year, she and her partner swapped a planned trip for a last-minute six-day off-track loop from near the Routeburn, up the Rockburn Valley to Lake Unknown, over Park Pass Glacier and out the Routeburn North Branch. Two perfect campsites, one horrific bush bash and one sooo long day.

So this post covers how she got into tramping, the trip day by day, what she packed (including her very strong opinions on potato chips), and her advice for going off route. I'll be jumping on Topo after this one.

## From Auckland to the Central Otago mountains {#sarah-story}

Sarah grew up in Auckland (she managed to escape down south to the mountains, but it's not too bad a place to grow up). Her family did typical Kiwi adventures, some hiking and fishing, and she was dragged along to the hunting somewhat unwillingly. She always loved hiking, then lost the enjoyment as a typically moody teenager.

She found it for herself at uni, when she joined the Auckland Tramping Club. A whole world opened up. She started spending every university summer living out of her car, travelling around the South Island for the three months they had off.

That's when she knew where she wanted to be. Driving through Wānaka for the first time, she couldn't believe a place existed with so many national parks on its doorstep. She remembers crying on the ferry back home when summer was over. After two more years, the degree was finished and she made it down.

Every time we're in Wānaka on a summer road trip, there's just something about that town.

## Learning with a university tramping club {#tramping-club}

One of Sarah's early club trips was a week camp in the Coromandel, where a group leader taught them to stream bash up a random stream. It involved a lot of falling off rocks into water, and it was so fun: a style of tramping (or swimming) she'd never done before.

Then she planned her own trip. It was a really unfeasible route in the Tararua Range: drive from Auckland on a Friday night after work (about eight hours, which she does not recommend), then squeeze what should have been a six-day route into a three-day long weekend, including the drive home. They made it up the first peak, then on day two, with the weather coming in, realised they'd been way too ambitious and picked a ridge to bail down. A phone call from a random road end on Sunday night got them a lift back to the car. A quick lesson in how not to plan a tramp.

Most of the club's trips were closer to home, around Ruapehu, [Tongariro National Park](https://abigailhannah.nz/138-an-obsession-with-the-mountains-of-tongariro-national-park-with-hazel-phillips/) and the Ruahine. There are fun events too, like extreme ironing: ball gowns, suits and an ironing board carried to the top of Ruapehu for a high tea.

My husband was in the Auckland Tramping Club in our first year of uni, and I tagged along on one trip because I was the only one with a car. We stayed at Mangatepopo Hut in the middle of winter, and they planned to summit and come back down. About 10 minutes in, I was too cold. I didn't have enough warm layers, and I spent the day huddled in my sleeping bag at the hut, shivering.

Climbing came at the same time for Sarah. She already had a tramping background, so she was keen to push past what typical tramping is and explore different terrain. She joined the university [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/) club because there was so much overlap, and on her first South Island summer she got into alpine scrambling, a step up from tramping that definitely involves hands.

## A last-minute change of plan {#change-of-plan}

Sarah and her partner had a big Christmas and New Year trip planned in Aspiring National Park, with everything packed and the whole route researched. The day before, they looked at the weather. That route had a lot of scrambly sections on slabs that need perfect weather, and the forecast showed maybe two good days with pretty average weather on either side.

So what could they squeeze into that window? Sarah had read briefly about the Rockburn area in Moir's Guide that day, and her partner had been close to it before without making it up to Lake Unknown or Park Pass, a spot he'd always wanted to go. Done. They Googled trip write-ups, looked at Topo maps, and were still reading as they drove over the Crown Range.

The Rockburn Valley is one over from the Routeburn, so you almost start from the same car park as the [Routeburn](https://abigailhannah.nz/009-solo-travel-routeburn-track-emily/). You pack ice axes and food for up to nine days, with everything strapped to the outside, in a car park full of Great Walk hikers. They looked very strange. The route passes through similar country to the Five Passes, near Glenorchy, looking into Fiordland the whole time.

I've had an episode on the [Three Passes](https://abigailhannah.nz/062-three-passes-hike/) but not the Five Passes. And this corner of the country keeps coming up: [Alex Hillary](https://abigailhannah.nz/120-multi-passionate-adventures-and-the-olivine-ice-plateau-with-alexander-hillary/)'s adventure started at that Routeburn end near Glenorchy too. This kind of off-trail wilderness is for experienced people only, and I haven't graduated to that level yet.

## Gear and food for six to nine days {#gear-and-food}

A listener asked how to plan and pack for six to nine days, so here's Sarah's list.

### Gear

* **The basics.** Tent, sleeping mats, sleeping bags, a puffer jacket, raincoat, thermals and hard shells. In summer you can get away with one set of everything.
* **Socks.** Normally three pairs. This time she somehow packed six, having shoved two pairs into each mountaineering boot.
* **Trail runners** for hiking (always her preference), with **mountaineering boots** strapped to the pack.
* **Technical gear.** A helmet, crampons, an ice axe, and 30 metres of six-millimetre cord.

That cord is incredibly light. Sarah recommends it for trips between mountaineering and tramping, when you might need a quick way out of something or to rope up for a little snow travel, without lugging a heavy 60-metre rope for six days.

### Food

Food was the heaviest part of the pack.

* **Breakfast:** oats and protein powder, with peanut butter and honey.
* **Lunch and snacks:** a bag of crushed-up potato chips mixed with scroggin, a different chip flavour each day, portioned out per day in compostable bags, plus a few muesli bars. Lunch is sitting down and chugging as much of that bag as you can, and they stop for a snack every 90 minutes.
* **Dinner:** dehydrated meals, because they're easy.
* **The most essential part:** half a block of Whittaker's a day, a quarter each, eaten when they reach camp. You're crashing hard and you still have to set up. That's when you need chocolate.

The scroggin gives you more substantial energy, and the chips are so light and energy dense. Sarah could go on forever about them:

> "It's actually such a life hack. I would 100% recommend it."

I learned my snack lesson on the [Milford](https://abigailhannah.nz/034-milford-track-nz-popular-walk/). I took a big bag of scroggin with no smaller bags to decant it into, and walked out with most of it. So on the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/) I portioned out my snacks for every single day, and I've done it ever since.

## Rockburn Valley to Lake Unknown {#rockburn-to-lake-unknown}

### Days one and two: Lake Sylvan to Theatre Flat

The plan was to start at the Routeburn Shelter and head straight over Sugarloaf Pass into the Rockburn. The weather on the first day was terrible, so they started from the Lake Sylvan car park instead, 10 minutes back down the road, and walked just two hours to a little shelter at the base of the Rockburn Valley. By the end, Sarah realised she was not pack fit for nine days of supplies.

The next day took them up the Rockburn Valley to Theatre Flat, named because the mountains around it form an amphitheatre. You sit at the bottom looking up.

### Day three: the bush bash to Lake Unknown

The night before, Sarah looked up at the slope and said it looked really awful. Her partner promised her there'd be a route: Lake Unknown is a bucket list spot for trampers, so it wouldn't be a bush bash.

It was very much a bush bash. The best way she can describe it is swimming up ferns. Up to that point there's a kind of track up the valley, so this is where the off-track section starts.

They started in the rain (the first two days had been torrential), and as they came out of the bush, it stopped and started clearing: the beginning of their two-day weather window. You come out at a col above Lake Unknown. It is so beautiful, and Sarah can see why people say it's tricky to get to. There are small lakes above it, so they camped at one, went swimming, and watched mist roll in at sunset and sunrise.

## Park Pass Glacier and the Routeburn North Branch {#park-pass-glacier}

### Day four: over Park Pass Glacier

They'd planned loosely, with extra food, so they could walk until they felt too tired and camp wherever felt right.

From above Lake Unknown, you scramble up the side and down into a hanging valley below Mount Chaos and Amphibian Peak, then climb out on slabs towards the glacier. That was a bit spicy: patches of snow, then rock slabs, and coming off the snow with wet feet meant sliding on the slab, willing her shoes to work.

It was scorching hot, so feeling very sunstroked, they had another swim at the bottom of Park Pass Glacier and ate a whole bunch of food. Then they remembered how to rope up, which took about another hour of faff. You walk up and around quite high on the glacier before dropping towards Park Pass, and they camped at another beautiful lake on the far side, just above the pass.

Sarah can't choose between that campsite and Lake Unknown. They were the two most beautiful places she slept. Five Passes trampers can reach that lake from Park Pass without a glacier, but they had it to themselves. They hadn't seen anyone all trip, so meeting people the next day felt quite strange.

### Day five: the longest day

The plan was to cross Park Pass to Lake Nerine, a beautiful spot between the Rockburn and the Routeburn. Sarah had an inReach, and since they knew the weather might turn, she messaged a friend for a forecast. As they crossed the pass, the reply came: the weather was going to be very bad that night. They needed to get down to the valley floor.

They still wanted to see Lake Nerine, so they went up there first. Then they dropped down and around to North Col, at the head of the Routeburn North Branch. Sarah was exhausted and wanted to camp. Her partner said no: it was so exposed, and in a summer tent, a storm up there would be awful.

The North Branch is meant to be on a track. Sarah still has nightmares about it. It's such bad travel: bashing through scrub, walking through a creek, then getting bashed up by scrub again. They aimed for a flat opening they could see, then collapsed. Five minutes after the tent went up, it started raining, and it poured all night and all the next day.

It was a 13-hour day. In summer down south, it doesn't get dark until 10pm, so they were in the light the whole time.

That day was the hardest of the trip for Sarah, because she'd set out expecting an easy one. When she knows it's going to be big, she can mentally prepare and lock in. This one kept getting longer and harder. Getting to Lake Nerine, they chose a ridge scramble over a tussock slope because of a red line someone had drawn on a topo map. It was more scrambling than she'd have liked with a pack that big.

Among off-track trampers, a blog called Southern Alps Photography has some pretty notorious red lines on maps that people go out and follow. In Sarah's words:

> "it's kind of a running joke that if you haven't been sandbagged by one of those lines, you haven't done … enough of the routes on there. And we definitely got sandbagged by this one."

Her strategy when a day blows out: as soon as she feels a breakdown coming, she eats another muesli bar.

### Day six: walking out

Their feet had swelled from being wet for 13 hours, so the last day was somewhat painful, but easy: follow the track out to the Routeburn, past Great Walk hikers on their first day, looking very dishevelled and smelly in the pouring rain. It was a loop, so there was no shuttle to sort out. With 5 kilometres of road to the Lake Sylvan car park, her partner refused to road walk and told Sarah to hitch. A lovely couple who'd just finished the Great Walk took one look at her and offered a ride to the car.

## Getting started with off-track tramping {#getting-started}

Sarah sees two parts to it. Fitness is the easier one to work on by yourself: go on well-marked day hikes that don't feel like too much of a challenge, and build up slowly over time.

The skills take more: route finding, reading a topo map, first aid and general bush skills. Her advice is to find an outdoors club. Uni clubs are great, and there's also the New Zealand Alpine Club and lots of regional tramping clubs, which often run courses on wilderness first aid and navigating with a compass and topo map. You learn the skills and meet people to go out with. Tramping Facebook pages work too.

These days Sarah is out nearly every good-weather weekend, with her partner, with friends or solo. Starting out was hard. She wasn't very socially outgoing, and she was terrified of being new to everything. Her way in was finding one or two things she could contribute: driving, extra snacks, some baking. If you have the strength and fitness without the skills, offer to carry things. In mountaineering, that might mean carrying the rope while someone else leads. As Sarah puts it:

> "I feel like tramping in the outdoors has given me a lot of social confidence that I never had before"

Driving was my way in on that first club trip too. And baking always makes you the group favourite: [my husband](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/) carries brownies up and saves them for the hardest day.

As for what's next, Sarah has a never-ending list. Her bucket list for the year is to get back up to the high glacier huts, like Pioneer Hut on the West Coast glaciers.
