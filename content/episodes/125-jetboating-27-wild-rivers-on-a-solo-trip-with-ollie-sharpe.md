This episode came straight from my Instagram stories. I asked what topics people wanted to hear on the podcast, Ollie suggested boating, and I said: you're up.

Ollie is 23, from Canterbury, and had only been jet boating for a few years when we recorded. He'd just come back from a two-week solo trip around the South Island where he boated 27 rivers. With no plan at all.

I know almost nothing about jet boating, so I asked a lot of newbie questions. We cover how he got started on a budget, that first trip that went sideways, his favourite rivers on both sides of the Southern Alps, why he loves winter boating, the rules of the river and how to try it before you buy a boat.

## Growing up in Canterbury and finding jet boating {#ollie-story}

Ollie grew up on a farm just out of Lincoln in [Canterbury](https://abigailhannah.nz/141-best-christchurch-and-canterbury-adventures-with-kids-with-jasmine-trafford/), so he's always been in the outdoors: fishing, hiking, tramping and lots of sport through his teenage years. These days he's based in Mid Canterbury, a 20-minute drive from the closest mountains.

Jet boating came later, from watching jet boating videos on YouTube. It looked like a cool way to see the backcountry, so he bought a boat, with no idea what he was doing, and learnt on the way.

Boats can cost over $100,000, but Ollie picked up a cheap one. The problem came after that: what do you do with it? He wishes he'd joined Jet Boating New Zealand (JBNZ) earlier, as the club trains people in driving boats and what to do when things go wrong.

## A first jet boat trip up the Hopkins and Dobson {#first-trip}

He backed the boat into the river and went straight to the Hopkins and Dobson, out the back of Twizel, which plenty of jet boaters will know. In hindsight, there wasn't much water around, so it probably wasn't the smartest move.

Going up took an hour. Coming back down, with two others on board, they kept getting stuck and spent hours pushing the boat back into the water. Everyone was so tired they headed home a day early.

The steepest learning curve, Ollie says, is driving a braided river:

> "you've got to make a choice at a split second … a couple of seconds to make a choice if you go on left, if you go on right"

Make the wrong decision, and it's a lot of work to get a boat back into the water. After that first trip, he joined the club and learnt from people who'd been doing it their whole lives, which he reckons is the best thing he's done.

## 27 rivers on a solo South Island trip {#solo-trip}

With no holidays planned over the summer break, Ollie sorted it last minute: hooked the boat on and headed south. No plan.

* **The Mackenzie Country.** The Tekapo and Pukaki rivers, then the Hopkins and Dobson, where he stayed three nights so he could meet up with a couple of mates.
* **Around Wānaka.** The Matukituki, the Makarora and the Wilkin.
* **Haast and the West Coast.** Then back up the [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/), doing every river possible on the way.

That came to 27 rivers. He stocked up on fuel and food in Twizel, Wānaka and Haast, and lived out of the [Land Cruiser](https://abigailhannah.nz/006-4wd-land-cruiser-bri-woolnough/), the boat and a tent, camping up a valley most nights. It was his first time in South Westland, and you can cover a lot of ground in a boat.

On his own, a typical day is setting up camp, then walking and fishing with his [dog](https://abigailhannah.nz/020-travel-south-island-dogs-courtney/), who comes on most trips. Fishing is usually off the bank, or off the boat on lakes like Lake Benmore.

> "We don't tend to do much catching, but we say we do."

And for a trip that size, nothing broke the whole two weeks. Probably a first.

### West Coast favourites

* **The Arawhata.** Hard to go past. It's the last river you drive across before Jackson Bay, a big valley with a lot of good hunting and fishing. You can also split off up the Jackson River to Lake Ellery, another beautiful spot.
* **Small West Coast creeks**, with lots of flax and low-hanging bush. Some you could walk into for a day or two. By boat, you're there in a couple of hours.
* **The Paringa River**, which runs out of Lake Paringa. He camped one night on the lake's edge and shot up the river in the morning.

I've done the drive out to Jackson Bay, but I don't remember that river in particular.

### Mackenzie favourites

* **The Dobson Valley**, at the top of Lake Ōhau. You can get in on foot or by four-wheel drive, and a jet boat lets you push further up. He's been in October with the whole valley under snow, then again in summer.
* **The** [**Godley**](https://abigailhannah.nz/overlanding-nz/), at the top of Lake Tekapo. It's a good river, though it's given Ollie trouble. The first time, the weather packed in and they had to pull out straight away. The second time, the jet pump (which drives the boat in shallow water) blew to pieces, and they floated the boat back down the river to the lake. Third time, on the October snow trip, they got a good chunk of the way up, short of the glacial lakes.

I've been up the Godley four-wheel driving, but not in a boat.

They normally launch straight into the lake and boat the whole way up, because towing a boat up a valley on a bouncing trailer isn't a good idea.

## Jet boating in winter and river hazards {#winter-and-hazards}

I had no idea you could jet boat in winter. Ollie prefers it. Snowmelt and more rain mean more flow, so you can often go further up a river than in summer. Wrap up warm and stay dry, and it's normally pretty good, as long as the wind chill isn't too bad.

The catch is rising rivers, so check the weather multiple times before a trip. In Canterbury, a nor'wester in the hills can raise the rivers without it even raining where you are, and it doesn't take long for the water to come down.

I get really paranoid about flash floods. Once, camped right by the edge of Lake Tekapo in heavy rain, I was sure we'd float away, and my husband had to point out that Tekapo is a massive lake.

Once you're on the river, the hazards Ollie watches for:

* **Rocks and trees**, and all sorts of things floating down a river.
* **Other river users.** In a tight creek, think about what you'll do if you come around a corner and meet another boat, kayakers or swimmers.
* **Keep right.** It's the opposite of driving a car, and it stumps a lot of people.
* **Rock gardens and big bouldery rapids**, in valleys like the [Landsborough](https://abigailhannah.nz/196-packrafting-the-landsborough-river-from-the-west-coast-with-tom-waters/) off the Haast valley, and in Central Otago. They can catch you out.

My husband does [whitewater kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/), so I asked what grade of rivers Ollie goes up.

It depends on the river. The Waimakariri is grade one and two, and that's where you see a lot of kayakers, especially people training for the Coast to Coast. There's normally a way around them without disturbing them.

## River rules and Jet Boating New Zealand {#river-rules}

JBNZ looks after a lot of the rules and keeps rivers open for everyone. Rivers follow the same rule as a lake, five knots within 200 metres of the shore, which rules out speed on every river. So each river needs an uplifting to be jet boated at speed. Most rivers have one, and the ones that don't are off limits.

One example Ollie gives is the top of the Makarora, into the Blue Pools. Some rivers are closed for fish spawning, and some are only uplifted for a couple of months of the year.

JBNZ's website lists which rivers are open and when, and members get a yearly handbook with every river in New Zealand, its rules, grades and launch spots.

## Getting into jet boating on a budget {#getting-started}

### Try it before you buy

Ollie's biggest tip is to join the club and ask for advice, even before you buy a boat. Experienced members know what to look for. And if you're keen, ring Jet Boating New Zealand: they'll put you in touch with people who can take you out to see what it's like, because a boat is a huge chunk of money if you decide it's not for you. Ollie's happy to take people out too: on his solo trip, he took two Irish lads out for a day around Haast.

Commercial jet boating, he says, is a good thrill ride, but pretty tame and tourism based. The [Rakaia](https://abigailhannah.nz/101-4wd-rakaia-valley-adventure-and-a-solo-northland-trip-with-billy-nunweek/) and the Waimakariri both have commercial operators, and a 20-seater tour boat feels a lot different to a small boat, and goes to different places.

### Choosing a boat

* **Size decides where you can go.** Taking friends and family means a big boat, which restricts you in the backcountry. For just you and one other person on small backcountry rivers, a smaller boat suits.
* **Small boats are easier to unstick.** Two or three people, or even one, can get Ollie's boat back in the river. A big boat might need a group of ten.
* **Look on Marketplace and Trade Me.** Ollie has two mini boats. His 3.5-metre boat is a Rakaia Marine, built by a local in Rakaia who's now retired. When we recorded, he'd paid $20,000 for it, and you could still find boats in the $10,000 to $20,000 range.

Like a car, there are oils, filters and grease points to keep on top of, and in a river, vibration, rocks and running aground all add stress and dents. He's had the motor out three times.

His final advice:

> "You don't have to have the flashest boat. … As long as you know your boat and learn about how it works, then you'll be fine."

That's how we started [vanlife](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/): a real small rust box van, a couple of weeks in the South Island in the middle of winter, scraping ice off the inside of the windows each morning. Now we're on our third van. You can start small.

### Club runs

The club also runs events where anyone can bring a boat, often up to 50 on a run. On one run up the Waimakariri, from the motorway bridge just north of Christchurch, 65 boats turned up: families, young and old, and inflatable jet boats, which are becoming popular.

## Next up: Southland and the Hollyford {#bucket-list}

When we recorded, Ollie was close to running out of South Island valleys he hadn't seen, with a North Island tour on the cards in the next couple of years. First, another two-week trip with mates: back down the West Coast to Haast, then on to rivers in [Southland](https://abigailhannah.nz/five-great-walks-in-five-days/), which he's never really been to.

Top of his bucket list, and he reckons every jet boater's, is the Hollyford River in Fiordland National Park. A few people have boated in, but there's a big rapid in the way, so a lot of people fly their boats in by helicopter. If he's paying for a helicopter, he'll stay a good week or two, and might take a couple of [packrafts](https://abigailhannah.nz/186-packrafting-and-tramping-48-days-through-fiordland-with-penzy-dinsdale/) to explore the next valley over.

The Hollyford is on my bucket list as well, in kayaks. So is canoeing the Whanganui, which is like a [Great Walk](https://abigailhannah.nz/10-great-walks-in-10-days/) except they call it a Great Journey, because you're not walking.
