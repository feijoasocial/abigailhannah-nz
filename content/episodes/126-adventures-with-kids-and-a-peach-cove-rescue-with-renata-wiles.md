Renata has been on the show before, talking [Northland and vanlife](https://abigailhannah.nz/northland-road-trip/) with a toddler. And this time she saved my butt, because I'd been a little bit slack with recording and she came on at very short notice. From a camping chair. In an empty lounge, because she'd just moved house. Legend.

Renata grew up in Auckland, in a family that didn't really go camping, then spent her last year of high school doing outdoor pursuits in Wānaka. These days she's a mum of two, getting back into adventures with her kids, and running her own business.

I remember watching her stories when this happened: lots of hype about Peach Cove, then suddenly a helicopter, and "I'll tell you about this later". So so much suspense. So this post is the whole Peach Cove story, from the missed turnoff to the rescue to the walk out with two packs and a three-year-old, plus her lessons and how she frames a hard day for her daughter.

## Finding the outdoors in Wānaka {#wanaka-outdoors}

Renata grew up in Auckland. They weren't a camping family, and she didn't do tramping or water sports growing up.

At school she was looking for something different and more challenging. A couple of friends weren't going on to seventh form, and she was craving more independence. Then she found out about Mount Aspiring College in Wānaka, where you can go for your final year and live in its hostel programme: a house where you cook your own meals and do your own grocery shopping like flatmates, with hostel managers keeping it all on track.

She applied, got in and moved down on her own as a teenager. It surprised a lot of people who knew her, because she wasn't super outdoorsy. She spent the year on the outdoor pursuits programme, building snow caves at the top of a ski field and waking up to the most amazing view, learning to snowboard, kayaking, mountain biking and tramping.

She wasn't the fittest, the fastest or the best at any of it, and that taught her something that's stuck. You don't have to be the best, and it's never too late to try something new. For a long time she thought she was too old to [learn to surf](https://abigailhannah.nz/learn-to-surf-new-zealand/), the same way she once thought she wasn't a camping person or a mountain biking person. She'd decided that because she hadn't done these things as a kid, she wasn't outdoorsy enough. So her question now is:

> "what is outdoorsy enough? … You can just go for a walk at your own pace but that you're comfortable with. And that's outdoorsy."

So many of my guests say the same thing over and over again: just start. I've had people on the show in their 50s going tramping for the first time, solo, way out of their comfort zone. It really is never too late.

## From an OE to coming home {#oe-and-home}

After a year at university in Auckland, Renata wasn't ready to go back to that life, so she transferred to Otago and spent her holidays snowboarding in what she calls the outdoor playground of the South Island.

Then she and a friend bought one-way tickets to London with no plan. She worked in a bar for a few months, then went to America with Camp America as a [rock climbing](https://abigailhannah.nz/143-intro-to-rock-climbing-and-nzs-best-crags-with-josh-parker/) instructor at a summer camp. Back in the UK, she tried the settled thing, decided it wasn't for her, and spent a whole summer as a tour guide around Europe. After that she worked at Flight Centre in London for quite a few years, so she could at least talk about travel all day.

She and her partner planned an epic three-month trip through India and Sri Lanka on the way home. About three weeks in, what she thought was really bad food poisoning turned out to be morning sickness: she was pregnant with her first daughter. They were at an airport hotel in Delhi, booked to fly south to Kerala, and decided to go home instead. They got back in December 2019, right before COVID hit.

Since then she's had two kids, worked part-time in the product team of a New Zealand luxury travel company, and launched her own business.

## Peach Cove Track with a three-year-old {#peach-cove-track}

Peach Cove is near Whangārei. It's supposed to be really child friendly, and it was easy to do as a weekend from Auckland: drive up, walk in, stay the night and be back in time for work on Monday. The walk to the hut is signposted at an hour and a half.

I haven't been to Peach Cove itself, but I've done the Bream Head Track ridgeline above it, walking one way and hitchhiking back to the car.

Renata had booked it with her partner and her daughter, who'd recently turned three. Then her partner hurt his ankle and couldn't hike. She'd been looking forward to it for ages, so when a friend with a three-year-old of her own said she'd be keen, it became a mums and kids weekend. Renata was pregnant with her second at the time.

### The missed turnoff

From the car park, it's quite steep to climb up onto the ridge. Once you're on the ridge, it's quite manageable, and Renata would still call it a child-friendly walk. It was really beautiful.

The turnoff down to the hut is where it went wrong. The sign is there, but the path down to the stairs angles back behind you, so as she approached, Renata didn't see it. She assumed the turn must be slightly further ahead, kept walking, and her friend didn't notice either. They were having a good time chatting.

Eventually it felt like they'd walked a lot further than the sign said, and they realised they'd passed the hut. Renata takes full responsibility for it. They turned back, tired and hungry, with two three-year-olds walking some of the way and being carried for the rest, surviving on M&Ms and sing-alongs. The hour and a half turned into five hours.

I've walked that ridge, and I can totally see how you'd miss it: it's almost a U-turn to get down the stairs to Peach Cove.

## A broken ankle on the Peach Cove stairs {#broken-ankle}

Everyone had warned Renata about the stairs down to Peach Cove, and she still couldn't imagine how many there would be. They felt relentless. Going down, her legs started to feel wobbly, and with a little person with you, you're also trying to stay really positive so they enjoy it.

You have to concentrate so much more on stairs, thinking about where you put your feet. Sometimes DOC stairs are great, and sometimes they're really uneven.

Almost at the bottom, her friend missed a step. Her pack was so heavy that she came down on an angle, and she broke her ankle. She couldn't get up or put any pressure on it.

They were so close to the hut you could almost see it. Another group came down behind them and helped get her, the kids and all the bags to the hut. With her boot off, the ankle was very swollen, and there was no way she could walk back up the hundreds and hundreds of stairs.

### Calling for help

They didn't know what to do. A boat could reach the bay, but how would they get her across the rocks? They weren't even sure it counted as an emergency. They had cell reception, so they called DOC, and DOC told them to call 111.

There was another family at the hut, with university-age kids, and having that outside voice to talk it through with was really helpful.

That's what I love about huts in the backcountry. People come together as a community, whatever you need.

## The helicopter rescue at Peach Cove Hut {#helicopter-rescue}

111 sent a helicopter. For Renata, it was one of those moments of being so grateful to live in a country where this is available in an emergency, without a bill afterwards. We have [DOC huts](https://abigailhannah.nz/160-hut-etiquette-in-doc-huts-and-exploring-the-whitcombe-valley-with-renee-cadigan/) that are really affordable, mostly safe tracks, no bears or snakes trying to kill us, and help when something goes wrong.

Peach Cove is a small bay at the bottom of the stairs, with nowhere for a helicopter to land. By then it was dark, so they waved their high-vis gear and torches to show where they were, a great reminder of why you carry brightly coloured things.

The paramedics were dropped off while the helicopter hovered further away, and they assessed her friend and talked everyone through what would happen. They took her friend and her friend's three-year-old, so Renata wasn't left with both kids, and flew them to Whangārei Hospital.

Renata is careful to say that part is her friend's story to tell. She has so much admiration for her, holding it together with a broken ankle and being the rock for a terrified three-year-old being winched into a helicopter. It could have happened to anybody.

### A night at the hut

Then it was Renata, her three-year-old and the other family, and her reaction was: okay, well, I guess we're going to have some dinner now. It was very surreal. The girls had been so excited to stay together, even getting into their pyjamas and into the bunk beds while they waited for the helicopter, and three-year-olds want to talk about what happened a lot. So they talked about it for the rest of the night.

## Walking out with two packs and a toddler {#walking-out}

The helicopter couldn't take her friend's pack. In the moment, that was the least of anyone's worries. The next morning, Renata had two packs, a three-year-old and a lot of stairs.

The other family were walking out early and offered to take a couple of things. Renata emptied her friend's very heavy pack, filled a little food bag for them to leave by her car, and moved as much as she could into her own pack. She'd packed light, so her friend's bag ended up almost empty.

Over porridge and banana, Renata and her daughter had a pep talk about being a team and problem solving together. No hurry. They had a packet of M&Ms left.

The stairs were the hardest part. Hard without packs, harder with one, harder again with two, and harder still with a toddler complaining about the stairs. They stopped so many times to sit down, have some M&Ms and some water. Every bend, she thought surely this was the top, and it was more stairs. Her daughter was singing Moana, and in her head, Renata was repeating to herself:

> "Like there is no other option, but for us to just keep putting one foot in front of the other."

Once they reached the ridge, with no more stairs and no more hills, the rest of the walk out was really enjoyable. They told stories, played I Spy and sang songs, and in the most weird way possible, Renata felt so proud of herself and of her daughter.

Back at the car, drenched in sweat and sore everywhere, they sat in the boot and ate the overnight oats she'd left there. Then they drove to Whangārei Hospital to pick up her friend, since Renata had driven her up, and they all drove home together wondering what had just happened.

Props to Renata, too: all those stairs with an extra pack and a three-year-old, while pregnant.

## Lessons from Peach Cove {#lessons}

* **Carry a** [**PLB**](https://abigailhannah.nz/plb-safety/). They had cell reception. Without it, Renata would have had to leave her friend there and go for help.
* **Pack light.** She had everything she needed, including a first aid kit and a spare change of clothes, and she was so glad she hadn't packed extra just in case. Take what you need and no more.

### Framing a hard day

Renata doesn't want to put anyone off [tramping with their kids](https://abigailhannah.nz/190-family-tramping-on-a-budget-and-child-free-trips-with-shoshannah-shand/). Her little girl loves staying at huts, and they've done a few now. She knows it's easy for her to say, as she's not the one who broke a bone, but a lot of it is how you frame it. She hopes to pass on that attitude to her daughter:

> "are we going to think like, wow, that was a real adventure and we're all safe and that's what matters. And next time we're going to be even more careful when we do it."

On the walk out, she kept telling her daughter they had really strong legs, snacks to fuel them, and that they could do hard things. It's hard for a three-year-old to walk for hours, and Renata thinks acknowledging that, and knowing you can do it anyway, will help her daughter well beyond tramping.

I think I need some of that positive self-talk when I go tramping too.

## Finding family-friendly spots {#family-spots}

Renata's business is Roam with Kids, an app of child-friendly places: cafes with play areas, playgrounds, pram-friendly walks, splash pads and indoor and outdoor activities, with photos and reviews from other parents. It started in [Auckland](https://abigailhannah.nz/123-best-auckland-adventures-for-kids-with-outdoorsy-hijabi-mum-veronika-edali/), and when we recorded, it covered most of the major cities, with filters for things like change tables and fully fenced spots, and family events in Auckland, Hamilton, Wellington and [Christchurch](https://abigailhannah.nz/141-best-christchurch-and-canterbury-adventures-with-kids-with-jasmine-trafford/). Accommodation and campsites were next on the list.

A lot of parenting is repetitive, she says, because you go to the same places you know will suit your kids. She wants to help parents and caregivers try something different.
