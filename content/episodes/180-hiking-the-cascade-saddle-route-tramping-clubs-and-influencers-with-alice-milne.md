Alice is back! The last time she was on the podcast, we talked about [hiking while pregnant](https://abigailhannah.nz/023-hiking-pregnant-alice/). Now she's a mum to a toddler, still tramping, and still writing the most detailed blog posts (I read them all the time to find out about places I'm going).

If you don't know her, Alice is a Kiwi gal from Southland who loves tramping, reading and chocolate. Maybe not in that order. And she's so good at going one layer deeper than the popular tracks everyone does over and over.

So this time we go up and over the Cascade Saddle route in Mount Aspiring National Park, with her dad, on a bluebird day, and down to a campsite run by some very cheeky kea. Then we get into why she keeps doing courses (river crossings!), why you should join a tramping club even if you're under 40, and a big one I've been chewing on: are influencers ruining the outdoors? Plus her favourite lesser-known tracks and her tips for tramping with little ones.

## Life since the last episode {#life-since}

Alice was in her third trimester when we last recorded. She wanted to keep adventuring and tramping with a little one, and she can confirm she has: there were day hikes in the Dolomites in Italy on her parental leave year, and her little one has already stayed in huts. Once Alice had more freedom and her fitness was back, she started doing more solo trips too.

Life did change, she says. It depends on your priorities. If you still want to get out tramping or travelling, you can make that happen.

## Growing up tramping in Southland {#growing-up}

Alice grew up on a sheep farm in Southland, so being outdoors was part of farm life. Her parents tramped before they had kids and kept going with the kids in tow. She had a break over uni (less disposable income, different priorities) and picked it up again once she was working.

## Cascade Saddle route from Aspiring Hut {#cascade-saddle-route}

Cascade Saddle is a route in Mount Aspiring National Park, starting from Aspiring Hut in the Matukituki Valley, north of Wānaka. It's the same valley where the French Ridge and Liverpool huts start from. Aspiring Hut itself is great for families, and Alice has stayed there with her little one since it was upgraded.

### The summer before

Alice walked Cascade Saddle in April, at the end of a summer when she was doing as many tramping trips as she could. If you have a fear of heights, she says, you shouldn't be doing Cascade Saddle at all. Her dad, a relatively experienced tramper, was keen. They picked a few weekends and hoped for really good weather, because you can't safely do this in bad conditions. They got lucky: the first frost of the season, then a bluebird day.

Her dad knows the farmer who owns the land you walk across before Aspiring Hut, so they got permission to drive up the farm track to the national park boundary, which cut out a couple of hours of flat walking.

### The climb

From there it's a big climb, well over 1,300 metres. Alice isn't the fastest tramper (her natural pace is slow, taking photos), so with full packs, tents and sleeping bags, it was a huge day. Some people do it as a day walk with light gear, and she doesn't understand how unless you're really fit.

She was nervous on the way up. There have been fatalities on the route, so they really wanted a good day. It was very steep, and at times it felt more like rock climbing than tramping. In the good conditions she was mostly comfortable, often with three points of contact, sometimes four, and there were a few spots where a fall would have consequences.

The first part is in the trees, like the climbs to Brewster Hut, Liverpool or French Ridge: lots of roots, very steep, but lots of handholds. Then you break out above the bushline, looking down to where you came from, with Mount Aspiring across the valley like a beautiful pyramid peak, and tussock all the way up to a ridge still towering over you. They stopped often to admire the views (and definitely not to catch their breath).

At the pylon, which marks the safe place to go up and over the ridge, they got their first view of the Dart Glacier, which is part of what makes Cascade Saddle so special. Alice hadn't quite realised how far you then drop, steeply, down to the campsite.

Steep scrambling with a pack on reminds me of the top of Robert Ridge, where I thought: wow, this got gnarly real quick.

## Camping with the kea at Cascade Saddle {#cascade-saddle-kea}

The kea at Cascade Saddle are notorious. Alice left her fancy ultralight tent at home and took a $40 Kmart tent instead. At the campsite, they stashed one of the packs and a lot of their gear in the long drop toilet, so the kea couldn't attack it while they were away (the other option is burying it under rocks, which the kea are probably smart enough to move).

Then it was a walk on to the saddle itself. They sat watching the golden light, the sun getting lower and the glacier's colours getting deeper. Since then, Alice has walked the Rees-Dart Track, and looked up at the same glacier from the valley below.

They waited until dark to put up the tent, so the kea wouldn't know they were there. The kea found them anyway. At 5.30am one was at the tent right next to Alice's head, then they were cawing and laughing. Their tent came out alive.

The official campsite, with the long drop, is the only place you're meant to camp. Some people camp closer for the glacier view, but the area is fragile.

On a sunny day, the route is worn enough that you can tell where people go, and the rock was grippy when dry. It's essentially straight up.

### Her favourite moments

Sharing it with her dad. He still talks about the trip, and it's one of his top three hikes, after a lot of tramping. He pushed himself in a safe way and did something he wasn't sure he could.

For Alice, it was Mount Aspiring from that angle. On the way back, at a respectful distance from a sheer drop, a stream had cut a little V, cascading in a waterfall below them, and that V framed Mount Aspiring perfectly as the sun set.

Surprisingly, she didn't struggle. The week before she'd done the [Hump Ridge](https://abigailhannah.nz/136-pushed-to-her-limits-on-the-hump-ridge-track-and-slow-mindful-travel-with-helen-griffiths/), three days averaging 20km, which she found harder. Twenty kilometres a day is a lot for her (the [Te Araroa walkers](https://abigailhannah.nz/157-setting-the-fastest-self-supported-record-on-te-araroa-with-billy-meredith/) on the podcast would call it a short day). She'd built Cascade Saddle up in her head so much that it felt easier than she expected. Alice thinks it's mental: go in intimidated and it's often not as bad as you expect.

I had that with Sealy Tarns. Everyone says the stairs are gruelling, and I don't remember them being hard at all. We climbed in the dark for sunrise, and at the top a gang of kea tried to pry the "don't feed the kea" sign off the picnic table.

## Cascade Saddle safety and when to go {#cascade-saddle-safety}

The fatalities Alice knows about were falls. On parts of the route, if you fall, there's no coming back. Snow, ice and wet snow grass make it slippery, and near the top, a slip sends you over the side. Her advice:

* **Go in late summer.** Too early and there's still snow and ice, and when we recorded, snow had been hanging around in the alpine a lot more than normal.
* **Never in bad weather or snow.** If you meet either, it's time to turn back.
* **Wait for the snow grass and tussock to dry** if you're starting early.
* **Remember you're far from help.** A helicopter from Wānaka will be quick in good weather, and in poor weather it won't be able to reach you.

Coming in from the Dart side is less sketchy, but it's still a rugged environment.

## River crossing courses and building skills {#river-crossing-skills}

Alice is always levelling up. She jokes that she's a goody two shoes who needs someone to tell her the right way to do things, and most of her courses came from spotting gaps in her own experience.

### River crossings

Growing up in New Zealand, and especially in the tramping community, you hear about so many deaths around rivers, so Alice grew up cautious about them. On her river crossing course she already knew most of the theory. What she needed was practice in a safe environment, pushed out of her comfort zone with support. It's made her a lot more confident.

On a recent New Year's trip (she won't name it, because she doesn't want it to become the next Instagram spot and it has river crossings not everyone could do safely), one crossing was up to her hips and fairly fast. The three of them linked up the first time. Coming back, she knew she could do it herself, with a safe run-out, so she sent the others ahead (to get a video, of course) and crossed alone.

She recommends the course so much. She cannot overstate how good it was.

### Navigation

Last year she did her first adventure race, and the compass training showed her how little she knew. When we recorded, she was running a group tramping experience in April, sold out, including the same river crossing course and a navigation course. There was a lot of interest, especially from women, who historically weren't always valued in these outdoor spaces.

On a [sea kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/182-finishing-a-two-month-slow-paddle-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/) course I did recently, a weekend with other women, I found out so much I didn't know.

## Joining a tramping club {#tramping-club}

Alice had wanted to join a tramping club for a long time. Since becoming a mum, she wanted more connection in the tramping community, so she joined what was the Southland Tramping Club, which has since been renamed because they do a lot of biking and kayaking too.

She's had such an enthusiastic welcome. A lot of clubs around the motu have an ageing membership, and more younger people are joining hers (and by younger, she means 40). The club does so many volunteer projects, like trapping and track marking, and some older members worry those taonga will disappear if nobody takes up the mantle. She's been a member for just over a year and is already on the committee, leading four trips this summer.

### Why people hold back

When Alice first joined a club, in Whanganui, it was the fear of walking into a meeting alone. She's not the most extroverted, despite how it looks online. She assumed she'd be one of the youngest and wouldn't have much in common with anyone, which turned out to be unfounded.

The trips have been good ones. One was Earnslaw Burn, with five of them, all under 40. Another was a track marking recce on a waterfall track near Winton in Southland, after a lot of LandSAR call-outs because it was poorly marked. It's meant to be a six-hour return walk, and they spent 12 hours knocking in triangles and cutting back brush. Alice was the slowest, with the 70 and 75-year-olds beating her easily.

### What a club gives you

* **Try before you join.** Alice's club lets people do one or two trips first. She'll say yes for easier trips, but not for a four-day off-track alpine mission or a climb above [Gertrude Saddle](https://abigailhannah.nz/037-earnslaw-burn-gertrude-saddle-lauren-murray/).
* **Knowledge.** Clubs have so much knowledge and are eager to share it.
* **Courses.** She did a wilderness first aid day course for free through her club, funded by FMC and the club.
* **Gear hire.** PLBs, first aid kits and tents, so you can test things out before buying.
* **Connection.** Growing up rurally, big community gatherings were normal. In a city, you might not know your neighbours.

Connecting with strangers in real life gives us all a bit more hope, and Alice has done that for me too: she made the group chats that got a group of women tramping together on two trips, including Routeburn Falls.

## Influencers, overtourism and the outdoors {#influencers-outdoors}

I posted on Instagram asking whether influencers are ruining the outdoors, and it went a little bit viral, with people on both sides.

Alice likes to ride the middle. Look at Roys Peak or [Mueller Hut](https://abigailhannah.nz/028-mueller-hut-backyard-travel-family/) and you can see how overtourism (Kiwis and locals included) has changed the landscape. [Shoshannah](https://abigailhannah.nz/010-toddler-travel-shoshannah/) stayed with Alice recently after doing Gertrude Saddle and was vaguely horrified at how busy it was, and how many people were camping up there.

On a walk up the Gertrude Valley, off the Milford Road, I saw a woman in platform heels with a little handbag, on a valley floor that's all rocks and boulders.

### Funnel people to places that can take it

Alice isn't mad about Roys Peak being popular. It's a four-wheel drive track, already a scar on the landscape, with a toilet at the top, so its footprint can handle hundreds of people (even if there are risks, with no shelter or water and people overestimating their abilities). Places like Gertrude Saddle can't: there's no toilet, and fragile alpine flowers and grasses get trampled. So highlight the places that can handle it, and keep the backcountry places a bit quieter. She doesn't want to gatekeep: she wants places open to everyone, with people educated to visit them safely and sustainably.

Long-form content like blogs and YouTube makes it easier to explain safety and the reasons behind it, and short-form content often shares the hook and nothing else. Alice saw a reel of a waterfall walk near Milford Sound that's closed, behind a gate you have to climb, with a rope. The first post shared all of that. The next one only shared the view.

And people who share going into the ice caves at Brewster Glacier don't know how unstable they are. Publicising it makes it look okay, and ice can fall at any point.

On people complaining about crowds:

> "anytime someone complains, i'm like, it's great. They're all there. Go somewhere else and they won't be there."

### Where I've landed

Influencers aren't going anywhere. I think the bigger issue is how people travel, and mass tourism. Alice points out that places have always been shared, they were just harder to find.

The Blue Springs near Pūtāruru were empty seven years ago. This time, on a weekday in the middle of winter, it was packed. Throwing coins into the water and rubbish down the toilets is the problem. The responsibility is on all of us as travellers to do our research and look after places. Want a pretty view without the people? Do Isthmus Peak instead of Roys Peak. For influencers, it comes down to motive: are you sharing a wow view for likes, or helping people experience a place safely and responsibly?

## Listener questions: Southland, hidden gems and kids {#listener-questions}

[Verity](https://abigailhannah.nz/169-tramping-hut-bagging-goals-cancer-and-recovery-with-verity-jackson/) asked what motivates Alice to get out. She's not ashamed to say it's sometimes content for her blog. Mostly, though:

> "it's become part of who I am, it's good for my mental health, it's good for my physical health, our country is just so beautiful."

### The best thing about tramping in Southland

Southland is so close to places that are hard for everyone else to reach. Te Anau is a two-hour drive, so Milford is a day trip, and Fiordland is right there. So are the [Catlins](https://abigailhannah.nz/139-slow-travel-in-an-ev-and-catlins-dunedin-and-oamaru-with-mature-flaneur-tim-ward/), Central Otago, Queenstown, Wānaka and [Rakiura Stewart Island](https://abigailhannah.nz/194-summer-as-a-doc-ranger-on-stewart-island-and-women-in-trades-with-brooke-thompson/). People love to hate on Invercargill and Southland. Alice says bring it on.

### Lesser-known tracks

Alice is careful here, because she doesn't want her favourites to become the next big thing. A few she shared:

* **Rees-Dart Track.** Right up there. When we recorded, it had a slip on it as of New Year, and you could still do the Rees Valley section.
* **Kea Basin**, from the Rees Valley. A day walk, or camp responsibly: there's no toilet, so carry your waste out. There are some bivvy rocks.
* **Mataketake Hut.** Built after COVID, with one side of the hut lined with shelves of books, and gorgeous sunsets.

She's more likely to share places like these in a DM, where she can check you know how to cross a river safely.

### Tramping with young kids

* **Follow people who inspire you.** Seeing the person you want to be helps. For Alice, that's people like [Sonia](https://abigailhannah.nz/015-old-ghost-road-family-backtothewild/), Shoshannah and Jen from Backyard Travel Family.
* **Manage your own anxieties.** The biggest barrier is overthinking: crying, feeding, temperature. There will always be something to fixate on.
* **Start easy and go slow.** Tent in the backyard first. Their first tent trip was a drive into Fiordland and only an hour's walk to camp, so they could walk out in the night if they needed to.

Alice has written a blog post about tramping with babies, and so has Sonia.
