I love Sam's videos, so I was really really excited to finally have a proper chat. He describes himself as your friendly bushman, a happy hunting, fishing, conservation type character from the East Coast who's more comfortable in the backcountry than drinking lattes in town. Which made me laugh, because so many of us are more at home with a flat white (guilty).

So we start with what a bushman actually is, and it's so much bigger than I expected. Sam learned to trap in Te Urewera as a 12-year-old, has trapped from there to Fiordland, and has written a book on it all.

This post covers his path into conservation, a kōkako mix-up on a Fiordland island, his favourite loop in the Waioeka, how North and South Island forests feel to him, eating from the bush, raising kids out there, and where to start if you want to help.

## What a bushman is {#what-a-bushman-is}

To Sam, a bushman is part of a culture in Aotearoa he was raised in from the age of 12. His grandfather was a bushman, and so were a lot of the old-school trappers he learned under.

A bushman works for abundance and balance in our ecosystems. They can read the bush: what birds are telling them with their actions and where they're going, and when trees will fruit in sequence with each other. In his words:

> "To me, a bushman is someone who is a hunter, but isn't into hunting for the egotistical hanging something on a wall … mentality."

A bushman [hunts](https://abigailhannah.nz/150-hunting-wapiti-in-fiordland-with-jarrod-mclachlan/) to feed his whānau and those in need in his community. He probably [fishes](https://abigailhannah.nz/161-empowering-kiwi-women-in-hunting-fishing-with-wild-chix-isabell-zitzelsberger/) for trout, because that's what our rivers can afford to feed us with now. A bushman or bushwoman has the ecosystem literacy to live within the abundance the ecosystem can provide.

## Learning to trap in Te Urewera {#learning-to-trap}

Sam grew up with a dad who was a keen tramper and put him in the bush all the time, and among whānau who knew their plants and hunted and fished. Conservation wasn't a concept he knew he was learning. It was just how they operated: this is what the ecosystem can afford to give us, and this is what we have to do to keep abundance for generations to come. In many parts of the East Coast, he says, that's still how they operate.

At 12, after moving from the East Coast to Hawke's Bay, he was sent into the bush in Te Urewera to learn to be a trapper under some of the world's leading trappers and bushmen. That was his formal start in conservation. Through his teenage years, the bush was always a safe place, and Te Urewera was the safest of all. Most holidays, he was sent back there.

### Polytech, a mentor and being paid in pork

University in Wellington, in a city lecture theatre with 300 people, lasted a year. After travelling through forests in America and England, he did a two-year environmental course at Toi Ohomai, a polytech in the [Bay of Plenty](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/), where a mentor kept pulling him out of class to monitor bats, catch frogs and help stop gold mines.

As soon as he finished, he went back to Te Urewera, walked into the DOC office and asked for a job. They asked when he could start. He said tomorrow. They said come back Monday, and they'd pay him in pork.

So he turned up every day, living out of the back of his white station wagon on a mattress, washing in the river, paid in pork and a little bit of cash. After a while they realised he was sleeping in his car and found him somewhere to live. And they got him back into trapping, looking after kōkako in Te Urewera.

## Kōkako, from Te Urewera to Fiordland {#kokako}

I've seen kōkako on Tiritiri Mātangi, and hearing that spooky, screechy call for the first time was incredible. I still haven't seen a takahē, though.

After Te Urewera, Sam was sent down to [Fiordland](https://abigailhannah.nz/110-a-sea-kayaking-expedition-along-the-fiordland-coast-with-mark-jones/) for a couple of years. Trapping on Secretary Island, fresh from working with kōkako, he climbed a steep hill and heard a call he knew. A kōkako. On a Fiordland island.

He thought he'd found the South Island kōkako. Rather than sound silly on the radio, he finished his trapping, chased the call through the bush (it kept moving away) and told the boat's skipper he was 90% sure.

The skipper explained that North Island kōkako had been moved out there a few years earlier, and most had died. There was only one left. So Sam was right: it was a kōkako, a North Island one.

## The Koranga to Tawa Hut loop in the Waioeka {#koranga-tawa-loop}

These days Sam is a dad, and most of his adventures are [micro-adventures](https://abigailhannah.nz/195-from-everest-to-nz-expeditions-to-local-microadventures-with-tsewang-nuru-sherpa/) with his kids. But the loop he loves is in the Waioeka, between Ōpōtiki and Gisborne. He first did it at seven, and his kids are still too little for it.

### Road end to Koranga Forks Hut

It starts from Moanui Road, and it's a nice three-hour walk, 7.5 kilometres, to Koranga Forks Hut. There's so much food on this track. You'll see pikopiko along the edges, which you can pick for dinner, a fern Sam says tastes like macadamia nuts, lots of kareao and mushrooms.

At the hut, the trout fishing is phenomenal. It's a nice hut in winter, with a great log burner and a perspex lean-to to dry your boots. Sam loves it. When things aren't okay in town, he'll walk the three hours in midweek in winter, when no one's there, to hang out and catch trout.

### Up the river to Tawa Hut

From Koranga Forks Hut, you head 9.5 kilometres upstream. There's no track: you walk in the river. If you're long-legged like Sam, that takes about three hours, and if you're short-legged and fighting water the whole way, more like four and a half. It's a bit of a battle in winter, and a beautiful river to walk up in summer, through tawa forest with kahikatea and rimu. Walk it in the morning or evening and you'll see deer on the slips.

Tawa Hut is another beautiful hut with a great log burner, a modified traditional forest service hut like Koranga Forks, and very comfortable.

### From Tawa Hut into Te Urewera, or back to the car

Tawa Hut is a jumping-off point into Te Urewera, which not many people get to visit:

* **Over to Waimana**, where the kōkako are.
* **Up to a hut Sam describes as one of the last unburnt huts in Te Urewera**, with a long drop looking out over river flats where you'll often see pigs running.
* **Up to Kahunui Hut.**

Both rivers are popular for hunting, though popular on the East Coast means you probably won't see anyone for a couple of weeks.

To finish the loop, it's 8.7 kilometres and three hours from Tawa Hut back to the car park, on an easy, undulating track. There are no big hills anywhere on the loop, which is why Sam thinks it's so accessible. It's marked track to Koranga Forks Hut, the river to Tawa Hut, and marked track back, so you can't really get lost. The river can flood in winter, though, so keep an eye on it. As long as the river's not up, you'll find isolation and space to yourself easily in winter.

Three to four and a half hour days with no big hills sounds like my kind of tramping. And I love a North Island gem, because when I ask guests for their favourite place, it's so often Milford Sound or Aoraki.

## North Island forests and eating from the bush {#eating-from-the-bush}

Sam finds Te Waipounamu big, beautiful and very dramatic. But the beech forest down there often couldn't care less whether you're there or not. In the North Island, the forest is always trying to feed you and offer you rongoā to heal yourself, and it feels grateful you're there. As he puts it:

> "This place could kill me in a heartbeat, but then I come to the North Island, it's always like, oh, we'll look after you, boy, come back into the world."

That's why they take really minimal food into the bush (a mate did once carry in a whole beef brisket with cabbage and potatoes). They definitely don't take vegetables. They pride themselves on being able to hunt, fish and gather kai from the land. You can know all the plants intellectually, Sam says, but unless you practise mahinga kai, you're not really interacting with the ecosystem around you: the trees giving you kai, and you trapping to look after them, or hunting to lower the deer numbers.

## Raising kids in the bush {#kids-in-the-bush}

When Sam asked his dad how to parent, his dad told him to parent where he's most happy and confident. His dad was a surfer and a tramper, so he parented at the beach and in the bush. Sam knows the bush, so that's where he parents. He doesn't want to tell anyone else how to do it.

* **Mushrooming.** His daughter knows her mushroom spots, and every time they drive past, she wants to go mushrooming.
* **Fishing.** She's obsessed with it. She sits on his shoulders while they throw a spinner into the river, and his son has his own net for the trout.
* **Eating as they walk.** The kids love picking fruit off the ground, and the stories that go with each fruit and tree help them feel at home in the ecosystem.
* **Silver fern hide and seek.** His son's favourite game. Their ancestors flipped silver fern fronds shiny side up to find their way back at night, so his son lays tricky arrows of fronds for them to follow, and he's learning navigation through play. He's even made arrows from horopito, because it has a white underside like the silver fern.

I'll never forget a night hike to the Pinnacles with head torches. If you look up at a canopy of silver fern by head torch, it's so bright.

## Getting into conservation and bush knowledge {#getting-into-conservation}

### Where to start

Sam's advice for getting into trapping:

* **Join your local community group** or Predator Free group. There are so many around the country, they hold a lot of knowledge, and they'll teach you to trap, which is really simple.
* **Trap your backyard** or your local bit of bush.
* **Volunteer** on projects like the Eastern Whio Link.
* **Give money.** Community groups have been starved of conservation funding, Sam says, and DOC has been stripped of a lot of its funding too. What biodiversity really needs right now is dollars on the ground. The money you'd spend on backyard traps probably has more impact going to a project already protecting high value biodiversity.

When we were in the South Island a couple of summers ago, almost every track had traps along it from local community groups, and we'd make a donation to the group every time we walked one. Sam likens it to paying a hut fee (and not many people pay those either).

### Learning the bush

It's always better to go bush with someone who already has the knowledge, Sam says, because you learn in context and can be 100% confident. Not everyone has that, so books and social media help. He loves Liv Sisson's book, and he started making reels on Instagram, TikTok and Facebook to make bush knowledge digestible, alongside other creators sharing plant and tree knowledge.

His own book, _Sam the Trap Man: Cracking Yarns and Tall Tales from the Bush_, was written for a hunting audience around Father's Day. Ecology-focused readers loved that it wasn't too hunty, and hunters learned things about plants they never knew, many of which help you hunt better and are delicious too. It talks about plants in a non-extractive way, and when we recorded, it had just been nominated for an Ockham.

For Sam, hunting and conservation can't be separated. To harvest kai from our ecosystems, we need to do conservation, and to have abundance, we need to take out the deer and the trout. Unless you're working on an extractive model, or the hands-off approach he sees as an outdated model of conservation, everyone's in the same boat.

Sam also runs foraging and tree workshops, walking in the bush with catchment groups, farmers and conservation groups, and his son often comes along to share what he knows. As Sam sees it:

> "I think there's no better way of helping each other understand our ecosystems than going bush together, eh?"
