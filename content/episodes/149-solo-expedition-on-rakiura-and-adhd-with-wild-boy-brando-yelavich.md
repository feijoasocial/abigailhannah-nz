A couple of weekends before we recorded, I went to the premiere of Brando's film, Wild Boy, in Auckland. And there was this little section about Stewart Island that left me with so so many questions. Everyone tells me I have to get down there at some point, and from the pictures I'd seen, I figured it looked a bit like Fiordland. Then I saw his photos. Jaw on the floor.

Brando, aka Wild Boy, has spent the last ten years exploring the world, one expedition after another, starting as a teenager walking the coastline of Aotearoa. He calls it chasing knowledge of himself, and of how to be the best version of himself in the world. He joined me from the side of the road in the South Island, bush and all.

So we talk about where his love of adventure began, ADHD and telling his story without a filter, and his 35 days alone on Stewart Island: the scrub, the hunger, a birthday cake made of beef fat, and the day he changed the plan. Plus his take on risk, being present, and his one big tip for anyone planning an expedition (spoiler: it's short).

## Who is Wild Boy? {#who-is-wild-boy}

Over the last ten years, Brando says, it's been expedition after expedition, with sponsors, books and films along the way:

* **The coastline of Aotearoa**, walked as a teenager. His first.
* **Greenland**, crossed on a [ski expedition](https://abigailhannah.nz/south-pole-expedition-2/).
* **Vancouver Island**, circumnavigated by [sea kayak](https://abigailhannah.nz/170-122-day-self-supported-north-island-sea-kayak-expedition-with-joel-buckley/).
* **Australia**, cycled across.
* **The coastline of Stewart Island**, walked to finish off his New Zealand coastline.

## Cycling across Australia and finding water {#cycling-australia}

Cycling across Australia is something I would never, ever want to do. So much sand, so many bugs.

Funnily enough, Brando would go and do it again in a heartbeat. A bike gives you the freedom to travel long distances with luxuries you couldn't carry on foot, and it got him into remote places in Australia that cars couldn't reach and he couldn't have walked to with enough water and food.

Water was the big worry when he was planning, but there is so much water in the desert if you know where to look. He had an old map of all the surveyed wells in the outback, and drank from everything from old wooden wells with a metal bucket and rope to modern solar-powered pumps, plus a lot of watering holes. He wouldn't call that water stagnant. He reckons the rest of the population would.

## An Auckland childhood and a Waitākere river adventure {#childhood-adventure}

As a kid, Brando didn't go on big hikes or multi-day trips. He grew up on the North Shore in [Auckland](https://abigailhannah.nz/123-best-auckland-adventures-for-kids-with-outdoorsy-hijabi-mum-veronika-edali/), on the estuary, and the strip of bush between home and the mud flats was his adventure playground, where he explored and built ponga huts.

### The river walk that lit the spark

His first real adventure came from his granddad, a former tramping club member and scout leader. Brando reckons his granddad is him in an older body, in the adventurous sense, and also (he wants to put it out there) super irresponsible.

He took Brando and two cousins up into the Waitākere Ranges to explore waterfalls, and little Brando had a great idea: walk back along the river to the car park. He knew the river got there. What he didn't know is how rivers wind and wiggle through the bush, with waterfalls and drops and all sorts. His granddad said sure, he'd meet them at the car, and off he went.

They swam through rivers, with spiders running across the water. It was terrifying and so overstimulating, in the right way. They made it back long after they should have, his granddad still waiting, and that's when Brando's adventurous spirit was ignited.

He did cadets too, learning bushcraft, and joining the military was his goal right through his teens, so he went on any military training programme he could. When he reached the right age, he couldn't pass the numeracy tests to get in.

I feel like we're quite good at cotton wooling kids these days. Finding tadpoles at my local park, or long bike rides with a friend down to the river, came with a freedom I feel we're losing.

Brando thinks a lot of that comes down to screens: we have the time, and it's taken up by more addictive things.

## ADHD and sharing his story without a filter {#adhd-and-story}

Brando talks very openly about ADHD in the film. If he had to take a stab at why people connect with his story, it's because he doesn't really have a filter when it comes to sharing. He's developed the skill to capture his life as a storyteller and an explorer, and he shares himself as he is, because it's really easy to just be himself.

He's been the other way too, putting on a facade as this great extreme explorer. Stewart Island, he says, was:

> "my biggest teacher of not living life as a character, not pretending to be someone I'm not"

And so many people have ADHD, at different levels of the spectrum, that everyone knows somebody who's on it. He's a guy who decided to tell his story, and because we all have stories, it's relatable to anyone who identifies as a human.

## Why Brando went back to the wild on Stewart Island {#why-stewart-island}

After walking around Aotearoa, Brando tried to fit back into the world, and it didn't really work. He studied adventure tourism in Nelson at NMIT, qualifying as a guide and rock climbing instructor: two years of absolute epicness. Halfway through the second year, he wanted another big expedition. By then he had a large online following and sponsors, and Stewart Island was the first expedition he didn't pay for himself. It cost him a lot of time, he says, and his soul.

### Running back to the wild

He was dreaming of the freedom he'd had walking around New Zealand, away from all the stuff you accumulate: 30 socks that don't match, a messy desk, all the noise of life.

I feel this every summer. For the last couple of years we've gone away in [our van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/), and when I come home I wonder why we have all this stuff.

Brando says out there you really value the few things you have, and you don't carry anything you don't need or really, really want.

There was another pull too. The idea that the internet was proud of this Wild Boy character was very intoxicating, and for someone with ADHD, the dopamine from proving he was an extreme explorer was really inviting. Stewart Island pulled him like a magnet, to do something extreme so he could say he was extreme. He often said he was born in the wrong era: 300 years ago, people would have told stories about him. So he decided to write his own.

## Stewart Island's coastline, caves and scrub {#stewart-island-landscape}

The plan was big and elaborate: walk the exact coastline of [Stewart Island](https://abigailhannah.nz/194-summer-as-a-doc-ranger-on-stewart-island-and-women-in-trades-with-brooke-thompson/), the way he'd walked around Aotearoa. Parts of the coast are inaccessible, so it wasn't practical to begin with. And he didn't realise how rugged the island would be.

Brando compares the bush to alpine scrub put on a coastal island and made tight and windswept, and the only similar coastal vegetation he knows is on the [Catlins](https://abigailhannah.nz/139-slow-travel-in-an-ev-and-catlins-dunedin-and-oamaru-with-mature-flaneur-tim-ward/) coast. [Fiordland](https://abigailhannah.nz/186-packrafting-and-tramping-48-days-through-fiordland-with-penzy-dinsdale/) is big and wild and bushy. Stewart Island is big and wild and shrubby, everything stunted and windswept, so you climb over it or under it. It's a tough place.

### From Oban into the wilderness

Brando doesn't research much beyond checking a map and the terrain. You arrive in Oban, the tiny village that is Stewart Island's township, which looks like any coastal fishing village, with a Four Square, a fish and chip shop and a pub. Then you head into the wilderness and quickly realise it's like being subantarctic: in the mountains, on the beach. He hadn't been anywhere like it.

### Beaches, bones and granite

The beaches kept changing: sandy, stony, schisty, no beach at all. The further he got from the real world (or the fake world we all try to exist in, as he puts it), the more he found caves with history in them, old bottles and bones, and beaches littered with thousands of years' worth of whale bones, and shipwrecks.

Further again were huge towering granite rocks that look like they've been put there. Between the stretches of scrub there were little openings, with the sea and the sun lighting everything up. Inland, there were swamps and waterfalls. It doesn't feel like a very big place, he says, but he spent 35 days there, and it's a big place.

## Food drops and a birthday on Stewart Island {#food-and-birthday}

On the day he left, Brando had a big breakfast at a little cafe and ate a pie. Then he went cold turkey, straight to 100 grams of food a day, trying to survive off nothing.

The first two weeks were psychological hell. He didn't want to be there, didn't know why he was there, and was so hungry that food was all he could think about. He resorted to eating rats, roasted on sticks over the fire.

### The beach where the planes land

About a quarter of the way around is a huge long beach where planes land on the sand. People walk from there through the centre of the island to Freshwater Hut and get a boat back to town. Brando's tip: if you've only got two days, it's a fantastic way to do it, and you'd see kiwi in the daytime.

For Brando, it was a chance to get staples flown in. Seven Sharp came to interview him (he was not a good interviewee, he says, being so over the expedition by then), and brought a bag of flour, a drum of beef dripping and some rice to go with his 100 grams of freeze-dried food a day.

### A beef-fat birthday cake

About a week later, it was his birthday. He made a cake out of mashed-up rice, flour and beef fat, stuck an emergency candle in it and sang himself happy birthday. It was mass calories, it did what it needed to do, and that extra food made a huge difference to his mental state heading into the wilder part of the island.

### Comms from the island

This was the early days of satellite communication, about eight years before we recorded. He had a big Iridium satellite phone that sent tiny thumbnail images, and he checked in with home every day. Each day he wrote an email, a brain dump of emotions and thoughts, that went up on Facebook or Instagram so his audience could follow along. He didn't read the replies: it was one way. The odd phone call was possible, but very, very expensive, and he carried an emergency locator beacon too.

## Hitting the bottom and changing the plan {#changing-the-plan}

At one point Brando stood at the bottom of Stewart Island, looking out to the ocean, and couldn't see a single boat or person. He was, he says, both psychologically and physically the lowest person in New Zealand, and so lonely. He'd gone there to show the world he was great, and out there it was just him, really thick scrub, hardly any food, bruised legs and a damaged ego.

### Days under the scrub

After his birthday he hit scrub so thick he was crawling underneath it, dragging his pack, and for days he didn't see the sky. A saying he'd been telling people kept playing in his head:

> "if you're not having fun, change something"

But changing the plan felt like failing, and it was eating him up. Every day he wanted to quit. Then he broke down, had a big cry, yelled at the sky, and calmed down.

### Up onto the tops

The tops were usually easier to navigate, so he headed straight up. At the top, it was open, and the sun was setting over the big beautiful rocks you see in the film. It looked like Jurassic World.

In his tent behind some rocks, he wrote in his journal and decided to change the plan. He didn't want to come home saying he'd done a really hard thing and had a horrible time. A straight line back would still have taken ten days, and he wanted to explore, so he picked a handful of geographically unique places on his maps, anything that sparked his curiosity, along a path back towards Freshwater Hut.

It was still so hard, up in the mountains where it hailed or snowed instead of rained. But that change of perspective changed everything. After about five days of feeling like he'd given up, he reached his first location, a massive waterfall that seemed too big for such a small island, and realised this was awesome. It showed him that doing things for other people isn't the answer.

### A collection of struggles

He knows there were really good bits on his journeys, and he thinks life is the same:

> "life is just a collection of struggles with good bits along the way"

So he chases the struggle, the type two fun you never want to do again, and wow, you did it. That's where the magic is, he says, and what creates character.

## Risk, ADHD and being present {#risk-and-presence}

A listener asked how he drew the line between pushing his limits and staying alive. On every expedition, Brando says, there's a chance he might not come back, but over his life he's built up a very healthy sense of risk. He's very good at seeing the risks in a situation, and that comes with time and experience. When he was much younger, he fell off cliffs and got dragged along the ground by pigs.

ADHD helps. He's hyper aware, and as soon as there's risk involved, his brain is working at its most premium. The riskiest thing for him, he reckons, is a long boring drive or a desk. Some people meditate in silence. He meditates in crazy storms, on a bike or in a snow storm.

The weekend before we recorded, I was [kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/114-sea-kayaking-for-beginners-and-the-best-of-the-hauraki-gulf-with-bennett-lim/) over to Tiritiri Matangi Island in Auckland with my phone left in the car. I saw takahē for the first time, and seals, and kept reaching for my phone. Instead I had to take it in.

Brando agrees. Take technology out of an adventure and you might forget the fantail feather next to your boot, but you won't forget the whole. He'd rather sit around later with the friends you went with and remember it together, revisiting past chapters for the nostalgia and the lessons. Ask him again in ten years, he says, and he'd have very different lessons from Stewart Island.

## A sea lion and tips for planning an expedition {#expedition-tips}

Another listener had been chased by a sea lion in the dark. Brando never has, though he's stepped on the odd seal walking beaches before dawn to beat the tide around headlands. Once on Stewart Island, he went down to the water for a wash and a big bull sea lion came flying out at him, barking and growling. There was no washing that night.

### Brando's one big tip: just start

His most useful advice is to start. Within reason, it doesn't take long to plan an expedition: get the things you need to keep yourself alive if something goes wrong, and go. You'll pick up what you need along the way, and get rid of what you thought you needed.

He'd never been on a [bike touring](https://abigailhannah.nz/beginner-bikepacking/) mission before Australia, and didn't even know how to change his tubeless tyres. Thankfully, someone on a bike on the side of the road helped him.

Adventures, like multi-day trips, can be planned for, he says. An expedition's outcome is completely uncertain, so he likes a start point and a finish point, and everything in between is living.
