Ciska and I are basically twins. She runs New Zealand Adventures, I run Aotearoa Adventures, and we both want the same thing: to help people discover what's right around the corner.

Ciska and her husband live in Tauranga, and she is the person to ask about the Bay of Plenty. She has thousands of pins saved on her Google Maps and confesses to lying awake at 3am hunting for hidden gems. In one of our first weeks living in the van, she took us to one of her favourite spots, and I've been soooo keen to pick her brain properly ever since.

So we build a road trip through Rotorua, Tauranga and the Coromandel, with a few detours (Taupō, the Manawatū, and the reason you should save the pin). Plus the Mount Maunganui tracks most people don't know, the glowworm chasm at McLaren Falls, waterfall after waterfall, and how she does a weekend road trip in 48 hours.

## From a family yacht to Tauranga {#family-yacht-to-tauranga}

Ciska's parents and older siblings were born in Holland and moved to New Zealand, and she grew up in Auckland. Her family was outdoorsy in a different way: they had a family yacht and spent a lot of time going out to Waiheke Island and the Bay of Islands. They even did a stint in Fiji, [sailing](https://abigailhannah.nz/025-sailing-stephanie-haden/) around remote outer islands. She lived in Holland for a year and went to school there for a while, too. Adventurous overseas and on the water, then. Hiking, though? A big fat no.

She was too young for the big open-sea crossings, and being out where all you can see is ocean freaks her out a bit.

Same. If we ever can't see land on our kayak trip from [Cape Reinga to Tauranga](https://abigailhannah.nz/162-we-are-kayaking-from-cape-reinga-to-tauranga-to-raise-20-000-/), we've done something very, very wrong.

### Leaving Auckland

After university and seven years running her own business, Ciska did the thing she'd always wanted: a long solo trip around the world on a one-way ticket, with no plan.

When she got back, she and her husband moved to Tauranga. Auckland had become too busy and too big, and they'd been visiting family in Tauranga on weekends for years. They loved having the beach right there, the mountains, fewer people, and how friendly everyone was. When we recorded, a Facebook memory had reminded her it had been 11 years. No regrets.

That move is part of how New Zealand Adventures started. Without an hour in traffic each way, they had more time, and they wanted to spend it outdoors, [waterfall chasing](https://abigailhannah.nz/north-island-hidden-gems/) and exploring around Rotorua, the Coromandel, Taupō and Whakatāne. They made little videos for friends and family (partly to convince everyone to move down), and when a friend suggested sharing them publicly, her first answer was no way.

## McLaren Falls Park and the glowworm chasm {#mclaren-falls}

In one of our first weeks in [the van](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/), we went to Tauranga and wondered why we didn't go more often. We paddled in the harbour and around the Mount to some of the islands, and saw seals and a little blue penguin off the coast. Then we met up with Ciska at McLaren Falls Park.

There's a glowworm chasm at McLaren Falls, plus a second, smaller waterfall that also has glowworms at night.

You can see the chasm on a night tour, or take your own kayak or paddleboard. It's quite a short paddle past a little power station, and you need to know where to go. The chasm has tall green mossy walls, little waterfalls and huge mossy boulders at the end. The whole experience is magical.

We went in the daytime, so no glowworms, but it felt like something out of a fairy tale. I think in the dark you'd lose the scale and the greenery.

Ciska's tip: go just before evening and stay until dark.

The park is far bigger than I realised. I didn't save the pin she sent, then arrived to a huge park full of narrow roads and no reception. Save the pin.

The park has a proper campground, free barbecues, an animal park, a cafe and frisbee golf, so it's a good one if you've got a van.

## Rotorua and Taupō: culture, hot water and lake waterfalls {#rotorua-taupo}

If someone flies into Auckland with a week (even though you need a few months), Ciska would send a visitor to [Rotorua](https://abigailhannah.nz/038-drone-photography-rotorua-petra-leary/) first, for two or three days:

* **Culture.** Go somewhere like Te Puia, where you get a Māori welcome and an insight into the culture.
* **Free thermal areas.** Kerosene Creek is beautiful and worth doing.
* **Hot pools.** There are plenty of thermal pools, like the Polynesian Spa, and a new one that had opened when we recorded.

From there, pop over to Taupō. There are loads of waterfalls on Lake Taupō itself: a boat tour takes you to the main ones, and some you can only reach in a small dinghy or a kayak.

We drove past the lake the week before we recorded, and Isaac suggested we paddle around it. How long have we got? There are also the Māori rock carvings, which you can visit on a tour or in your own boat.

## Tauranga and Mount Maunganui tracks {#mount-maunganui}

Next, head over to Tauranga for the Mount, the bustling cafes and the coffee culture, which is quite big there. There are amazing hikes and waterfalls, too.

Ciska goes up [Mount Maunganui](https://abigailhannah.nz/094-climbing-mt-maunganui-38-times-in-14-days-and-living-a-nomadic-lifestyle-with-bri-obrien/) all the time, and takes different routes:

* **The old way up.** Straight up the guts, with scrambling, tree roots and a hole in the rock to climb through near the top. It's not recommended: always stick to the tracks.
* **The goat track.** Up the four-wheel-drive side. It was her favourite, with a bit of rock climbing, until signs went up a few years ago asking people to stay off it because of erosion.
* **The bird track, from the Pilot Bay side.** Go about halfway around the Mount. The first few minutes are full of doves and other birds (and smell a bit), then it's straight up lots of new stairs, rebuilt after a landslip. It gets your heart pumping.

## Waterfalls around Tauranga {#tauranga-waterfalls}

Kaiate Falls blew my mind. You think you're at the falls, then you keep following the track and there are more falls, and more falls. I imagine it's pumping in summer, but I really highly rate it.

Ciska agrees: there's another fall, then another one.

### Omanawa Falls

The work that's gone into Omanawa Falls blows Ciska's mind, and it's a whole experience from the car park on. There are three viewpoints:

* **The big platform**, which overhangs and looks out at the falls.
* **The old conveyor belt viewpoint**, part of the old power station, with a slightly different view.
* **The viewpoint near the top.** A long walk with lots of stairs, and no good view of the falls, so skip it if you're short on time.

That conveyor belt viewpoint is the one I've been to.

### More falls, on and off the map

In Te Puke there's Raparapahoe Falls. And at another popular waterfall, a friend of Ciska's has found a chasm a bit like McLaren Falls, which they plan to explore on boogie boards (lighter than kayaks).

There are lots of hidden waterfalls too, either on private land where you need to know the landowners, or on the side of the road where you need to know where the little hole in the bush is. When we recorded, a farmer was about to take them down to the waterfall on their property.

## A Coromandel road trip, from Whiritoa to Fletcher Bay {#coromandel-road-trip}

From Tauranga, Ciska would head up the coast and do the big Coromandel loop. You could spend weeks there, and by now, she jokes, you've extended your New Zealand trip by six years and ended up living here.

### Whiritoa to Whangamatā

* **Whiritoa.** One of their favourite local gems. They call it a blowhole, but no water shoots up, and there are lots of secret little nooks and crannies.
* **Waihī Beach.** Nice for an hour or so.
* **Homunga Bay.** It has a beach waterfall.
* **Whangamatā.** One of their favourites there is the magic Donut Island.

I've paddled Donut Island a few times in rented kayaks, and the rental shops sort you out with wetsuits, directions and the weather. The first time, we tagged on behind a guided tour into the cave, which is a bit scary if you're not used to swell. We've also overshot it, because you have to turn around to see the gap in the rocks.

Ciska says it doesn't feel like New Zealand.

### Onemana and Pokohino

Onemana is amazing, says Ciska, and a good place to base yourself. Pokohino Beach is now quite popular, and back when it wasn't, they'd camp overnight there with a big bonfire and their tent on the beach.

We've spent a lot of weekends at a friends' bach in Onemana. Once, with the forestry road to Pokohino closed, we walked the whole track in jandals, with cutty grass cutting up our feet, and had the beach to ourselves.

### Slipper Island, Pauanui and the east coast

* **Slipper Island.** Not many people know about it. Most of it is owned by the resort there, with a small area where people live, and you can rent out pretty much the whole island. It's magic.
* **Pauanui.** There's a tiny waterfall, and it's cool to drive through and see how the rich and famous live, with planes in their backyards and private waterways with their own wharves and jetties.
* **Hot Water Beach, Cooks Beach, Hahei and Whitianga.**

When we recorded, Cathedral Cove was open again after being closed for a few summers, and I think they've done a pretty good job with the new track.

### New Chums to Fletcher Bay, and back down to Thames

Then shoot across to New Chums Beach. Even the drive up is full of little beaches and amazing walks. Ciska really enjoyed driving right up to Port Jackson and the Fletcher Bay area, which not many people do. It's secluded and raw, and she highly recommends it, even for Kiwis, for a few days.

I'd love to drive that road, which follows the coastline past all these ancient pōhutukawa, when they're in bloom.

Coming back down the other side towards Thames to finish the loop, there's the Pinnacles walk, loads of nice little green waterholes and a couple of waterfalls. In summer, with the pōhutukawa out, it's magic, with amazing fishing and the mussel barges out on the water.

## Beyond the Bay: the Manawatū and Auckland {#beyond-the-bay}

From the Coromandel you could head back to Auckland, or across to Raglan via Hamilton, to Waitomo Caves and all the waterfalls, hikes and caves in that area, or around [Taranaki](https://abigailhannah.nz/156-taranaki-three-peaks-traverse-and-supporting-families-in-the-outdoors-with-jenn-julian/) on the surf highway.

I was in the Manawatū when we recorded, wondering what there was to do. Ciska's pick is Paloma Gardens, on private land: a massive bamboo forest with a yellow walkway, colourful stairs, sculptures, a bridge over water and a greenhouse full of ginormous cacti.

### Don't skip Auckland

Overseas visitors often get told to leave Auckland as soon as they're through the airport gates. Ciska thinks [Auckland](https://abigailhannah.nz/123-best-auckland-adventures-for-kids-with-outdoorsy-hijabi-mum-veronika-edali/) is massive, with so much beauty, especially the west coast beaches, hikes and caves. North Auckland has really clear water on the east side, and South Auckland has amazing hikes and waterfalls. And the food.

That's how my own adventures started, in the regional parks like Tāwharanui, Te Ārai, Shakespear and Wenderholm. Even without leaving the city, there's Mount Eden, Māngere Mountain and One Tree Hill.

## 48-hour road trips and finding hidden gems {#finding-hidden-gems}

Ciska's favourite way to road trip is in a campervan. They specialise in 48-hour road trips: leave after work on a Friday night and make a weekend of it. Sometimes it's a 24-hour one: drive at night, wake up at the destination (a campground or a freedom camping spot), go hard for the day, then drive home.

A small van is easy to park, and you can stop right at a waterfall or somewhere really secluded, with a little gas cooker for a meal on the beach. These days they also enjoy the comforts of a car and an Airbnb.

I'm biased, obviously. Campervan.

### How Ciska finds the spots

* **Talk to the locals.** Lately, almost every weekend, someone has shown them their private waterfall or waterhole, then mentioned another one up the road that needs no permission.
* **Zoom in on Google Maps.** If a river has one waterfall, it often has loads.
* **Read the reviews.** Google Maps reviews often have extra tips you won't find anywhere else.
* **Use offline maps.** They use AllTrails' offline maps a lot in Rarotonga, where there's no reception.

### Why she shares it

New Zealand Adventures started as a way to keep their memories, and went public to inspire families, friends and solo travellers to explore their own areas and a bit further. New Zealand is so good, she says, it wouldn't be fair not to share it.

> "you don't need to spend money. You don't need to be going to shopping malls and stuff. You just get out and jump in the ocean or jump under a waterfall and just how magic that is and how much happiness it can give you."

The best part is when someone comments that they've lived here their whole life and never knew a place was there. For Ciska, it's like free therapy:

> "it gives you so much joy and happiness and you can experience that with the people you love, or the strangers"

When we recorded, she'd been working all year on a public map with about 800 pins, due to launch soon. And if you're planning a trip to a specific area, she's happy for you to send her a DM.
