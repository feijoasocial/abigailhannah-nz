Charlotte had the most epic summer. I was trying to follow along with her stories, and it felt like she was everywhere at once. So I had to get her on to explain herself.

Charlotte is 22, a Kiwi girl who works a normal Monday to Friday job and makes the absolute most of her weekends. She's a climber, a winter ocean swimmer, an outdoor ed graduate and, as of this summer, a [packrafter](https://abigailhannah.nz/196-packrafting-the-landsborough-river-from-the-west-coast-with-tom-waters/). And she shares it all to get more people outside, which is pretty much exactly why I started this podcast.

So we talk about how she grew up outdoors, her outdoor ed degree, and three weeks in the South Island where she did something every single day. Then we go deep on packrafting: buying the boats two days before the trip, a two-day mission to Lake Crucible with icebergs at the top and the Wilkin River on the way out, the Eglinton River and Tasman Lake. Plus her safety tips and gear list. I'm a girl who does NOT like getting cold and wet, and she might have sold me on it anyway.

## How Charlotte got into the outdoors {#charlotte-story}

Charlotte has always been an outdoorsy girl. She grew up in what she calls a typical Kiwi household, spending weekends at the beach, on walks and on lots of bike rides. She has vivid memories of camping at Hahei and out at Muriwai as a kid (more glamping, she says), and of walking to a waterfall in the pouring rain in her little raincoat and gumboots.

As she got older she started hiking with friends, went from competitive swimming to swimming in the ocean all through winter, and took up climbing once she started work. She's recently become a lifeguard too.

Since meeting her boyfriend, she's been pushed out of her comfort zone into lesser-known hikes, where you get an almost eerie sense that not many people have been before. They've got into canyoning, packrafting and, most recently, whitewater rafting. She thinks she's addicted. Even so, she says getting outside doesn't have to be big: a hike in your local area gives you so much.

## Studying outdoor education at AUT {#outdoor-education}

Charlotte's mindset has always been to do what you enjoy, and you'll end up fine. She loved being outdoors and loved getting other people outdoors, so she studied outdoor education at AUT. When we recorded, it was no longer offered as a major.

The trips were her favourite part. She slept under a bivvy for the first time, and did sea kayaking and whitewater kayaking. Students could borrow the gear in their own time, which was huge, because not everyone has a whitewater kayak. And it gave her confidence, which she sees as the first step before anyone can trust you to guide them.

It also gave her some of her closest friends. In her words:

> "There's something about being outdoors with people that just bonds you in a way that you can't in any other way."

My first night under a bivvy was in a borrowed AUT bivvy too. And I've met so many of my friends through the outdoors. All the cool people live outdoors, I reckon.

## The Pinnacles and a sunrise redemption {#pinnacles}

We both have history with the Pinnacles.

I held a grudge against them for seven years. We left Auckland at midnight, started walking at about 2am and got to the top for sunrise, and there was nothing to see. I'm a girl who really really needs her sleep, so after an all-nighter with no views, we were delirious on the way down. Then I went back the weekend before we recorded, stayed at the hut, and the views were spectacular. My rule of thumb now: sunset is a better bet up there than sunrise. I also found out the stairs were made for horses, which explains why they're so brutally huge.

Charlotte reckons they need a zipline. She walked it with two friends a few years ago, booked so far in advance that they couldn't wait for a weather window. They went up for sunrise in rain and fog, sure the sun would burn it off. It didn't, so she might need a redemption trip too.

That's the catch with the hut: it books out so far ahead that you can't really check the weather and go. It's a very bougie hut, though, with lights and a cold shower.

## Three weeks in the South Island {#south-island-trip}

Charlotte and her boyfriend first went to the South Island together the winter before, and came back thinking it was the best trip ever. Somehow, they topped it. This summer they flew to Nelson, borrowed a car and spent the next 21 days road tripping.

There was no set plan, but there was a list. Her boyfriend spends hours studying topo maps and finds all the hidden gems, and everything went onto the offline topo map on their phones, colour-coded. With no accommodation booked, they followed the weather. Why sit in bad weather when you can drive two hours north into sunshine?

They did something every single day. Whenever Charlotte thought she needed a rest day, she told herself she could rest back in Auckland.

Her highlights:

* [**Earnslaw Burn**](https://abigailhannah.nz/037-earnslaw-burn-gertrude-saddle-lauren-murray/), with all its waterfalls and the glacier. Crazy cool.
* **Isthmus Peak**, really cool as well.
* **Rob Roy Glacier**, walked with some girls she met through the outdoor community on Instagram. You can make friends on Instagram now.
* **Packrafting** on Tasman Lake, at Lake Crucible and through small chasms.
* [**Canyoning**](https://abigailhannah.nz/105-a-multi-day-canyoning-trip-in-abel-tasman-with-aimee-jules/), plus a bit of tubing, mountain biking and mountain karting at Cardrona.

## Getting into packrafting as a beginner {#getting-into-packrafting}

Charlotte and her boyfriend got their packrafts very recently: two days before the trip. She'd told him she wanted to try whitewater rafting, and he managed to get them a whitewater raft. They wanted to take it to the South Island's rivers, but it was just too big. So they looked into packrafts instead.

Theirs are average packrafts, nothing fancy.

A listener asked what experience they had before buying. Both were confident in the outdoors, and Charlotte has done [whitewater kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/white-water-kayaking-nz/) and guided kayaking. Neither had been in a packraft, but she found it similar to a whitewater kayak, and more stable.

New pursuits intimidate me, because I want an instruction booklet: this is what you do, these are the risks, this is how you stay safe.

So how did they learn? They eased into it, and talked to people they knew who did similar things about the risks, because a lot goes on in rivers that you can't see. They stuck to grade one rivers and a couple of really nice grade twos, with small rapids and a few wave trains. A lot of the rivers were so shallow she doubts you could flip.

If you're starting out:

* **Do a course.** There are packrafting courses.
* **Try a guided trip** before you invest. Another listener asked how to test a packraft without buying all the gear, and a guided trip will be cheaper than buying a packraft, a life jacket and everything else.
* **Borrow one** from a friend who has a packraft.
* **Use packraftingtripsnz.** It's a free website with a map of packrafting trips and trip reports, so you can see who's paddled a river recently and whether there are new hazards. They use it so much they've started donating to it.

## Lake Crucible by jet boat, foot and packraft {#lake-crucible}

Lake Crucible was the highlight of the trip for both of them, because it involved so much: jet boating, hiking and packrafting. It's in Mount Aspiring National Park, and her boyfriend found it after seeing a YouTube video of someone paddling on an iceberg-filled lake. Charlotte's reaction: we have to go.

They made it a two-day trip, which she says is the common way to do it, because there's a bit of getting in and out.

### Wilkin River Jets to Kerin Forks

They started at Makarora and took the Wilkin River Jets 20 minutes up the river to Kerin Forks, where there's also a hut on the other side of the river. The jet boat is way more fun than walking that first stretch, she says, and the price was pretty reasonable.

It had a bonus, too. They were packrafting back down the Wilkin on their first ever packrafting trip, and she'll admit she was scared. Seeing the river from the jet boat gave her peace of mind: if a jet boat could get up it, beginner packrafters should be okay.

### Packing two packrafts into tramping packs

Packing was an adventure in itself. Packrafts roll up small, hence the name, but their carry bags weren't comfortable, so the rafts went in hiking packs with more support. The paddles split into four pieces. With a tent and sleeping bags too, they had three bags between them.

### Siberia Valley and up to Lake Crucible

From Kerin Forks they walked to Siberia Hut, through the valley, then up to Lake Crucible. The climb was so steep it almost felt like a rock climb, but a fun kind of steep, grabbing tree branches with the valley behind you. In the Siberia Valley, Charlotte kept asking herself if this was really New Zealand.

At the top, her jaw dropped. There were icebergs all over the lake, glaciers all around and a few waterfalls. Do a 360 spin.

Charlotte got scared of falling into that freezing water, so she was the photographer while her boyfriend went for a paddle. On the way up, people had asked if they were going to paddle down the big waterfall they could see. No, very dangerous. Just the lake.

They camped back down in the valley. Charlotte recommends camping if you do this trip: there are so many places to camp in that huge valley, as long as you do it responsibly.

Lake Crucible has come up on the podcast before, on a loop over [Gillespie Pass](https://abigailhannah.nz/023-hiking-pregnant-alice/) that comes out through Siberia and down the Wilkin.

### Day two: packrafting the Wilkin River

On day two, they walked back to where the jet boat had dropped them. This time, instead of catching the jet boat down, they blew up the packrafts by the river. They use a pump bag, like a camping air mattress: fill it with air and squish it down. Their bags went in dry bags at their feet, under the spray skirts. Some packrafts have a T-zip for storing gear inside the tube, but those cost more.

The jet boat knew they were coming, so they moved to the side when it passed.

At the first little rapid, her boyfriend said they'd go right. Charlotte protested that it was a rapid. "Charlotte, that's kind of the point," he said. Once she knew the boat was stable, the rest was so much fun, with a snowy mountain behind them. She smiled the whole way down.

When she felt nervous on later rapids, she reminded herself to stay calm and think rationally. Her paddle was basically touching the bottom, the flow wasn't crazy, and if anything happened, she could hop out and get back in. As she puts it:

> "I know as soon as you start to panic, you're kind of done for."

I need to carry that into my sea kayaking. When you panic, everything feels so big and scary.

### The walk out and the hitchhike

At the end, they crossed the other river (nice and easy that day) and carried their packrafts back to the road. The plan: her boyfriend would [hitchhike](https://abigailhannah.nz/hitchhiking-new-zealand/) to the car, 5 kilometres away, while Charlotte packed up the rafts. There's a lot of hitchhiking in packrafting, she says.

Two things went wrong. The unpacked rafts were really hard to carry, so she wishes they'd packed them up earlier. And he couldn't get a ride, so he ran 4 kilometres before someone picked him up in the last one. Meanwhile it started raining, and all she wanted was to get to Wānaka for a big burger.

With tramping, you throw your bag in the car and go get your burger. With sea kayaking, there are a few more steps first.

Charlotte still reckons she gets more out of packrafting than hiking, so it's worth it.

## Packrafting the Eglinton River and Tasman Lake {#eglinton-tasman-lake}

The Wilkin gave Charlotte a real confidence boost. By the end, she wanted bigger rapids and wave trains.

### Eglinton River

Near Te Anau, they paddled the Eglinton River. You normally park at the end and hitchhike up, and two packrafters they met there ended up being their ride, with some tips:

* **The river had changed.** There'd been a slip since they'd last paddled it, so stay river right to avoid a tree.
* **Prime your boat.** Pump it up, then splash it with water. In the South Island, the cold water can make your boat deflate a little.

I learned that one this summer, rafting with friends: once it's on the cold water, you top it up a bit.

The Eglinton was beautiful, with lupins all around, a gorge and a couple of waterfalls. At one grade two section, the water hits a wall. Charlotte spun around, and only afterwards did her boyfriend tell her that had been the grade two bit. Sometimes it's better not to know, she says.

### Tasman Lake among the icebergs

[Tasman Lake](https://abigailhannah.nz/029-tasman-glacier-guy-alisha/) is where they found out their boats deflate in freezing water. They paddled past icebergs with the glacier at the far end, a completely different experience from the viewpoint most people walk to. Charlotte felt safe on the flat, calm lake, with tourist boats heading out to the glacier nearby.

When we were at Tasman Lake, our photographer friends asked if we could get our kayaks out there. The lake freaks me out, because the glacier can calve, and that day it was way too windy anyway. Packrafts are so much more practical for getting to where you want to start.

They also paddled a few lesser-known places they're keeping to themselves, through chasm walls with crystal clear water, where people on foot and in bigger boats can't go.

That's why we got into [sea kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/114-sea-kayaking-for-beginners-and-the-best-of-the-hauraki-gulf-with-bennett-lim/): we'd look down at beaches we couldn't reach. Now that we're kayaking the [coastline from Cape Reinga to Tauranga](https://abigailhannah.nz/162-we-are-kayaking-from-cape-reinga-to-tauranga-to-raise-20-000-/), I can't wait to get into those caves and rock pools. [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/) hasn't got me into white water yet, because I get really cold when I get wet. But in a packraft, the aim is to stay in your boat, so I could potentially be convinced.

## Packrafting safety tips and gear {#safety-and-gear}

Charlotte's safety advice:

* **Research the river.** Look at the guides, recent trip reports and even YouTube videos. The amount of water changes all the time and can bring new hazards.
* **Check the weather and the flow** in cumecs, which tells you how much water is coming down.
* **Know where to get out.** They always had the topo map on a phone in a waterproof case, because go too far on some rivers and you could end up at a big waterfall.
* **Carry warm gear and a** [**PLB**](https://abigailhannah.nz/plb-safety/)**.**
* **Communicate with jet boats.** The packrafters they met had once been given a radio by a jet boat driver, so he could tell them which side to keep to.
* **Scout rapids.** You can get out and look, and on many trips you can walk around.
* **Know your limits.** It's okay to skip something too far out of your comfort zone.

> "the last thing you want to happen is to be pushed a bit too far and be put off it forever."

The whole point of being outdoors is to have fun, she says. Work your way up, and one day you'll love it. And when a rapid looks interesting, Charlotte sings to herself.

Her gear list:

* **The packraft kit:** the raft, its bag, the pump and a repair kit.
* **A life jacket and a spray jacket.**
* **A wetsuit**, because South Island water is cold, with a fluffy thermal wetsuit top underneath for extra warmth.
* **Water-friendly shoes** that are also good for hiking and don't weigh her down if she falls in.
* **The topo map on her phone and PLBs.**

Her final word: do it. There's a cool community, including Facebook groups where people look for company on trips.

## The Hollyford Pyke loop and a growing bucket list {#bucket-list}

Charlotte grew up in the North Island, so she's seen the beaches up north, the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/) and a bit of out west. The South Island scratches an itch, and she wants to keep exploring it in less usual ways: packrafting, canyoning and whitewater rafting.

One trip she's looked into is the Hollyford Pyke loop in Fiordland. One river takes you out to the coast, then you cross a saddle to the top of another river that brings you back to where you started. Loops are rare on rivers, but these two valleys go the right direction.

Isaac has been looking at renting packrafts to do it. We didn't get to half of what we wanted this summer, because the weather wasn't great. The tramps will still be there.

Charlotte has stopped making a list. It got too long.
