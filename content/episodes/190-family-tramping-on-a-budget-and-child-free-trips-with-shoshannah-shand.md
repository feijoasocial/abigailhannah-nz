Shoshannah was one of my very first guests. Back then she was [living in a bus with a toddler](https://abigailhannah.nz/010-toddler-travel-shoshannah/), and the podcast was a little baby. Now she's based in Canterbury, homeschooling two kids, and still tramping as often as she can.

She's a solo mum who shares tramping and camping adventures with her two kids to 160,000 followers across social media, and she's been featured on Seven Sharp and The Herald. So many families look to her for proof that you don't need to be a hardcore athlete, or spend a fortune, to take your kids on adventures.

So this one is packed. Kids' gear on a budget, food, huts or tents, how she manages risk (and the keyboard warriors), and a very wet turnaround on the Paparoa with a baby and a five-year-old. Plus her first child-free trips (turns out you can leave the kids at home), including a big day over snowy tops from Lake Christabel.

## Growing up in the Marlborough Sounds {#shoshannah-story}

Shoshannah grew up at the top of the South Island in the Marlborough Sounds, on a sheep, cattle and mussel farm three hours from town. Her whole childhood was fishing, hunting, camping and tramping, with camping trips to D'Urville Island every year.

She grew up in the outer Pelorus Sound, on the opposite side to Queen Charlotte Sound, so she's never been to Queen Charlotte. There was no reason to go. It's on her list.

We spent six weeks in the [top of the South](https://abigailhannah.nz/192-six-week-road-trip-in-the-top-of-the-south-island-with-abigail-isaac/) a couple of summers ago and kayaked Queen Charlotte. It was 50-50 between that and Pelorus, so we'll have to go back for Pelorus.

Shoshannah became a solo parent with her first child seven years ago, and that's where her social media journey started. After the first COVID lockdown, she put a mattress in the back of her car and started car camping around New Zealand. She shared more and more of the travel, got back into tramping after a few years' break, and loved it all over again.

## From bus life to a home in Canterbury {#bus-to-canterbury}

She loved bus life, but after a year she needed regular stability, and felt a strong pull towards Canterbury, where she had a good community and so much to do outdoors. Now she's back in a house, with gardens and a bit of a balance between home and travel, after three years of renovating.

People think she does a lot more than she does. In the bus, everything was right outside the door. Now they drive (though half her house lives in her car). She plans a tramp about once a month, usually when cabin fever kicks in, and in between they spend lots of time outside with other homeschoolers, at the river, the beach, the bush or the playground. She's enjoying the slower pace.

## Tramping gear for kids on a budget {#gear-on-a-budget}

Going from one kid to two was mostly about the mental load of a whole extra person and their gear, so you have to be really organised. Shoshannah's first overnight with the baby was at six weeks old, into Rod Donald Hut and out the next day, and a couple of weeks later they went into Hooker Hut.

The hardest part is keeping up with gear as the kids grow. When we recorded, her oldest needed new tramping boots, and the youngest would be next. Gear gets expensive for people who don't stop growing.

### Rain gear first

Rain gear is one of the most important things in New Zealand. Shoshannah has been caught out in the rain and got absolutely soaked because her gear wasn't good enough. A decent rain jacket matters most, rain pants not quite so much.

The trouble is kids' rain jackets aren't rated as highly as adults', which is very frustrating. So once her oldest is big enough, she'll get him an adult's jacket in the smallest size.

### Secondhand and borrowed

She's always scouring Marketplace and Trade Me for secondhand gear: keep looking and be patient. Facebook groups are good too, where people sometimes give gear away, and Wānaka and Queenstown are good spots because they're more touristy (that's where she found tiny toddler ski boots).

She's given a lot of products through social media, and she keeps spare tramping gear so she can take friends out. She has enough for a friend and her kids to come tramping without buying anything.

### The bare basics

When we recorded, Shoshannah was helping a friend get ready for her first tramp, and working out the very basics:

* **A rain jacket**, always first.
* **A sleeping bag.**
* **A cooker and pot.** Shoshannah gave her a spare.
* **A tramping pack.** Her friend found an old one secondhand. It's not new or flash, but it'll get her where she wants to go, and she can save up for a better one later if she wants.
* **Shoes.**
* **A PLB.** Shoshannah has one, or you can hire one instead of buying it.

That's how Shoshannah started tramping with her oldest, when he was two. She hadn't tramped in a few years and didn't have a lot of gear, so she borrowed a pack, a cooker and a pot, used the sleeping bag she'd had since she was 13, bought a cheap foam bed roll and the cheapest two-man tent she could find, and went. It's still one of her most memorable experiences:

> "The gear was not perfect, it was all secondhand or cheap, and it got me to where I wanted to go, and it gave me a great experience."

When we started hiking, we gathered whatever we could too. Beg, borrow and steal, and see if you enjoy it, before you spend a bunch of money in an outdoor shop.

## Planning a tramp with kids {#planning-trips}

They used to only visit new places, but they've been to a lot of them now. Living in one place, they revisit a lot of the same huts, because it's easier than travelling a couple of days to a new hut for a quick, spontaneous trip.

Some trips she plans weeks in advance, waiting for a good weather window or because it's a booked Great Walk. Her work is flexible, so she'd rather wait for good weather. When the weather packed in on a planned Christmas trip, she was salty, canned it and rebooked it a few months later.

Other trips happen the next day: a friend suggests going tomorrow, and they're both at home packing like crazy. Having the basic gear ready makes that easier.

## Huts or tents with kids {#huts-or-tents}

It comes down to personal preference. Shoshannah prefers huts because they're so much more convenient:

* **The mattress is already there**, and it's all set up.
* **Huts often have a fireplace**, and even without one, they're warmer than a tent.
* **Tents with kids are way more stressful**, especially if the weather has packed in. You're setting up and packing down with kids running around, sometimes packing the tent up around them while they complain that it's cold.

Some families prefer a tent so they don't disturb people in the hut. Shoshannah's kids are reasonably well behaved and aren't bothered by other people, so she isn't too worried.

She's recently upgraded to a three-person ultralight tent that weighs 1.2 kg, found on a good sale in Europe. An ultralight tent changes your life when you're carrying kids and so much gear.

I don't like carrying a tent on my back, so if there's a hut at the end, that's my preference. The last time I went tent camping on a tramp was with Shoshannah at Woolshed Creek, though I did sleep in a tent for 49 days on our [sea kayaking](https://abigailhannah.nz/182-finishing-a-two-month-slow-paddle-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/) trip.

## Tramping food, from nachos to steak {#tramping-food}

Shoshannah does a mixture. She's made her own dehydrated food since she was a teenager, and it's usually nachos: mince and veggies on repeat, because it's her favourite meal and the easiest to make. It dries overnight in her dehydrator. If she gets sick of that, she buys dehydrated meals, and a friend has a freeze dryer, which preserves food perfectly so it tastes exactly the same as fresh.

Food is another easy place to budget. Her options, from fancy to cheap:

* **Bought dehydrated meals.** Delightful, easy and convenient, and expensive.
* **Your own dehydrated meals.**
* **The supermarket.** A packet of tuna, a packet of dehydrated peas and a packet of instant noodles, which she did a lot when tramping with her toddler. Or a stick of salami, crackers and cheese.
* **A fresh meal you made at home.** On an overnight trip, weight doesn't matter so much, because whatever you eat, you don't carry out. They'll even carry fresh fruit, and the kids carry apples and fruit puree pouches.

On one of her last trips, she carried in steak, and it was so good, except a friend's dog tried to eat it. And on the last day of the [Paparoa](https://abigailhannah.nz/168-from-first-tramp-to-paparoa-and-all-the-great-walks-with-nicola-harvie/), someone pulled out sausages, the whole hut smelt of them, and they sat there eating dehydrated food.

## Child-free tramping and Lake Christabel {#lake-christabel}

Shoshannah has been on a few overnight trips without the kids in the last year, including one with us to Woolshed Creek. As a solo parent, finding people she trusts with the kids is hard, but a friend who's also a single mum has been one of her lifesavers.

People ask if she misses her kids when she tramps without them. She doesn't, at all. You need time and space, even when you love them so much.

### A solo night in Fiordland

Before that came her first ever solo trip, camping and tramping in [Fiordland](https://abigailhannah.nz/186-packrafting-and-tramping-48-days-through-fiordland-with-penzy-dinsdale/) with her tent in the mountains. It was absolutely epic. She sat in her tent with her audiobook and cups of tea, and came back ready to see the kids.

### Lake Christabel and over the tops

More recently, she went into Lake Christabel with her sister and her sister's dog. Lake Christabel is in the Lewis Pass, near Springs Junction, and there are a few routes in. It's not a well-known or well-maintained track.

They walked in along the lake, 17 kilometres, longer than they'd expected, around slips and over rough ground, so the last stretch was in the dark. Her sister is fast, and Shoshannah is a plodder (except downhill). They reached the hut very, very cold and got the fire going. Her pedometer said 100 flights of stairs.

The next morning, her sister walked out the way they came in, and Shoshannah went up and over the tops, because she wanted a different route. The climb was steep, and being winter, there was snow and ice on the tops, which she hadn't registered before going. She had to climb up and over a really sketchy saddle, and once she was there, turning around wasn't really safe, so she kept going. Her sister was so fast she walked up from the bottom to meet her. They reached the car a couple of hours before dark, and Shoshannah ordered pizza on the drive home, arriving a minute before it did.

The views were incredible, and the dog was great company. But the best part was time for herself: sleeping, walking and stopping when she wanted, without being needed by anybody or convincing anyone to walk.

She loves a challenging track, and this one had route finding, trees down everywhere, lots of scrambling and snow. She loves a little bit of risk and danger. Just a little bit.

It's not a trip she'd take the kids on. Her oldest might have managed it, but you're responsible for your child on that mountainside.

### Route finding with topo maps

Shoshannah relies on the NZ50 topo maps on her phone, with the inbuilt GPS, and the kids always want to check how far is left. On another child-free trip earlier this year, most of the track had no markers, so they used the GPS maps almost the whole way. She thinks route finding is quite fun, like a scavenger hunt.

It's saved her so many times: she can point her phone to find the direction, like a compass.

My life changed when someone first told me about topo maps on your phone. It's worth its weight in gold.

## Managing risk when tramping with kids {#managing-risk}

The keyboard warriors love to tell Shoshannah her kids are going to die in the wilderness. On one trip they got very upset about, along alpine tops, there were adults ahead of them on their hands and knees on the shingle scree, scared, and her son was loving it. He's as capable as some adults, because he's grown up doing this. He does get scared of things, like the dark (same as her), so they've been on night walks from the hut to explore, which is a great way to get brave in the dark.

She plans carefully, especially for a more risky trip, and thinks through all the risks:

* **A PLB, always.** She carries a [personal locator beacon](https://abigailhannah.nz/plb-safety/) on every trip.
* **Trip intentions** with a reliable, trusted contact: which hut, which day, and when they plan to be out.
* **Satellite texting** on her phone, for when there's no cell service. It's not super reliable, and she doesn't recommend relying on it over a PLB. She carries both, and on some recent trips with tricky weather, texting home for weather updates was really reassuring.
* **An emergency shelter**, like a tarpaulin and an emergency blanket, so if the weather cracks in or someone gets hurt, you can shelter and stay cosy while you wait for help.
* **Don't rush.** Make sure the kids have eaten, and if everyone's tired, stop for a break.
* **Know your abilities, and theirs.** She won't pick a trip she doesn't think they're capable of. On the trip the trolls didn't love, she'd researched it so carefully that she knew it would be steep and rocky with shingle, and that they could handle it.

Someone on the other side of the world watching a video has none of that context. Doing the [Dusky Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/049-dusky-track-fiordland/) as your first hike with kids would be irresponsible, and Shoshannah has built up to what she does.

She still makes mistakes, like forgetting the first aid kit, but she doesn't want anyone put off by the risks. Start small. The question she gets most is where to start, and she suggests something under 5 kilometres, while watching the elevation: 5 kilometres with 1,000 metres of climbing took them almost eight hours, and 5 kilometres with low elevation took two to three. You won't gain experience if you don't start.

### Turning around on the Paparoa Track

Shoshannah has never had to press her PLB. The trip that stands out is her first attempt at the Paparoa Track. She was with her sister, her baby was six months old and her oldest was five.

They walked up to the first hut in the rain, and the next morning set off for the next hut. It wasn't too far, but with the wind, rain and cold, they were soaked within a kilometre. Shoshannah hates turning around, even though she tells people it's okay. They sat down for a minute, and her sister said no, mate, we've got to turn around, it's not safe.

Their car had already been relocated to the other end. Back at the first hut, warm and dry, they found cell service, but the car relocation company couldn't help and the shuttle wouldn't take kids. So the next morning her sister caught the shuttle to bring the car round.

Shoshannah walked down from Ces Clark Hut with the kids in the most torrential rain she's ever seen. They were drenched within minutes. A DOC ranger was coming up to close the track and turn everyone else around, and stopped to check they were okay. The track had turned into a river, and some of the swollen creeks were murky, fast water that made her nervous. Her five-year-old was having a lot of fun with the marshmallows she was feeding him, though she had to carry him at times.

Halfway down, she strung her emergency tarp between a couple of trees and fully changed the kids into warm, dry clothes. They all wear merino, and this is why it matters: her baby, in the carrier against her chest, was soaking wet but completely warm. They reached the bottom just as her sister turned up. Overnight, it snowed.

Booking makes those calls harder. On a Great Walk you book far in advance, plus car relocation, so you can't plan on the forecast, and turning around costs a lot. It's why she hasn't done many trips like the [Kepler](https://abigailhannah.nz/003-hiking-the-kepler-track-in-fiordland-with-dua/) and the [Milford](https://abigailhannah.nz/034-milford-track-nz-popular-walk/) with the kids.

As adults, they would have kept going along the exposed tops to the next hut and waited out the weather, as other adults did. With kids, you have to make the call sooner. On their second Paparoa trip, it rained, but not as torrentially.

> "there's absolutely no shame in turning around, because staying alive and not being on the news is far more important."

## Keeping kids happy on the track {#kids-on-track}

A listener question from [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/): how does she wrangle two kids on the trail, and keep them entertained on long walks?

The bush is full of entertainment, and her kids love it. Her youngest asks to go hiking (she gets carried most of the time, so why wouldn't you?). Her tips:

* **Pest control traps.** They check every single one.
* **Orange markers.** They love finding and tapping each one.
* **The map and the pedometer.** Checking how far they've gone, how far is left, and how many steps they've walked.
* **The right friends.** They have an amazing group of friends to tramp with, and the kids love each other. On those trips, she barely sees her oldest because he's off with his friends.
* **Mud and ice.** On a recent trip, the kids played for so long cracking the ice and getting covered in mud.
* **Lollies.** So many lollies.
* **Songs.** Thanks to Shoshannah, her friend's kids now sing Yellow Submarine on repeat.

The hard part is a bigger walk when they're tired and cold. Getting a toddler to put clothes on is one of her biggest nightmares, and she once had to pin her son down to get warm layers on him as it started snowing. Meltdowns teach resilience. You keep a bit of a no-nonsense attitude: yep, this sucks, yep, you're cold, but we'll be at the hut soon, with hot chocolate and the fire going.

### Her best advice for parents

The biggest fear is that kids will refuse to walk. Parents tell her their child won't even walk around the block, and Shoshannah won't either, because that's boring. Kids are just like her: give them a challenging, interesting track, good views and a cosy hut. Skip five kilometres of river walking or four-wheel drive track, because kids get so bored on those. Turn it into a fun adventure. You need an insane amount of patience and perseverance, but the reward is priceless:

> "I can stay at home and it's hard, or I'll go tramping with them and it's hard, but it's also much more rewarding."
