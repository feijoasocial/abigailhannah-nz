Viktoria and I have been talking about doing this episode for a few years. A few years! So I'm so glad we finally locked it in.

She's a Chicago-born mum of two who now lives in Queenstown. She visited Aotearoa back in 2015 and felt at home straight away, and more than 10 years on she's built a whole life here across two cities, with road trips, trails with her two kids and solo adventures, which she shares online.

So if you're dreaming about making the move to Aotearoa yourself, this one's for you. We talk about how a message to a workmate turned India into her first passport stamp, the long road to staying here, six years of Wellington trails, and why she loves living somewhere touristy (yes, really). Plus a lupin road trip to Aoraki, a wild and windy Doubtful Sound cruise with the kids, the hard parts of living far from family, and a Queenstown track I'd never heard of. It's going on my list.

## Growing up outside Chicago {#viktoria-story}

Viktoria grew up in the Chicago suburbs: very flat, no mountains, a few hills. They weren't a big travel family, they were very into sports, and trips were mostly local.

Her favourite memories are from a family property in Wisconsin called Journey's End: 60 acres of forest, fields, a pond and trails. All the cousins would wake up and spend all day outside. They built a tree fort, played capture the flag across the land, went fishing, caught turtles and swam in the pond, and in winter they ice skated and rode snowmobiles. Little wild things. That's probably where her love of nature came from.

She didn't get her first passport until she was 23, which she says is pretty normal for Americans.

## India, the Himalayas and the travel bug {#travel-bug}

Viktoria always dreamed of travelling. She's always been a big reader, and she loved the French countryside in Beauty and the Beast as a little girl. But plans to work her way across Europe or study abroad in London kept falling through. So she went to a big university, moved into the city and got the corporate job, and quickly realised a windowless cubicle was quite soul-destroying.

Then a girl she worked with mentioned that she and her sister were going to India on a big spiritual trip. Viktoria didn't know her that well, but the next day she messaged and asked if she could come along. She'd never have expected India to be her first passport stamp, but she thinks it was meant to be.

It was a big culture shock, and she loved throwing herself into the deep end. She prepped as much as she could, went veggie and got tips from friends from India. Landing in New Delhi at night, she woke up to cows next to the hotel, rickshaws, people walking, bougie cars, and the madness of all of it.

On that trip she did a really intense yoga teacher training, then a 10-day silent Buddhist meditation course, then her first proper tramping, in the Himalayas. India is easy to travel on the trains, and everywhere they went, someone raved about the next place, so they stayed until their visas ran out. Then came half a year working in Melbourne, and Southeast Asia.

That trip opened her eyes to the world and changed her so much. She'd got the bug, and she didn't want to get stuck back in a corporate job. Back in Chicago, she worked multiple jobs and saved, with little trips to Europe and Guatemala, then spent five months backpacking South America, slow as possible and hiking as much as possible, finally getting good at her Spanish.

## Moving to New Zealand on a work visa {#moving-to-nz}

Knowing she'd have to go home after South America, Viktoria wanted a backup plan. People had raved about New Zealand when she was in Australia, and the work visa was an opportunity. So she saved a bit more and flew into Auckland, over 10 years ago now.

It felt like home quickly. She did a little road trip from Auckland down to Wellington. She'd originally wanted to go straight to Queenstown for a ski season, but a bunch of friends were heading to Wellington (pretty classic for people coming from Melbourne), and her second job could sponsor her, back when that was easier. Flying back from a friend's wedding in the States, before she'd even been here a year, she felt like she was going home.

When [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/) and I flew back from a big overseas trip two years ago, I had the same feeling: oh, we're coming home. That's when we realised New Zealand will always be home base.

Staying wasn't easy. It was years and years of work visas with no pathway to residency, and so many of her friends had to leave because of visas. She was here through COVID when the 2021 residency visa was announced. A customer told her the day before that something big was coming, and sure enough, it was. She says it saved a lot of people who stuck with New Zealand through COVID.

### Her advice on visas

Visa rules change all the time, so she always tells people to do their own research. When she came, Americans got a one-year work visa, and people from some other countries can get longer. If you have a niche skill, there might be an easier path, and the Green List shows roles New Zealand needs, a lot of them in medical, engineering and IT. Some have no pathway to residency, and some go straight to residency.

> "if you come here and you really want to make this home, like, there is a way."

You might have to fight for it, hire an immigration lawyer, or do a job you don't like for years. She knows people who studied a role New Zealand needs, adding to the society. Her message: don't give up.

## Six years of Wellington trails {#wellington}

Viktoria lived in Wellington for six years. Of the friends she started out with, almost everyone else left, many because there wasn't enough money. Her view is that you can't compare New Zealand and Australia too closely: you'll make more money in Australia, and it's a different lifestyle.

She thinks [Wellington](https://abigailhannah.nz/021-wellington-gems-local-travel-tilleigh/) is the coolest little capital: a compact city on the waterfront, wrapped in hills of houses and little bays, with so many inlets and hidden valleys that it took her years to know which way was which. You can walk from one end of the city to the other in 25 minutes.

As soon as she arrived, she was hiking every trail she could get to on foot, by bus or by train. During the COVID lockdowns, when walks were allowed, she took up [trail running](https://abigailhannah.nz/187-from-beginner-trail-running-to-setting-course-records-with-david-zebedee/).

The coolest thing about Wellington, for her, is the town belt of trails that wraps almost around the whole city. Start at Mount Victoria, the main viewpoint over the harbour, and follow the trails through the woods and little neighbourhoods, past the zoo fences (a weird experience), to her favourite spot, Mount Albert. It's the first place on that trail where you can see the South Island, and she'd look at the snow on the Kaikōura Ranges and dream about living there one day.

She loved the culture too: street festivals, two huge free music festivals a year, and Te Papa, which she misses so much.

## Moving to Queenstown {#queenstown}

Any chance she had, Viktoria flew to the South Island for road trips (you can fly around cheaply if you get the deals), and she fell in love with Queenstown quite quickly. When she went on maternity leave with her second, she packed up the Wellington house and found a beautiful little rental home she's still in, over four years later.

She took the ferry, waving Wellington goodbye on the way out of the harbour, and moved in during spring, with the wisteria on the house in full bloom, snow on the mountains and the cherry blossoms next door going off. It felt like heaven: mountains all around, and trails everywhere.

People tell her they could never live in Queenstown because it's so touristy. She loves it. She lives somewhere people dream of coming to, she watches visitors in awe taking pictures of everything, and she gets to call it home. Even driving to the supermarket, she's surrounded by mountains, passing cows, alpacas and horses. It's busy with visitors, but the town of people who live there full time is quite small, so you see people you know everywhere.

Adventure is still a core part of life. She always needs an idea of a trip to look forward to. Her son is too big to carry on her back now, so the long trails are on pause unless she's out on her own, but she and the kids thrive on the road. They've spent hours in the car for years, happily staring out the window, and they spend a lot of time at playgrounds these days.

## A lupin road trip to Aoraki and Lake Ōhau {#mackenzie-road-trip}

One of her favourite recent road trips was kid-free, which is rare, with a best friend visiting from Austria with her little daughter. It was the end of spring, one of the most popular times to visit, thanks to the lupins (that beautiful invasive weed).

They went to [Aoraki Mount Cook](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/) and up Sealy Tarns, which Viktoria calls the best Stairmaster in the country. One morning they went out for sunrise and frolicked in the lupins next to the highway and the river, the most beautiful morning. The rest of the quick trip took in Twizel and Tekapo.

Her favourite part was a dreamy, completely isolated cabin at Lake Ōhau, somewhere she'd never been, watching the sunrise and sunset. Places like that make her feel so calm and happy. New Zealand is a tiny nation with a big, vast landscape, and you can feel like the only person for miles.

> "New Zealand really ruins it like for the rest of the world. … give us 5 minutes in a car, we'll be on a trail and yeah, be the only person in paradise."

You can feel like that even near Auckland. When we take the [kayaks](https://abigailhannah.nz/182-finishing-a-two-month-slow-paddle-sea-kayak-expedition-with-isaac-and-abigail/) out, there's often no one else on the little beach we pull up on, and we recently did a Tuesday hike in the Hūnuas and saw one other person.

## Fiordland in the rain and Doubtful Sound {#doubtful-sound}

Another favourite was a school holiday road trip with the kids. The weather was bad every which way, and when the weather's bad in the South Island, there's always one place to go: [Fiordland](https://abigailhannah.nz/186-packrafting-and-tramping-48-days-through-fiordland-with-penzy-dinsdale/).

Viktoria loves prepping for a road trip, packing the kids' little backpacks for the car and the food for the next few days. Her rule is to stay cheap and spend more on the adventure, because you're barely in your accommodation anyway. Cheap holiday parks always have a playground, so the kids make friends while she cooks.

In Te Anau, the plan was a water taxi across Lake Manapōuri for a hike on the other side. On the drive there that morning, she found missed calls: the taxi was cancelled because of the wind. They played in the rain and walked a trail instead, and a few hours later it was a completely different day.

### Doubtful Sound with the kids

Doubtful Sound had always been on her list, so the next morning they drove back to Manapōuri for a cruise. A boat takes you across the lake, then a bus takes you over a road she learned is the most expensive in New Zealand to build, dropping into the valley past waterfall upon waterfall, with everyone's heads pressed to the glass. Then you board another boat and cruise Doubtful Sound out towards the ocean.

They hit the waves, the sea spray soaked them, the wind howled, and the kids were laughing and holding on to the railings, having the best time.

Her favourite moment was when the crew asked everyone to put their phones away, stop talking and be still. She wanted to cry, it felt so powerful.

Travelling taught her to connect with nature, and with how other cultures do. In the Andes in Peru, you give gifts to Pachamama, Mother Earth, before a hike, and in the Himalayas there are prayer flags and incense and prayers to the mountain. She thinks New Zealand does it better than a lot of the Western world, and she loves learning about the Tiaki Promise on Air New Zealand flights.

## Building community far from family {#building-community}

Being away from family is hard, especially when you've grown up relying on them. Viktoria is very well connected with home, and she doesn't know if she could do it without the technology we have now.

New Zealand is far, and that's why it's so special: it's harder to get to, so our favourite places aren't overrun. But in 10 years, very few family and friends have visited. She puts that down to American work culture, where taking a month off just isn't normal, compared with starting a full-time job here with a month of paid leave.

It's also a transient place, with backpackers and people on working holiday visas. You build friendships, then boom, they're gone, and she could count on one hand the people left from her Wellington community. In Queenstown, it's rare to meet a family with two Kiwi parents: someone's from Canada, Oz or Brazil. The upside is that everyone is in the same boat, so having people you can rely on is key.

She sees a lot of posts in Queenstown from people struggling to make friends, and she thinks COVID made people turn inward. Her advice is to find your people through what you love. Someone she followed online started a book club, Viktoria joined, and now she has a whole new group of girlfriends. Look for group hikes and photo tours, and put yourself out there:

> "you have to get uncomfortable. And when you do get uncomfortable, … The outcomes are incredible."

## Moke Lake and the Moonlight Track {#moonlight-track}

A listener asked for Queenstown mini adventures and local hidden gems, and Viktoria will never stop talking about the Moonlight Track.

It starts from Moke Lake, which she'd send everyone to anyway. It's on a beautiful road towards Glenorchy, then a gravel, four-wheel-drive kind of track past cows, sheep and wild goats to a little paradise lake. It's close to the city, and it's still cut off from cell service, which she loves. There's a huge horse paddock, a great loop track around the lake, and a slightly hidden viewpoint: head past the horses, take a sharp right at their fence, and follow the goat track up.

The Moonlight Track isn't on the DOC website and probably isn't well marked (she's taken a wrong turn on it). You can start at Moke Lake or Arthur's Point, and it takes you through the big field and back into a valley where you won't see anyone. In winter, expect a lot of cows, and a lot of cow pie. There are so many little viewpoints, and a few ways to go: up the back of Ben Lomond and down into town, or across to Arthur's Point.

Last time we were in Queenstown, we deliberately didn't stay at Moke Lake, because I needed signal to work. So you won't find people out there working, only people enjoying the scenery.

## What's next, from Greenstone Caples to overnights {#whats-next}

Viktoria had plans this summer that didn't happen, because it wasn't the greatest summer. Her oldest could hike a lot further now, but her youngest is at the stage where he's too big to carry and won't walk that far.

Her next three:

* **The Greenstone Caples.** A loop with a few huts, less popular than its next-door neighbour, the [Routeburn](https://abigailhannah.nz/009-solo-travel-routeburn-track-emily/).
* **Back to the North Island.** She's been craving a [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/) trip, and from Wellington she'd spend every few days off up at [Tongariro](https://abigailhannah.nz/138-an-obsession-with-the-mountains-of-tongariro-national-park-with-hazel-phillips/).
* **Nelson and** [**Abel Tasman**](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/). It's been so long.

Her bigger goal is to start [tramping with the kids](https://abigailhannah.nz/190-family-tramping-on-a-budget-and-child-free-trips-with-shoshannah-shand/) and doing overnights again, which she used to do when it was just her daughter and she could carry her.

The Greenstone Caples is on my list too, and now the Moonlight Track is as well. There will always be a new trail in New Zealand I haven't heard of.
