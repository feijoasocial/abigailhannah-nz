I first heard about the [St James Walkway](https://abigailhannah.nz/113-tramping-the-st-james-walkway-and-an-unexpected-encounter-with-olivia-jacinda-and-sarah-blair/) on the [Old Ghost Road](https://abigailhannah.nz/solo-old-ghost-road/). One of the rangers there mentioned it (I can't remember if she was telling me or I was eavesdropping), and it sounded so so interesting, and really beginner friendly too. So when April said it was one of her favourite multi-day hikes in Aotearoa, I was in.

April is originally from the UK, and when we recorded she'd spent the last five and a half years loving life in New Zealand. She's a designer of outdoor technical and sport performance gear, and she moved here for a job at Kathmandu without ever having visited. Which is wild to me.

And she's honest about not seeing herself as a runner, which is exactly why I wanted to hear how she started trail running.

So this post covers her move, her Christchurch favourites, the St James Walkway hut by hut, the big day on Mount Somers that changed how she sees herself, what goes into designing outdoor gear, and her advice for anyone who thinks big adventures are for other people.

## Moving to New Zealand without visiting first {#moving-to-nz}

April lives in Sumner, right on the beach in Christchurch, and she counts herself lucky to call it home.

Her job brought her here. She was living in Germany, working at adidas, when Kathmandu approached her about joining its design team. She was having an awesome time in Germany but was ready for new challenges. She had never visited New Zealand, she'd only heard amazing things about it, and she moved in the middle of COVID, which she doesn't recommend, but they made it work.

How do you move to a country you've never seen? For April, it comes down to being ready for the unknown. She plays with a decision in her mind, one step at a time, until it starts to feel normal. Then it's time to do it.

I'm a pros and cons list person, so I was fascinated by this.

April's way of thinking is less organised than a list, and more about knowing she can always go back:

> "That's something that I don't see as a failure necessarily. I think trying is the exciting part and the part that helps you grow."

It's how she moved to Germany at 22, on a one-way ticket for a job. It was always in her mind that she could go home if things completely hit the fan. Sometimes the timing is wrong and things fall through, and that's okay. Other times the trying is the hardest part, and once you've done it, it feels great.

I'm a big believer in giving things a shot too. Nothing's locked in forever.

## Finding her way back to the outdoors {#love-of-outdoors}

Looking back, April says her family was relatively outdoorsy. Holidays were mostly on the south coast of the UK, trailer tent camping on big campsites, where she was let loose to explore and run riot.

Then came the grumpy teenage years, when she didn't want to be dragged up the coastal walks. She came full circle in her later teens and at university, and her design career did the rest. It led her into performance sportswear and technical outdoor wear, which she finds so fascinating, and it gave her a deeper view and love of the outdoors, because it's intertwined with what she does.

As a kid, it was mostly about time in the sea: boogie boarding, learning to surf and cycling everywhere.

## Christchurch and Banks Peninsula favourites {#banks-peninsula}

When April first arrived, the recommendations came thick and fast, from Kiwis and from friends in Europe and the UK who had travelled here. It felt like a tick list she had to cover fast, and she found it a little overwhelming. So she and her partner took a step back. They lived here, with an undetermined amount of time, so they spent the first year exploring closer to home in [Canterbury](https://abigailhannah.nz/141-best-christchurch-and-canterbury-adventures-with-kids-with-jasmine-trafford/).

Her picks from Banks Peninsula:

*   **Mount Herbert.** You can hike it straight from Diamond Harbour, which sits at sea level, and you climb 900 metres. It's a bit of a grueller, but if you need motivation, turn around for the 360-degree view. It doesn't matter if you turn back early, because you get views however high you climb.
*   **Diamond Harbour.** Finish a sweaty summer hike with a swim. Combining hiking and wild swimming is one of April's favourite ways to spend a day.
*   **Okains Bay.** One of many beautiful bays on the peninsula.
*   **Magnet Bay.** Worth a look for surfers, or for anyone who wants to watch people doing incredible things on surfboards.

I always struggle with Christchurch, because I'm usually passing through on the way to Mount Cook or Queenstown, so I'm adding these to my list. The only short walk I've done there is Godley Head.

## A first South Island road trip {#first-road-trip}

April's shipping container took about three months longer than normal to arrive, because of COVID container shortages, so they were living with camping furniture. When it finally came, they'd booked three days off work to unpack. They took one look at it, decided it was too much, got in the car and went on a road trip instead.

It was a classic first loop:

*   **Fairlie**, for a pie. April won't claim it's the best pie in the pie wars, but it's really good.
*   **Tekapo and Lake Pūkaki**, then a drive up to [Mount Cook](https://abigailhannah.nz/aoraki-mount-cook-national-park/).
*   **Omarama**, for the hot tubs. It wasn't quite summer, so it was cold enough to enjoy them, and the [night sky](https://abigailhannah.nz/027-astrophotography-larryn-rae/) in the dark sky reserve was incredible.

Then they looped back up the east coast. Short but sweet, and their first glimpse of so much of the landscape.

I love that the container got unpacked in its own time.

## Planning the St James Walkway {#st-james-planning}

The St James Walkway is in the Lewis Pass Scenic Reserve, roughly halfway across the South Island. If you're driving from Christchurch up towards Nelson, you pass through Lewis Pass.

DOC says it's 66 kilometres, but April found that when you track it, it always ends up a bit longer.

April rates it as beautiful as some of the Great Walks she's done. It's a lot quieter (it wasn't a Great Walk, at least when they walked it), and it crosses a section of [Te Araroa](https://abigailhannah.nz/202-looking-after-te-araroa-hut-etiquette-and-trail-fees-with-matt-claridge/), so you meet lots of fascinating people on much longer thru-hikes.

### Huts and bookings

There are so many huts that you can split the walk up however you like. When they walked it, the hut that crosses over with the Te Araroa walkers was the one to be conscious of, because it gets much more crowded. The other huts were low-key, on standard hut tickets.

### Getting to the start

The Lewis Pass trailhead car park isn't particularly secure. People say cars get vandalised there, and the DOC website says not to leave cars there for long.

The Boyle River Education Centre offers a car drop-off. You drive one of their team to the trailhead in your car, they drive it back to their secure parking at the end of the walk, and there's a key drop box. The catch is that you book a slot within their office hours, so you can't start really early.

### Horses and cows

People can walk the St James on horseback, so in places the trail splits into a horse trail and a human trail. The horses skip the boardwalks and go along the riverbeds instead.

One day in the forest, April saw hoof marks in the dried mud and assumed they were from horses. Then they found three big cows on the single track, and had to gently encourage them to move so they could get past without freaking them out.

Cows on a tramping track are new to me. You don't usually see mammals out there, full stop.

## The St James Walkway, day by day {#st-james-days}

April and her partner packed food for four days. They got a bit carried away and finished in two and a half.

### Day one: Lewis Pass to Christopher Hut

They started from the Lewis Pass end, after the car drop-off. They kept reaching a hut, deciding they could go a bit further and walking on. They were going at a good pace and having a great time, so they passed Cannibal Gorge Hut and Ada Pass Hut and stayed the night at Christopher Hut.

The St James doesn't have a lot of elevation, so you can keep up a nice pace. You do pass through a couple of avalanche paths, which are all signposted, so note where they are at different times of year and don't stop in them.

Christopher Hut has a horse paddock around it and looks out over a wide, open valley, with ridgelines on either side and a river below. It had been grey and raining, and then the clouds cleared. April loves wild swimming and cold water dips, and so does her partner, so they left their packs in the hut and ran down with dry bags, microfibre towels and warm layers. Warm layers for afterwards are one of the most important things for any cold dip. It was a tiny stream, and they had a quick dip with geese flying overhead before the wind changed and the rain came back, so they threw everything on and ran back to the hut to get warm.

There was no one else at the hut that whole night, so they had the place to themselves.

I'm still not great at cold water. I'll put my feet in at the end of a long day, but then they go numb, and there's no way the rest of me is getting in.

### Day two: Christopher Hut to Boyle Flat Hut

From Christopher Hut, the track follows the river as it bends through the valley, up and down along the edge of the bank. Then it climbs to a big open plain between the mountains where Anne Hut sits. April says it almost looks like a western movie. With nothing to block the wind, the hut stands alone in the middle of the landscape, and it was windy enough when they were there.

Christopher Hut to Anne Hut is 13 kilometres. They had the whole afternoon ahead of them, and the signposts said it was only another 17 kilometres to Boyle Flat Hut, the last hut. So they kept going.

It didn't go to plan. When April's tracker already showed 30 kilometres, they were still about an hour from the hut. She got tired and grumpy, and it was less fun than they'd expected. Boyle Flat Hut is tucked away in the trees, so you don't see it until you're right on top of it, and there's nothing to walk towards.

That's when they relied on each other. If one of them is flagging, the other is the hype crew: we're close, look how far we've come.

I know that feeling too well. On my solo Old Ghost Road walk, my map said I'd reached the end of the day, and the trail kept going. It was the longest stretch of the five days, I was on my own and struggling, and I sat down at that signpost and cried.

### Boyle Flat Hut

Boyle Flat Hut was worth the push. It feels retro, nostalgic and homey, with a big wood burner in the centre and windows wrapping around the front, with window seats. There was a family there with their kids, who'd walked one night in from the Boyle River end, and a few others who'd walked the whole St James, so it had a lovely atmosphere.

It was a little crowded, so they pitched their tent outside. That's their preference anyway, even when there are bunks free, because they love having their own little sanctuary.

### Day three: out to Boyle River

April likes to hike at a decent pace so she doesn't have to get up early. On the last morning they had a chill breakfast and a coffee with a view from those window seats.

Then it was only 14 kilometres out, which feels short after the day before. The last section goes back through forest, which sums up the walk's variety: forest on day one, river banks and small climbs with views, wide flats, then forest again. There are also a few smaller, unserviced shelters along the way if you need a stop.

They walked out happy to have most of the food weight gone, and a little amused that they'd carried four days of food. April would still rather come over prepared.

I love that some people sleep in and then walk fast. On a trip to [Angelus Hut](https://abigailhannah.nz/039-angelus-hut-dylan-arthur/), I met two lovely older women who had booked two nights there, so they didn't have to go anywhere the next day. Genius. There are no rules, and I hate waking up early, so I might take a note from April's book.

## Starting trail running on Mount Somers {#trail-running}

April worked with so many incredible endurance athletes at adidas, and she never considered herself a runner. She grew up sporty and active, but the picture she had of a runner wasn't her, and [trail running](https://abigailhannah.nz/187-from-beginner-trail-running-to-setting-course-records-with-david-zebedee/) always seemed like something to admire in other people.

From home, there's a 19-kilometre circuit that heads up into the hills at the back of Sumner and over to Godley Head, covering that whole part of the peninsula. They hike it often, and she'd see people running sections of it and think it would be so cool to try one day.

So she built up slowly. A 5k on the road was her comfortable distance, and a few years ago she challenged herself to get comfortable running 10k on the flat. She did it, and decided to try a bit of running in the hills.

### The big day on Mount Somers

Then came a big day out on [Mount Somers](https://abigailhannah.nz/hiking-mount-somers/), a hike April recommends, because you can connect huts, hike the summit, or do a day or a multi-day trip. Her partner had done the route years before, when he was very fit, and remembered it as a lot easier than it is.

They went up to the summit, scrambling over boulders, then down a scree slope on the north face and through to Pinnacles Hut, then back to the car park. It's hard on your knees. Even the good trails on the other side are big boulders, so every step feels like a big step.

When her mood dropped, they stopped for a river dip to get her motivation back. Towards the end she was not happy. Her partner kept saying they were almost there, and then they'd reach another river crossing and realise they were on the wrong side of the river again.

It left her wanting more:

> "It left me with this feeling of you're capable of so much more than you give yourself credit for. And even though I was so slow… it was so good and… by the end just feeling that sense of achievement…"

The trails at home suddenly looked small. It was her moment of clarity about why she was holding herself back. It doesn't matter how fast or slow she is, or how sweaty or silly she looks when she runs. If she wants to do it, she can, and she can have her own version of it, at her own level.

She called it a hike, and it wasn't meant to be a big thing. But it showed her that with more of a run mentality, she could do that too.

I love that she used both approaches: baby steps from 5k to 10k, and then jumping in the deep end with a day that pushed her limits. My first solo hike was also the longest hike I'd ever done, so I'm all for the deep end if you want it.

## Designing outdoor gear that lasts {#designing-gear}

April is head of design at Kathmandu, so I brought listener questions.

### Customer feedback

On the day we recorded, April had spent a heap of time reading website reviews. Her team reads every customer review, especially when they're working on a new version of something they've made before. They field test with users too. It all goes into the melting pot of information they use to work out what the next generation of product looks like.

I met April earlier this year at the launch of the XT series, and as a guest there, I felt very listened to.

### Durability and repair

April calls the balance between durability and new product lines a delicate one. First and foremost, her team doesn't want anyone buying new gear because something failed too early. Gear should stand the test of time, and ideally get passed down. They love seeing old fleeces and backpacks handed down from parents and still in use, and they try to get hold of heritage pieces for their archive.

They also design with repair in mind. Where would a product fail, and how hard would that be to fix? Some fixes are possible on the trail, but repair usually needs skills, so stores can link you up with a repair service.

### What's in her kit

April keeps a fair few prototypes and past products as design references, but the gear she uses on the trail comes down to comfort. She's not a gram saver. She carries an inflatable pillow and a spare pair of shorts.

Her XT rain pants and three-layer rain jacket have made her kit. The fabric is durable, packs smaller and moves with you, without the crisp packet crunch that can drive you nuts on a long tramp. Her jacket is bright orange, because it makes her happy. The XT down jacket is now her favourite too.

I started the Old Ghost Road in $10 rain pants and took them off, because I'd rather get wet than listen to that all day.

## April's advice for starting out {#starting-out}

For anyone who thinks big adventures are for other people, April's advice is to give it a go, gently:

> "I feel like there's no harm in giving it a go and… start gently and don't be too hard on yourself."

Chop things into tiny chunks, and make them bigger as you get comfortable. For running, that was a longer distance on the flat. For hiking, it might be one night in a hut. Pick a cute one, like Boyle Flat Hut, and take people who will champion you. Then maybe dive in the deep end, like she did. Go at your own pace, as long as you can still smile, even if sometimes the smile comes afterwards.

April finds something special in carrying yourself over a distance with your own body, whether that's running, cycling, hiking or paddling.

Next up, she has a place in the Coast to Coast Mountain Run in February, with lots and lots of river crossings to get her head around. And she's in admiration of everyone who has walked Te Araroa, so maybe one day she'll carve out the time for it, or at least a section.
