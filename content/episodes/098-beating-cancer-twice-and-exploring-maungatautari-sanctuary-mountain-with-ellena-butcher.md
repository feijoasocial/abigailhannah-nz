I met Ellena on a community hike on Auckland's west coast. We were strangers at the start of the day, and by the end she'd told me about her past hikes, her big plans and her cancer journey.

Ellena is 58 and lives in the Waikato. She's done four marathons and a lot of section walking on Te Araroa, and she's beaten ovarian cancer twice. These days she calls what she's doing "attacking life", and I love that so much.

So this post covers how she went from hating cross country to marathons, her first very long day on Te Araroa, her cancer journey at the level she shared it, the walks on Maungatautari Sanctuary Mountain (her mountain), the thermos tip I'll never forget (it's so so simple), and what's next, from the Camino to Japan.

## From hating cross country to four marathons {#ellena-story}

Ellena is proud to say she didn't have an adventurous childhood. At primary school she thought cross country was a swear word. Her family played sport and weren't inactive, and holidays were the beach type of thing.

At 16 she discovered running, and she's done four marathons since. She liked bushwalking at school too, and as the kids got older and time freed up, she got really into it.

I hated cross country at school too. At mine, only the first six people to qualify ran on the day, until a new PE teacher decided everyone had to. Let the six best have their run, I say.

## Section walking Te Araroa {#section-walking-te-araroa}

Ellena, her sister and her sister's husband have done a lot of [section walking](https://abigailhannah.nz/173-section-walking-te-araroa-over-multiple-seasons-and-battling-cancer-with-te-aroha-hohaia/) on Te Araroa. They haven't done as much since COVID, because of Ellena's cancer.

Their first day was Anzac Day at Pirongia. They went to the Anzac parade and the traditional toast and tea first, and got dropped at the start nearly at nine o'clock, because that's how naive they were. They went right up and over, it was a huge job, and they didn't get out until dark. Their lift was a bit frantic, because they weren't really prepared for anything to go wrong. Luckily, nothing did.

You can stumble across Te Araroa all over the country. The morning we recorded, I'd walked a bit of it with a friend in the Bombays.

## Beating ovarian cancer twice {#cancer-journey}

Ellena's first diagnosis was ovarian cancer, when her kids were preschoolers. She had a lot of surgery, and she jokes that she's half the woman she used to be. The really good news was that her lymph nodes were clear, so she needed no other treatment. After that came regular testing, then a yearly blood test and mammogram.

She had 19 years free of what she calls "that beast". Then in 2021 it came back.

One of her goals that year was pole dancing, for fitness, and she thinks it may have saved her life: she wasn't strong enough to pull herself up the pole and thought she'd pulled something near her belly button. When things got worse, she went to the doctor, and tests found a mass. The surgery to remove it turned into major abdominal surgery, then came chemo, then more surgery on 19 April 2022.

Seven months later, in November, she walked the Queenstown half marathon. As she puts it, it's not pretty, but she gets it done.

### What Ellena wants people to know

Fitness, first. She's had eight abdominal surgeries, and she's 100 percent confident she got through the surgeries and chemo as well as she did because she'd always stayed fit. Her oncologist told her that if there was one thing she could do, it was to keep moving, because it helps with recovery and survival.

So if she could do nothing else all day, she would get up and walk, some days still 13 kilometres, in the rain if need be. It gave her the strength to heal well, and it was the one thing she could control when she had control of nothing else.

Second: if you think something's wrong, go and get it checked. If she hadn't done that twice, she wouldn't be here. And she has never googled her cancer, because she doesn't want the statistics.

Third: cancer isn't a death penalty. She knows some listeners may be going through a really terrible cancer journey, or have lost someone, and she's sorry for that. She also wants people to remember:

> "if there's a cancer diagnosis, it doesn't always mean it's the end."

That's why every year, if she's around, she does the Relay for Life survivors lap. People need to see survivors.

## Walking Maungatautari Sanctuary Mountain {#maungatautari}

Maungatautari is Ellena's mountain. She grew up looking at it from the age of six (her first mountain was Pirongia, because she grew up on a farm out there), and it's a 10-minute drive from her, just out of Cambridge, whenever she wants a good walk.

It's known as Sanctuary Mountain. Ellena says it was the first pest-proof fence built around the base of a mountain, and with the pests eradicated, there's takahē and kiwi, a whole lot of programmes going on, and amazing birdlife you can hear because there are no predators.

When I looked it up before we recorded, it was described as the largest pest-proof fenced forest in the world, and I'd seen in the news that 50 kiwi were moved from there to Wellington.

### The northern and southern enclosures

* **The southern enclosure** has the visitor centre, tours to see what they're up to, and a big viewing platform. Ellena has only been up the platform once, because she lives nearer the other side.
* **The northern enclosure** means a walk up through some fenced farmland to the small enclosure, where you can experience the bush inside the pest-proof fence. It's really neat to take your family up there, and Ellena took her kids when they were little.

From the northern side you can also head up to the top or right over the mountain, either on a well-maintained quad bike track or up through the bush, which gets quite muddy after rain. It's a big climb, and it isn't hard on the quad track because the terrain isn't rough. At Maungatautari Peak, the tall trees and lack of a trig mean the view is only a little glimpse.

The up and over to the other side is five to six hours, and Ellena points out that's very individual, because everyone walks at a different pace.

### Takahē and kiwi

Neither of us has seen a takahē in the wild. Ellena has seen a kiwi, though: years ago, on a visit to the small enclosure, one of the volunteers had a baby kiwi and they all got to have a hold, something she doubts would happen now.

## Slow walking and a thermos of tea {#slow-walking}

Ellena thinks there's a lot to be said for walking slowly. You're going for the destination, and there's a whole lot between A and B. Slow down and you hear the birds and see things, like the cobwebs. So she thinks it's quite good that I'm a slow walker.

I'm generally on the slower end of every DOC sign. If it says four hours, I'm like, cool, it'll be five. I love stopping to look at a mushroom or a spider while the person at the front charges ahead.

Ellena's top tip for anyone who goes tramping is to take a thermos. They always carry a small one each, and hers has green tea in it:

> "Makes you stop, take a break, take something to eat and take the time to look around you."

I took a thermos on the [Milford Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/034-milford-track-nz-popular-walk/), and it was the one thing I didn't use. I don't even drink hot drinks at home.

For Ellena, life is like that too: you can get so caught up in getting to the end that you don't see anything.

## Staying fit, the Camino and attacking life {#attacking-life}

### How Ellena stays active

I'm not great at moving every day, so I asked how she does it. She gets up every morning at five, because she knows she doesn't feel as good if she doesn't do something. She does strength work in the garage, because at 58 you need to look after your bones. She's done Pilates in a class for 20 years, follows a weights programme at home, and walks lots and lots. For the Rotorua Marathon, she did weight training four days a week plus her long walks, and she knows that got her through the 42 kilometres.

Her advice is to find what works for you. If you're not a morning person, find the time and the thing that suits you, so it feels like joyful movement.

### The Camino and what's next

Since her treatment, Ellena and her husband have been attacking life. They bought a motorhome and spent four months in the South Island last year walking a lot. They also went to Portugal for three weeks, a trip they'd first booked for 2020, and finished with a section of the Portuguese Camino along the coast (which was actually in Spain). They loved the whole experience.

Next, they'd like to do the whole Camino: 800 kilometres, or 40 days at 20 kilometres a day. For Ellena it's about the experience: you meet people, you walk, you stop for lunch, and it's that slowing down thing again.

When we recorded, they were about to go overseas for three months, including three weeks in Japan. They're also hoping to walk the South Island section of Te Araroa, and might spend time in the UK walking and house sitting.

### Cancer as a gift

Ellena says cancer was her gift. It hasn't been easy, and there are days she gets scared. But she gets to look at the world through eyes lots of people have never looked through:

> "I know how to stop sweating the small stuff because I know how to attack life"

She calls herself an active person, and being active has helped her in every part of her life. We're really lucky in New Zealand to have so much on our doorstep and the freedom to go and do it. So go out there and attack life.
