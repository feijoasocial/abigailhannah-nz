I've met Hannah in real life, and if you follow her online, you'll know she's a ray of sunshine. She's exactly the same in person. So I was really really excited to get her on the podcast, especially with our [kayak trip](https://abigailhannah.nz/162-we-are-kayaking-from-cape-reinga-to-tauranga-to-raise-20-000-/) down the Northland coast coming up (I needed some spoilers).

Hannah is 27, originally from the UK, and has called New Zealand home for 18 years. She works in marketing, has just finished her degree, and when she's not doing either, she's outside or planning her next trip. She also runs a community hiking group in Auckland, with a waitlist that blew my mind.

So this one is a local's guide to [Northland](https://abigailhannah.nz/northland-road-trip/): the Bay of Islands without a boat, her favourite walks and campsites, the drive to the Cape and the beaches closer to Auckland. Plus a pie opinion that might get her in trouble. Then we get into fitting adventures around work and study, why she started The Back Roads, and what a long OE taught her about home.

## From the UK to a love of hiking {#hannah-story}

Travel came first for Hannah. She grew up hearing wild backpacking stories, and at 15 a school trip took her on a two-week expedition to Vietnam and Cambodia, including a massive hike through the jungle. It completely changed how she saw the world, and showed her she loved getting out of her comfort zone.

Hiking took longer. Her first memory of it is climbing the Old Man of Coniston in the Lake District as a little kid, which is probably a really big hill by New Zealand standards. For years, she hated hiking.

That changed on a South Island road trip at the end of 2020, while the rest of the world was still in lockdown. In Wānaka, she talked her partner into climbing Roys Peak. She thinks it's a truly diabolical walk, but it showed her that hiking gets you to the most amazing views. And in New Zealand, most of the most beautiful places are only reachable on foot, so if she wanted to see every corner of the country, she'd need boots.

I walked Roys Peak through the night, expecting an inversion layer at the top. In hindsight it was entirely the wrong time of year, and I was so mad there wasn't one. Go do it, but know what you're getting into.

## Microadventures around work and study {#microadventures}

Hannah finished school at 17 and graduates at 27, after the last four years by distance learning. The year before we recorded, she was working full time, studying full time, doing her own projects on the side and trying to keep her social life afloat. Her experience is that you get better at juggling the more you have on your plate.

What helped most was not getting caught up in what travel is meant to look like. Social media makes it seem like you have to quit your job, travel full time or live in a van for it to count. So they stuck to weekends away camping, a night or two at a time. Their two-night trips (Friday after work to Sunday) have become early Saturday starts with a Sunday return.

She loves the idea of [microadventures](https://abigailhannah.nz/weekend-trips-ashley-elliott/): even a half-day hike or heading out to watch the sunset counts.

That word changed things for me too, years ago: travel doesn't have to be two weeks overseas. When [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/) and I bought our first tent, at 10pm one night we asked our flatmates if we should go camping. We searched for the closest place we could legally pitch a tent for free, found a spot near the ferry terminal at Whangaparāoa, drove up at 11pm, slept there and came home in the morning. That's a microadventure.

And it can look like other people travel constantly. When I looked back at last year, we didn't do a trip until November. [Our van life](https://abigailhannah.nz/137-a-5-year-journey-from-budget-diy-van-to-dream-home-on-wheels-with-abigail-and-isaac/) took a very long time to get to where it is now. I wish I'd filmed that messy middle.

Hannah's car camping setup took years to build up too, little by little. Online, people go from day one to year eight overnight.

## Why Northland is so underrated {#why-northland}

Hannah has two reasons for loving Northland. It's close to Auckland, so you can go a lot and get into all the nooks and crannies, away from the main route. And she thinks it's criminally underrated. In summer, in her opinion, nothing compares.

She feels about Northland the way some people feel about the [Coromandel](https://abigailhannah.nz/163-best-spots-and-hidden-gems-in-the-bay-of-plenty-coromandel-with-ciska/). Ride or die.

The Bay of Islands is one of her favourite places in the whole world, and the first place she remembers camping after moving to New Zealand: a week or two off the grid on Urupukapuka Island, at Cable Bay. She didn't appreciate it as much as she should have then, but looking back, those memories feel so special.

Isaac and I did both coasts of Northland, all the way up to the Cape, and it wasn't long enough.

How long should you go for? Hannah wouldn't do the Bay of Islands in less than three days, which makes it a good long weekend from Auckland. For the whole region, she loves slow travel, so she'd say ten days at minimum, and as long as possible.

## The Bay of Islands without a boat {#bay-of-islands}

You don't need a boat to enjoy the Bay of Islands. Ferries run from Russell and Paihia to Urupukapuka, the largest island: book your tickets and walk on, no faffing around. And if you get seasick, there's plenty on land.

Hannah's Bay of Islands picks:

* **Rainbow Falls in Kerikeri**, one of the best waterfalls in the country.
* **Barefoot Sailing Adventures**, the best tour she's done in New Zealand. It's an eco-tourism sailing company run by a couple, with a full day on their catamaran around the islands, lunch, and close ties to Project Island Song, the conservation project in the bay.
* **Hone's Garden**, one of her favourite restaurants in the country, for wood-fired pizza.
* **Paihia**, a really nice town.
* **Waitangi Treaty Grounds**, next to Paihia. She thinks everyone should go at least once, because there's something special about learning there.
* **Kerikeri**, which she reckons is the nicest town, with streets lined with cafes and little shops.

I love Kerikeri too, and I'm surprised people skip it. There are walks along the river, the old Stone Store and Rainbow Falls.

### The pie opinion

Then there's Kerikeri Bakehouse, Hannah's wild card for the best bakery ever. Better than Fairlie? Yes. She went to Fairlie on her South Island road trip, and the pies were really good, but Kerikeri Bakehouse is a little bit better, with the wildest pie flavours. Her pick: the brisket and mac and cheese pie.

I love a controversial pie opinion, so I'm hoping to stop there on the way up to Cape Reinga to start our kayak trip.

## Best walks in the Bay of Islands and Whangaroa {#bay-of-islands-walks}

The Bay of Islands isn't packed with hikes, Hannah says, but Urupukapuka has beautiful walks all over the island. Her favourite is the Island Loop Trail, from where the ferry drops you all the way to the other side of the island, which you have to be intentional about reaching. There are sheer drops down cliff faces, but even though she's scared of heights, it never feels like you're looking down into a crevasse.

An hour's drive north, the Duke's Nose track is phenomenal. The view from the lookout looks like you're not even in New Zealand.

If you don't have time for the full trail, there's a lesser-known option: St Paul's Rock, a short walk on the other side of the harbour. Then head down to the Whangaroa Sport Fishing Club, which has the cheapest beer she's found.

I've done Duke's Nose more than once, and I'm not usually a hike repeater, so that says something. It's on our kayak itinerary too, with a night at Lane Cove Hut and a cheeky hike.

## Camping in Northland {#northland-camping}

Hannah was meant to go on a big OE in 2020. When that fell through, she bought a 2004 Ford Transit and converted it. It was her pride and joy, but it turned out to be a money pit and got too expensive to maintain (most people who've owned a Transit will tell you the same thing). They swapped it for a car with a rooftop tent, and she'd never go back. She sleeps better outside than at home now.

The first night in the new rooftop tent, they were determined to camp whatever the weather, and went to Uretiti. At one or two in the morning, they woke to wind from a tropical cyclone off the coast, wind like she'd never experienced, with the car moving. The tent survived. New Zealand weather changes fast, so if MetService says sun, don't be surprised if it rains.

Her Northland camping picks:

* **Cable Bay on Urupukapuka**, where you can paddle right up to the campsite and beach.
* **Uretiti DOC campsite**, about 40 minutes north of the Mangawhai turn-off. It's huge, really affordable and right by a beautiful beach.
* **Kai Iwi Lakes**, on the west coast of Northland. They've been in winter and summer, and it's no less beautiful in the cold.

We've stayed at Uretiti. You're tucked behind the sand dunes and walk straight onto the beach, a bit like Tāwharanui. Our special one is Rarawa Beach and its DOC campsite, where we spent Christmas one year. It has that squeaky white sand.

## Northland must-dos from the Cape to Langs Beach {#northland-must-dos}

If you're in the Bay of Islands, drive up to the Cape. With a four-by-four, you can drive [Ninety Mile Beach](https://abigailhannah.nz/006-4wd-land-cruiser-bri-woolnough/), with its huge sand dunes. It's a complete gas guzzler, but everyone has to do it once.

Not in a two-wheel drive, though. The last time Hannah was up there, a local had to tow out someone who'd taken a Honda Civic onto the beach.

We've pulled out a hatchback ourselves. Don't be that person.

### Closer to Auckland

The Bay of Islands is a three to four hour drive from Auckland, which won't suit every weekend, so Hannah has picks closer to the city:

* **Mangawhai Heads**, with cute shops and a Sunday market.
* **Langs Beach**, up the coast from Mangawhai, one of the most beautiful beaches she's been to in this part of the world. On a Sunday evening there's a real fruit ice cream truck, which makes the beach ten times better.

I've done the coastal walk that links Langs Beach with Waipū, as an out and back from the Waipū end. It has rock formations like the Pancake Rocks at Punakaiki, less than two hours from Auckland. It'll take as long as you give it, with all the photo stops.

Hannah was about to walk it for the first time.

That's her point about track times, too. When she talks about hikes online, she gives the official time, but it could easily be double. At the start of the year before we recorded, the Rob Roy Glacier track took them eight hours, because it's so pretty you stop every five seconds. That's what she thinks we should be talking about online.

### Tutukākā and the Poor Knights

Hannah has explored a little of the Tutukākā Coast. It's home to the Poor Knights, a huge marine reserve with a ridiculous amount of marine life. Their snorkelling trip there was cancelled by terrible weather, but the drive down from the Bay of Islands was its own reward, with another wow out the window every five seconds, the colour of the ocean and the white sand beaches.

That's what she loves about Northland: it feels so much slower than Auckland. And for her, how laid back and come-as-you-are New Zealand is, is the number one reason she loves it here.

## The Back Roads community hikes {#community-hikes}

Hannah's favourite feeling in the world is pre-hike excitement, the kind that makes you feel a little bit sick. Then there's reaching the summit, or sitting down halfway for lunch, and realising her body can take her anywhere she wants to go. She talks a lot about what a privilege it is to be able-bodied, with the time and resources to go to these places.

Then there's sharing those moments with people you love. She's done a lot of solo travel, but a shared experience takes something from a 9.5 to a 10.

That's where The Back Roads came from. In November 2024, she posted on TikTok that nobody tells you how hard it is to make friends in your 20s. At school or university you're in the same four walls as everyone else, then you finish and suddenly, where do you meet people? She thought her 20s would look like Rachel Green's in Friends. Logistically, she doesn't live in New York.

The conversations her post started showed her how many people felt the same, and how much need there is for spaces where like-minded people can meet. Through her work at an audiology company, writing about hearing loss and cognition, she came across research on social isolation.

The first Back Roads hike was in January 2025, and they've run one a month since. Each time they do a walk, then go to a brewery, a cafe or a restaurant. Hannah thinks walking is the best way to meet people, because you're not sitting face to face.

She's happy to see other hiking groups popping up, too. When people ask if she's worried about competition, her answer is simple:

> "if the competition is getting people together … decreasing social isolation, making these spaces where people can connect, then bring it on."

I went to one of her hikes in the Hūnua Ranges, and we heard and saw kākā. I didn't even know we had kākā in Auckland.

Hannah and her partner still talk about it: they'd spent their whole South Island trip waiting for a magical wildlife moment, and it happened in Auckland. Hannah adds:

> "that is the thing i think I miss the most when I go overseas is the way that New Zealand sounds."

A lot of people go into the bush here and say how nice and quiet it is. It's not supposed to be quiet. It's supposed to be really, really loud.

## A long OE and advice for first-time visitors {#first-time-visitors}

Hannah did get her OE in the end, in 2023: months in Europe, everywhere from Poland to Portugal, with Southeast Asia to follow. Backpacking had been her dream for as long as she can remember. But she completely underestimated how exhausting it would be with no routine, no family or friends around and no set schedule, and she found it really hard without that stability. They cut the trip short and came home before Christmas.

For a long time she felt like a failure, because social media makes the OE look like nothing but rainbows. It taught her this:

> "what we see online is just the ultra curated 5% of somebody's life."

It also taught her how spoiled we are at home. New Zealand has ruined her for the rest of the world, she says, the way people in Western Australia say their beaches ruined them for all others. New Zealand is so much easier to travel, with one main road and everything within driving distance (though you do need a car). Living here has also made her want her own space when she travels, and focus more on the outdoors overseas.

And she was shocked to find things aren't free overseas the way they are here: water, parking, hiking. Paying 40 euro to park at a trailhead in the Dolomites nearly gave her a heart attack, though she fully supports it if it keeps the trails maintained.

I think overseas there's history, culture and cities competing for your time, so you have to chase the outdoors more. On my trip to South Korea, I only realised the day before I flew out how many big mountains there are right in Seoul.

Hannah's advice for anyone visiting New Zealand for the first time: don't get caught up in what people tell you to see, whether that's social media, family, friends or colleagues. Choose where to go based on what you like to do. Queenstown has a cult following, but if you're more of a [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/) or [Catlins](https://abigailhannah.nz/139-slow-travel-in-an-ev-and-catlins-dunedin-and-oamaru-with-mature-flaneur-tim-ward/) person, go there. And spend much more time there than you think you'll need.
