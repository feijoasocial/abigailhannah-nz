Jenn and I had been talking about her coming on the podcast for almost two years, so I was really really excited to finally hit record. It had also been a while since we'd talked Taranaki on the show, and I love Taranaki.

Jenn is an outdoor education teacher, originally from Canada, now living in Taranaki, where her partner is from. She's a mum of two young girls, and she started Trails and Tots, a parent hiking group that has grown into so much more. She's also done the Taranaki Three Peaks Traverse twice, most recently solo, in a single big day.

So we cover the traverse (which I couldn't even find on Topo maps), how she manages risk on her own, the trails in Taranaki for the rest of us, and the shiny new Pouākai Hut. Then we get into getting outdoors with kids: what her research found, the barriers parents face, gear, and why the baby-asleep-in-a-backpack dream isn't everyone's reality. I'm not much of a sufferfest person, so I'll be sticking to the circuit.

## How Jenn got into the outdoors {#jenn-story}

Jenn grew up in rural Ontario, Canada, surrounded by outstanding trail networks, beautiful forests, lakes and rivers. Her family was outdoorsy, and their main ways of connecting with the outdoors were mountain biking and skiing. They had a cottage (a bach, here), so there was lots of time on the lake and fishing too.

It wasn't until around university that she got into bigger backcountry trips: camping, alpine travel and climbing.

After teachers' college, she moved to British Columbia, largely to connect more deeply with the outdoors, and met her partner, who's from Taranaki. They spent about six years in BC before moving to Aotearoa, and they've been here about eight years. She feels very privileged to call it home.

She still does a lot of [mountain biking](https://abigailhannah.nz/047-mountainbiking-nz-laura-goldsworthy/), and Taranaki has some great trails. [Skiing](https://abigailhannah.nz/014-snowsports-nz-olympian-anja-barugh/) took a hit, because North Island skiing doesn't quite live up to the standard once you've lived in British Columbia. So they've swapped skis for [surf](https://abigailhannah.nz/learn-to-surf-new-zealand/).

## Why Taranaki is underrated {#taranaki}

Jenn agrees Taranaki is underrated, and in some ways she likes it that way. There's the surf, there's Taranaki Maunga, and there are amazing trails and amazing off-track routes too. You can fit so much into a single day.

It's a great place to raise kids, she says, 30 minutes from the mountain, with lots of huts to explore and a really beautiful community. And a bonus: Taranaki doesn't have South Island [West Coast](https://abigailhannah.nz/064-west-coast-road-trip/) sandflies, and there's something to be said for that.

## The Taranaki Three Peaks Traverse {#three-peaks}

When I looked for the Three Peaks Traverse on Topo maps, I couldn't find it. That's because it isn't an official traverse. It's a local gem that has gained popularity in the last couple of years, and when Jenn first did it, about seven years ago, it was still pretty low key.

### The three peaks

* **Taranaki summit**
* **Pouākai Trig**, then down the back side to the Kaitake Range
* **Patuha Trig** in the Kaitake Range, then out the other side to Ōakura, the town by the sea

There are variations. The four and five peaks versions tie in Fanthams Peak and Paritutu, and there's even a six peaks version that ties in the Shining Peak brewery in town. It's a niche challenge among keen alpine people in Taranaki.

### Distance, elevation and terrain

For most people, it's a single-day mission. You could technically break it up, but the appeal is the big one-day challenge. In total it's around 36 to 40 kilometres, depending on how you do it, with about 3,000 metres of elevation gain and loss.

The part people shouldn't underestimate is the terrain. It's really technical. There's no reason to run into snow or ice if you pick your weather window and go in summer, so it's summer only. But anything around the summit of the maunga is technical by nature. If you know the scree on the main summit route, imagine unmarked scree slopes on other aspects of the mountain, and how much risk management that involves.

Jenn's preface: if you're from out of the region, you need time on the ground in Te Papakura o Taranaki first, to have the confidence and competence to navigate it.

### Coming back to it after kids

Jenn did the traverse before having kids, then repeated it this past year. That second time was special and meaningful: a marker of feeling like herself again and getting back to bigger missions since having kids. It had been a carrot hanging there, and she kept thinking she couldn't wait to do it again.

## Doing the traverse solo and managing risk {#solo-risk}

Jenn did it solo, and she loves solo time in the mountains. Big challenges put her in a particular headspace:

> "I find myself in a very unburdened state of mind. It's like, you're only focusing on what you're doing and what's in front of you."

She's quite a risk-averse person, which might sound crazy to people who know what she gets up to, and she gets a lot of enjoyment out of the risk management: knowing exactly what's within her limits and working within that. Solo, on a challenge like this, you make decisions for yourself and work at a pace that's right for you the whole time. Others she's talked to who've done it solo feel the same.

Groups let you make risk management decisions together, and they have their own heuristics: some people will default to letting others decide. As an outdoor education teacher, Jenn has a good read of people, and the last thing you want is someone dragged along completely uncomfortable.

I'm always the person in the group who speaks up and asks whether we're sure, because you can see on everyone's faces that they're all thinking the same thing.

For the Three Peaks, Jenn knew herself, knew her limits and knew the tracks. She has good friends she'd happily do it with, and there was something intriguing about doing it on her own.

## Route finding from Carrington Ridge to the Kaitake Range {#route-finding}

The last time I was in Taranaki was quite a few Easters ago, on the [Pouākai Circuit](https://abigailhannah.nz/005-pouakai-circuit-syme-hut-with-kristina/), and that terrain is gnarly. So how do you avoid getting lost?

### Up the summit and down Carrington Ridge

To summit the maunga, you can take the main summit route or one of a couple of variations. Jenn takes one of the variations. On a previous traverse, she came back down the same way and around to Holly Hut.

This time, she came down Carrington Ridge, a very seldom-used aspect of the maunga. She'd scoped it out beforehand and had GPS files. It's a super exposed scree slope, with a high risk of getting cliffed out and several no-fall zones, so you need your navigation dialled and your head on straight, and you have to take your time and manage where you are on the slope.

She had the GPS file on both her inReach and her phone, for redundancy, plus paper maps.

### Old hunters' tracks to Ōakura

Off Carrington Ridge, you're back on main track for a little while. After Pouākai Trig, it's old hunters' tracks, which Jenn knows well and enjoys most about Taranaki's trails. You need that familiarity to feel confident and succeed.

On a map, Pouākai Trig looks like you're over most of the climbing, with only the Kaitake Range left. In fact, it's your halfway mark. The stretch off Pouākai Trig and into the Kaitake Range is a huge distance, all on old hunters' tracks: bush bashing, rough terrain and steep sections, and it can be quite slow moving.

It took Jenn just over 12 hours, start to finish, and she was lucky to pick a pretty perfect weather window. She wouldn't do it without one.

## The Pouākai Circuit and the new Pouākai Hut {#pouakai}

For trampers who aren't after a sufferfest, Taranaki has some popular options:

* **Pouākai Crossing:** quickly becoming one of the most popular tracks in Aotearoa, and definitely in Taranaki.
* **Pouākai Circuit:** Jenn's recommendation for best bang for buck if you're visiting and want an overnight or two-night trip, with beautiful views and diverse terrain.

### The new Pouākai Hut

Pouākai Hut has been upgraded and reopened, and Jenn and her daughter went up the first weekend it was open. It's beautiful and so well built, and Jenn reckons it'll add a lot of value to the crossing and the circuit.

It has stained glass windows, which make it feel a bit like a church. Working with iwi, it kept the same footprint as the old hut. In the old hut, the windows were oddly high, so you couldn't see much from the table. Now they're much lower and bigger, with the same beautiful vantage out to sea and some other angles too.

They were on the third page of the hut book, because Taranaki was keen, and her daughter was very proud to be one of the first 50 people to sleep there.

## Trails and Tots and getting outdoors with kids {#trails-and-tots}

### From research to real life

Jenn did a master's degree in outdoor experiential education curriculum, researching the role of, and need for, female leadership and feminist pedagogy in outdoor programmes. Her research found that one of the biggest barriers to women staying engaged with time outdoors is motherhood.

She did that research before she was a mum, sure she'd never lose that outdoorsy part of her identity. Then she had kids and was very quickly humbled. The visions of tramping at pace with a baby asleep in a backpack went out the window when she had a baby who didn't sleep in a backpack. It became a new navigation to figure out the balance.

### Starting Trails and Tots

After her second child, Jenn started Trails and Tots in Taranaki as a parent hiking group, and it quickly grew into much more, bringing in her career background and education side. She'd hoped to get maybe 10 parents out over the first year. In the first two and a half years, over 500 whānau in Taranaki have taken part.

People come for safety in numbers, to learn which trails suit little ones, or for the motivation of a group. It took Jenn a lot of trial and error to find tracks that suit a toddler.

### What the research found

In 2024, Trails and Tots had funding through Curious Minds for a community-run research project on the benefits of, and barriers to, parents getting outdoors.

The benefits:

* **Improved physical and mental health and wellbeing**
* **Greater connection with nature**, with te taiao
* **A stronger sense of community** and support for parents getting out together in a facilitated group
* **Getting to know local tracks** and green spaces

The barriers include money and time, and Jenn recognises how much privilege is on her side. Access to gear is another, so Taranaki has a gear library of hiking backpack carriers and kids' sleeping bags, set up with funding and private donations. Information is a third: knowing what a trail looks like, whether it's appropriate and whether you've got the confidence to do it.

When we recorded, Trails and Tots was transitioning to a charitable trust, with the goal of expanding to support more of the community, and maybe one day outside the region.

### If you're not in Taranaki

Jenn gave a shout-out to [Jasmine at Kiwi Kids Outdoors](https://abigailhannah.nz/141-best-christchurch-and-canterbury-adventures-with-kids-with-jasmine-trafford/) in Christchurch, and there are other little nature play groups around. Otherwise, connect with other parents, even at a parent group that isn't nature based, and suggest a really low-key walk. Use the tracks within town boundaries. It doesn't have to be a massive mission to the mountain, and most of what Jenn does with her kids is the low-hanging fruit around the corner.

Parenting is a unique journey, with no one-size-fits-all answer, so take what you see on social media with a grain of salt:

> "if you see someone who's got a baby who just sleeps in a backpack and is happy as all the time, like that is not everyone's norm. And it is a juggle and it is navigating a new normal and an ever-changing normal."

People connect with the outdoors in different ways too. Jenn loves big challenges, and for most people that probably doesn't work, so it's important to appreciate slower ways of connecting with nature.

That's how my adventures started: going to the beach, then walks in the local park, then a day trip, then an overnight, slowly growing how far we went. That was as me and my husband, without kids, and you can do the same with kids. Start small, start where you are and see where it takes you.

## Gear for little ones and what's next {#gear-and-next}

### Carriers, layers and snacks

A listener asked about carriers and gear. Jenn's family is out of the carrier days now, and their favourite was the Osprey Poco Plus, because it has the highest volume for packing for overnights. Fitting all that gear and a small human onto your back is part of the learning curve.

Her tips:

* **It doesn't have to be the best gear.** Look secondhand, on Marketplace. Carriers are built to last, and theirs has already gone on to someone else.
* **Know what you need for safety** and what's nice to have, and keep gear circulating.
* **Snacks.** The number you need is always one more than you've got.
* **Warm and dry.** Kids only enjoy being outdoors if they're warm. Use moisture-wicking base layers (merino doesn't work for everyone) and ideally a good waterproof layer on top. In Jenn's words: "warm and dry equals happy".

### The Heaphy, the Tararua and the Dusky

Jenn's family had hoped to bikepack the [Heaphy Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/133-tramping-the-heaphy-track-from-karamea-to-collingwood-with-jacq-nesbit/) with the kids, while both girls fitted on shotgun seats. That window has passed and plans changed, so if they do the Heaphy now, it'll be on foot, slowly, carrying the little one quite a bit.

For herself, she's looking at the Southern Crossing in the Tararua this summer, a single sufferfest day. And on her bucket list, without the kids: the [Dusky Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/049-dusky-track-fiordland/).

The Dusky isn't on my list yet. Give me low effort and beautiful views, without the sandflies, and I'll be there. I'd love to get back to Taranaki for the Pouākai Circuit again, or maybe even the summit, and I haven't been to Syme Hut yet. Jenn says that's pretty special too.
