A few listeners have asked me to get Jonesy on the show, and after watching the short film of his Fiordland trip, I was really really keen too.

Mark Jones, or Jonesy as most of the outdoor industry knows him, lectures in outdoor education at AUT. He's been on some serious expeditions, including sea kayaking down the Antarctic Peninsula. And his most recent trip was meant to close the loop on Fiordland by kayak. Fiordland had other ideas (I'm sure that never happens).

So we talk about how he got into the outdoors, the years it took to pull off his first big expedition, and the Fiordland trip itself. The pivot from the south coast to Doubtful Sound, staying in huts and living off crayfish and venison, the toughest surf exit of his life at Big Bay, and stitching a split kayak back together with fishing line. Plus why he paddled for mental health, and his advice if you want to start sea kayaking or plan something big.

## Growing up outdoors in Rotorua {#jonesy-story}

Jonesy grew up in Rotorua and lives in Tāmaki Makaurau, and the outdoors is a really important part of his life. He reckons he was extraordinarily lucky as a kid (and took it all for granted) because his parents were outdoorsy: tramping, skiing and deer stalking. Every summer they'd head to the [East Cape](https://abigailhannah.nz/056-vanlife-the-east-cape-and-life-done-differently-with-lisa-jansen/) for a coastal tenting holiday of fishing and [free diving](https://abigailhannah.nz/107-learning-to-freedive-spearfish-and-building-community-with-renee-taylor/).

He remembers pulling up on the side of the road to get the thermette out for a brew, or camping right there on the roadside. You could do that back then, when there were fewer people and life was simpler.

## Sea kayaking the Antarctic Peninsula {#antarctic-peninsula}

His first big expedition came out of working at the Outdoor Pursuits Centre. A colleague had the vision of sea kayaking down the [Antarctic](https://abigailhannah.nz/south-pole-expedition-2/) Peninsula and pulled Jonesy and another colleague into the plan, and Jonesy was in from the get-go.

It took years from the idea to the trip, including a year's delay because they weren't ready, of building a profile, writing media articles, asking sponsors for money and applying for grants. It was an incredible learning journey. He remembers thinking that even if they never got to do it, it had been a hell of a good time trying, because of all the training trips they'd never otherwise have done.

The icing on the cake was that they did get to go. In 2001 they hitched a ride across the Drake Passage with Sir Peter Blake, who dropped them at the tip of the Antarctic Peninsula. Then they were on their own for 35 days, sea kayaking down the peninsula.

A key ethos of their expeditions was adding to New Zealand's adventure story, sharing it to inspire others the way earlier adventurers had inspired them. What's changed most since, he says, is being able to share a trip live.

It was a great ride and a treasured chapter of his life. Once his family grew, it all got a bit hard, so he bowed out of the last one, a crossing of Greenland.

## Sea kayaking Fiordland: the plan and the pivot {#fiordland-plan}

Jonesy had already traversed Fiordland on foot from south to north, from Te Waewae Bay through to the [Milford Track](https://abigailhannah.nz/034-milford-track-nz-popular-walk/), which he walked out with his family. The Fiordland kayak trip was meant to complete the circle, by sea kayak from Te Waewae Bay up to Milford Sound.

He'd even felt a bit of a fraud heading to the Antarctic or South Georgia without it under his belt, because it's one of the world's iconic wild, remote coastlines.

But it wasn't the season for the south coast. [Fiordland](https://abigailhannah.nz/186-packrafting-and-tramping-48-days-through-fiordland-with-penzy-dinsdale/) just hadn't had a summer. They drove down from Auckland in a heatwave and found snow on the hills in Te Anau. At Te Waewae Bay conditions were ridiculous, and the ten-day forecast wasn't going to change.

So they pivoted. They paddled across Lake Manapōuri, did the 21-kilometre portage over into Doubtful Sound, and headed north from there, which was half the planned length. When they reached Milford Sound they didn't feel like finishing, so they kept going all the way up to Jackson Bay. That leg brought most of the trip's dramas, with surf landings and departures.

> "Yeah, well, the one thing I've learned, I suppose, is that you can't impose your will on nature."

It didn't take them long to get philosophical about it and enjoy the trip they were having. The catch was that they had no maps north of Milford Sound, because they'd never planned to go there. Jonesy's navigation tip: keep New Zealand on your right and you can't get too lost. They also had the Topo50 app and a satellite messenger with maps.

### Sharing the trip as it happened

The satellite messenger paired with his phone, so Jonesy could send a text each day to his wife, who posted it on their Facebook page.

When I'm in the bush I'd rather disconnect. Milford Sound has 4G now, and when we were there in August, I put my phone on aeroplane mode.

Jonesy feels that tension too. A trip for yourself is nice, and so is sharing one to inspire people in the moment. He likes both.

## Fiordland huts, campsites and living off the land {#huts-and-food}

There are quite a few huts spread through Fiordland, enough that you could go hut to hut. They tried to stay in all of them, and to go into as many fiords as they could. The idea was to get from A to B in the most interesting way they could.

Places to pitch a tent are few and far between. One early campsite was barely above the tide, and the next morning, with a slightly higher tide, the spot where the tent had been was underwater.

For food, they carried pasta, noodles and rice and planned to get their protein from the sea. Each of them had a wetsuit and flippers, and they had a small pole spear and a rifle:

* **Crayfish.** If they went diving, it was pretty easy to grab one.
* **Venison.** They only went [hunting](https://abigailhannah.nz/150-hunting-wapiti-in-fiordland-with-jarrod-mclachlan/) twice and came back with a deer each time, and a deer feeds you for quite a few days.
* **Fish.** Every time they went in, they were sorted pretty quickly.

They were fully self-contained, with no food drops, no one to meet and no end date, which made planning really easy. They could make it up as they went along. If there was no following wind, they waited for a tailwind.

## Big Bay surf and a broken kayak {#big-bay-surf}

The first drama was in Doubtful Sound, when Jonesy found out his phone cable didn't work, which meant no daily updates. So he radioed a passing tour boat (not a Mayday, he's clear) to ask whether anyone on board had a spare iPhone cable. It was really embarrassing, he says, but they did a U-turn and came over, and one of the passengers gifted them a cable. A lot of phones were filming.

I reckon that was the highlight of the day for those tourists.

Further north, getting out of Big Bay was the toughest exit he's ever had from any bay: a relentless succession of breakers. After ten of them he was ready to turn around, then he saw his paddling partner carrying on, so he did too. There were another ten to get out the back, and one was so big he had to tip upside down, let it break on top of him and roll back up, or he'd have been surfed backwards. He felt that one was touch and go.

There were some touch-and-go landings too, and both boats were damaged. His partner's needed first aid on the nose. Jonesy's needed major surgery: the boat had essentially folded at the front hatch, splitting the seams and the bow, with a split about half a metre long.

His fix:

* **Melt holes** along the gunwales on either side of the splits.
* **Lace it up** with really heavy gauge fishing line.
* **Seal it** with a bit of silicone and lots of duct tape.

He'd never done anything like it. The alternative was calling for a rescue. It was sturdy enough to pick up fully loaded from both ends.

They'd taken their own Barracuda kayaks, which have light three-millimetre hulls. Crash into a river bar fully laden and something's got to give. Barracuda would have supplied boats with five-millimetre hulls if they'd known about the trip, and when they got back, they came on board as sponsors anyway. They fixed up the boats, replaced his partner's completely, and offered boats with bigger hulls for the next trip.

## Albatrosses and paddling into the mountains {#highlights}

Jonesy really loves wildlife on a trip. Going up and down on the big West Coast rollers with albatrosses, shearwaters and petrels cruising around, and some wandering albatrosses coming through, gave him really special moments with the big birds. Dolphins are always special too.

Some of the best moments were arriving somewhere. Like reaching the head of George Sound and finding a sweet little hut there, after coming off a big, scary sea onto flat fiord water.

> "you're actually paddling into the mountains, right, down these fjords. And you just get right in amongst the mountains with waterfalls."

## Paddling for mental health {#mental-health}

For Jonesy there's a really strong connection between wellbeing and the outdoors.

He knows people who have found mental health services really hard to access, with a long wait to see a counsellor or psychologist. The Mental Health Foundation's website has an amazing suite of resources, many of them self-help.

He also talks about biophilia, our innate connection to nature. For most of human evolution we've lived in the wilderness, and cities are a tiny sliver of our existence, so nature is where our brains are most comfortable.

## How to start sea kayaking and plan an expedition {#getting-started}

Jonesy has enjoyed his solo trips, his trips with friends and his family trips. The relationships you build and deepen in the outdoors can be quite profound, he says, because nature shows you the real person. He's good with his own company too, especially if he's sharing the trip with a text each night.

### Getting into sea kayaking

* **Do a course or join a club.** Groups around the country run courses.
* **Learn to read the weather.** Sea kayaking can be really simple on a flat day, but things can change really quickly, and judgement is a skill you have to learn.
* **Learn from others and from books**, and get alongside someone more experienced.

My first multi-day sea kayak trip was in [Abel Tasman](https://abigailhannah.nz/026-abel-tasman-kyle-mulinder/) with [Isaac](https://abigailhannah.nz/099-moving-to-new-zealand-and-learning-to-white-water-kayak-with-isaac-naylor/). The weather was so good, and we took it slow and cruisey, with lunch on the beaches and afternoon naps. It didn't put me off at all, and I've done a few trips around [the Hauraki](https://abigailhannah.nz/114-sea-kayaking-for-beginners-and-the-best-of-the-hauraki-gulf-with-bennett-lim/) since. When we recorded I was reading [Red Paterson's](https://abigailhannah.nz/nz-kayak-circumnavigation/) book about her kayak circumnavigation of New Zealand, and I couldn't wait to get to her Fiordland section.

Jonesy calls her quest a great example of the growth that can come from a big adventure. Whether you pull it off or not, you learn so much along the way.

### Planning something big

His advice is to just do it. There's real power in stating what you're going to do, and you'd be surprised who comes out of the woodwork to help. People want to see you succeed and be part of it, so trust that it'll come together.

And if you haven't done any adventures yet, start small:

> "Start with something micro and that's within your comfort zone … maybe extend yourself a little bit each time and then before you know it, you'll be working towards something bigger"

As for Jonesy, he's heading back down this summer with the same paddling partner to try for a better window along the south coast, from Te Waewae Bay to Milford Sound. Watch this space.
